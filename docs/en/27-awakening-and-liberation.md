# 27. Awakening and Liberation

I have a question.

How do you know you're awake?

It seems fairly simple.

You're reading these words.

You recognize where you are.

And you're probably not dreaming.

Although if you're dreaming about reading a book on awakening, I have to admit your subconscious has excellent literary taste.

But have you ever had a dream in which you were completely convinced you were awake?

During the dream, everything seemed real.

There were people.

Situations.

Emotions.

And a story you accepted without too many questions.

Until you woke up.

Then you realized that what had seemed so convincing was an experience constructed by your own mind.

Of course, this comparison has limits.

Buddhist awakening doesn't simply mean discovering that we live inside a dream.

But the metaphor can help us ask a fascinating question.

What if some of the things we take for granted about ourselves and our experience depend on processes we haven't yet learned to recognize?

And if we came to understand them deeply, what would change?

Welcome to awakening.

## What Does Awakening Mean?

In Buddhism, we encounter the word bodhi, usually translated as awakening or enlightenment.

It refers to a liberating understanding of reality as presented by the Dharma.

It isn't simply experiencing great tranquility.

Or attaining an extraordinary state of concentration.

Nor is it having an intense experience of unity, emptiness, or the absence of a personal identity.

Awakening is fundamentally related to liberation from ignorance and from the dynamics that sustain suffering.

In early Buddhist teachings, we encounter a particularly important explanation.

The extinction of greed, aversion, and ignorance.

But here I want to recall something we've explored throughout the book.

We shouldn't confuse traditional terms with our functional interpretation.

The tradition describes a profound liberating realization.

Our model attempts to investigate how certain mental configurations may perpetuate suffering and how they might be transformed.

There's an interesting relationship between these perspectives.

But we needn't claim they're exactly equivalent.

In fact, perhaps one of the best ways of respecting the teachings is to recognize when we're interpreting them.

And when we still don't know.

## Awakening Isn't Having an Extraordinary Experience

I want to begin with a fundamental distinction.

During meditation, we can experience extraordinary phenomena.

Deep states of concentration.

Rapture.

Equanimity.

Changes in perception.

Alterations in our usual sense of identity.

Even experiences in which the distinction between observer and observed seems to disappear completely.

Some of these may profoundly transform how we understand experience.

But does that mean we've awakened?

Not necessarily.

An experience may be extraordinary and still be conditioned.

It can arise depending on certain circumstances.

Remain for a while.

And disappear.

Afterward, we may return to our usual habits.

Perhaps we still worry excessively.

Perhaps we respond with hostility.

Or perhaps we continue constructing an identity around our experiences.

That doesn't mean those experiences lack value.

They can teach us a great deal.

I've personally learned extraordinarily important things from some of my meditative experiences.

But over time I began questioning an idea.

What if awakening weren't about attaining a special experience, but about deeply transforming our relationship with experience?

I'm not claiming that sentence is a complete definition of Buddhist awakening.

But perhaps it offers a starting point for investigation.

Because there's an important difference between experiencing something for a few minutes and stably transforming the conditions that organize our lives.

And here we encounter one of the most interesting questions along our entire path.

## The Ten Fetters

Early Buddhist teachings describe ten fetters, known as saṃyojana.

These are bonds that keep beings tied to saṃsāra.

Traditionally, they're divided into five lower and five higher fetters.

The lower fetters are:

1. Identity view (sakkāya-diṭṭhi).

2. Doubt (vicikicchā).

3. Mistaken attachment to rules and rituals (sīlabbata-parāmāsa).

4. Sensual desire (kāmacchanda).

5. Ill will (byāpāda).

The higher fetters are:

6. Desire for existence in the form realms (rūpa-rāga).

7. Desire for existence in the formless realms (arūpa-rāga).

8. Conceit, or the notion “I am” (māna).

9. Restlessness (uddhacca).

10. Ignorance (avijjā).

This classification appears, for instance, in the Saṃyojana Sutta (AN 10.13).

And there's something particularly interesting here.

The fetters don't simply describe unpleasant behaviors.

Some concern how we understand identity.

Others concern tendencies toward desire and rejection.

And still others concern deeper configurations of experience.

From our model's perspective, we could view them as different mechanisms contributing to the continuation of certain conditioned dynamics.

But we must be careful.

There's no established scientific correspondence between each fetter and a particular psychological or neural mechanism.

The traditional classification serves a liberating purpose.

Our interpretation tries to understand some of its functional implications.

Let's explore them.

## First Stage: Entering the Stream

The first traditional level of realization is called sotāpanna.

It's usually translated as “stream-enterer.”

I love that image.

Imagine a river.

For a long time, you've been walking along its bank.

Observing it.

Studying its waters.

Reading books about the direction it flows.

And wondering if it really reaches the sea.

Then a moment comes when you step in.

You're no longer simply looking at the path from outside.

You've started traveling it.

According to the traditional account, a stream-enterer has eliminated the first three fetters.

Identity view.

Paralyzing doubt concerning the path of liberation.

And mistaken attachment to rules and rituals as though they alone were sufficient for liberation.

Let's look at these in more detail.

**Identity view.** This doesn't mean the person stops using the word “I.”

Or loses their autobiographical memory.

Or stops recognizing their family.

The fetter concerns identifying the five aggregates of experience as a permanent self, or as belonging to such a self in the sense relevant to the teaching.

From our model's perspective, we might investigate what happens when we no longer need an independent controller to explain our mental processes.

Perhaps we begin recognizing that our intentions are conditioned too.

That thoughts arise depending on circumstances.

And that personal identity can be understood as a dynamic construction.

But we need to distinguish understanding these ideas from eliminating the fetter in the traditional sense.

Intellectual understanding may be helpful.

It doesn't, by itself, prove a liberating realization.

**Doubt.** Here we find another interesting question.

Eliminating doubt doesn't mean someone stops asking questions.

Or possesses absolute knowledge about everything.

The doubt constituting this fetter concerns fundamental uncertainty about the path of liberation and its central elements.

Someone may develop deep confidence in the Dharma while continuing to investigate historical, scientific, or philosophical questions.

In fact, mature understanding may help us recognize the limits of what we know.

We needn't turn awakening into omniscience.

**Attachment to rules and rituals.** This fetter doesn't mean we have to abandon all discipline either.

Ethical conduct remains fundamental.

What's abandoned is the mistaken understanding that following certain rules or performing certain rituals is, by itself, enough to attain purification and liberation.

From our model's perspective, this is particularly interesting.

Reproducing external behaviors isn't enough if we don't understand the conditions sustaining suffering.

We can meditate for hours.

Chant mantras.

Follow rituals.

And develop an elaborate identity around our practice.

But none of that necessarily transforms the fundamental dynamics we're trying to understand.

That's why remembering the purpose of training matters.

Not accumulating rituals.

But developing wisdom and liberation.

According to traditional Buddhist cosmology, a stream-enterer has irreversible assurance of future liberation and will not be reborn in lower destinations.

Our functional model can't verify these claims about rebirth.

We should acknowledge them as part of the traditional teaching rather than silently replacing them with a psychological explanation.

## Second Stage: Returning Once

The second level is known as sakadāgāmī.

“Once-returner.”

According to the traditional teaching, this person has eliminated the first three fetters and considerably weakened greed, aversion, and ignorance.

The name relates to the traditional claim that they will return to the human world at most once before attaining final liberation.

From our model's perspective, we can explore a possibility.

Perhaps some profound insights don't immediately eliminate every harmful tendency.

Some responses may still arise, but less intensely or less often.

And perhaps certain dynamics of attachment and aversion lose some of their ability to dominate our experience.

But we should avoid an overly simple equivalence.

We can't say that anyone who gets angry less often has attained sakadāgāmī.

Nor turn the traditional classification into a scale of emotional regulation.

The doctrine speaks of a specific liberating attainment, not merely a gradual improvement in behavior.

Even so, this stage may help us understand something important.

Transformation need not manifest solely as the sudden disappearance of all our habits.

We can also investigate how certain tendencies become weaker.

And how our relationship with them changes.

## Third Stage: Not Returning

The third level is known as anāgāmī.

“Non-returner.”

According to tradition, this person has eliminated the five lower fetters.

That includes sensual desire and ill will.

Here our functional interpretation encounters an interesting difficulty.

Because we're not simply talking about someone who knows how to restrain reactions.

The traditional account implies a far more radical transformation.

The extinction of certain forms of desire and hostility.

Not merely their regulation.

From our model's perspective, we might ask what it would mean for certain dynamics to stop arising because the fundamental conditions sustaining them had been transformed.

Not because the system was continually spending energy suppressing them.

But because they had ceased to be generated in the same way.

It's an interesting conceptual possibility.

But we shouldn't pretend to know exactly how it would happen.

Or claim that someone can prove they have attained this stage simply by describing a tranquil experience.

In traditional cosmology, a non-returner is no longer reborn in the sensual world and attains final liberation in a higher realm of existence.

Those claims belong to the doctrinal account of the path.

And we should distinguish them from any psychological reinterpretation we may propose.

## Fourth Stage: Arahant

We come to the final level in the early classification.

Arahant.

It's usually translated as “worthy one.”

In early Buddhist tradition, it designates someone who has completed liberation.

All ten fetters have been eliminated.

Not only those relating to sensual desire and ill will.

Also the desires associated with subtle forms of existence.

The conceit of “I am.”

Restlessness.

And ignorance.

There's a detail here I find extraordinarily interesting.

Identity view disappears at the first stage.

But māna, the conceit associated with “I am,” remains a higher fetter until complete liberation.

Why?

Perhaps we can understand the difference between ceasing to hold a conceptual belief in a permanent identity and eliminating much deeper forms of self-reference and comparison.

We may understand that there's no independent self.

And still experience tendencies involving self-importance.

The need to compare ourselves.

To be better.

Or to turn certain experiences into an identity.

From our model's perspective, that would fit the possibility that conceptual understanding changes before other deeply learned tendencies do.

But it's an analogy.

Not an exact identification with the traditional meaning of māna.

The arahant's liberation is described as the definitive extinction of unwholesome roots.

Not merely learning to contain them.

And that raises a difficult question.

Can we understand functionally what a truly irreversible transformation would mean?

## Liberation as a Transformation of the System

Let's return to our hypothesis.

Throughout the book, we've viewed ignorance as a conditioned configuration that contributes to perpetuating certain dynamics of suffering.

We've explored how interpretations, desires, aversions, and identifications may reinforce one another.

And how practice may develop different conditions.

But what would happen if this transformation reached the fundamental dynamics that sustain suffering?

Imagine a bonfire.

As long as we add fuel, the fire keeps burning.

We can try to reduce the intensity of the flames.

Move some of the wood away.

Or prevent the flames from spreading.

But we can also ask what happens if the conditions necessary to sustain combustion cease.

The fire would go out.

From our model's perspective, we could view liberation as the stable cessation of processes that generate and perpetuate certain fundamental forms of suffering.

But here an important distinction arises.

It's not enough for a process to stop manifesting for a while.

A latent tendency may remain, ready to activate when certain conditions appear.

Nor is it enough to develop an extraordinary ability to suppress a reaction.

Suppression means the response still needs to be regulated.

The traditional description of complete liberation points to something more radical.

An extinction of the unwholesome roots.

Therefore, our functional interpretation should ask not only how to reduce certain responses, but what it would mean for the conditions generating them to cease operating irreversibly.

We don't yet know how to demonstrate such irreversibility through everyday observation.

And perhaps that's one of the greatest difficulties in developing a testable model of awakening.

## What Does Irreversible Mean?

This word deserves our attention.

Irreversible.

Because ordinary experience shows us that habits can return.

We may develop tranquility for years.

And then go through an extraordinarily difficult situation.

We may believe we've overcome a tendency.

Only to find it still arises under certain conditions.

How, then, do we distinguish a profound transformation from a temporary disappearance?

It's not simple.

We can observe changes over months or years.

But we can't reproduce absolutely every possible situation to test how we'd respond.

Nor do we know all the latent processes in our minds.

From our model's perspective, this presents an evaluation problem.

It's one thing to establish that a pattern occurs less often.

Another to conclude that its fundamental causes have been eliminated.

And something more demanding still to claim that it can never arise again.

We shouldn't confuse them.

Perhaps we can develop practical criteria for evaluating transformations.

Greater stability.

Less harmful reactivity.

Greater ability to make amends.

Less rigid identification.

And a more compassionate relationship with ourselves and others.

But such criteria don't constitute an arahant certificate.

They're possible indicators of useful changes.

The traditional claim of irreversibility belongs to a wider framework that our model can't yet verify.

And acknowledging that seems essential to me.

## Can an Awakened Person Get Angry?

Here we reach a question you've probably heard before.

Can a fully awakened person get angry?

The answer depends, among other things, on what we mean by anger and which tradition we're using.

In the early discourses, complete liberation implies the extinction of aversion as an unwholesome root.

So it would be inaccurate to describe the traditional arahant simply as someone who still feels ordinary hatred but has learned not to act on it.

However, we should distinguish aversion from other phenomena.

We can speak firmly.

Correct behavior.

Set a boundary.

Or respond forcefully to a dangerous situation.

Those actions don't necessarily demonstrate hatred.

We can also experience startle responses, pain, or physiological changes that aren't automatically equivalent to the mental roots described by tradition.

But from outside, it's difficult to identify someone else's internal processes with certainty.

A facial expression isn't enough.

Nor is a stern sentence.

And an appearance of permanent serenity doesn't prove liberation either.

Our model may help us ask more precise questions.

What process has appeared?

What conditions sustain it?

Is there identification?

Is a dynamic of hostility being fed?

Is the response aimed at protecting or harming?

But even these questions have limits.

We can't directly observe the entirety of another person's experience.

Perhaps we should be careful before classifying them.

And before classifying ourselves too.

## Who Decides Whether Someone Is Awakened?

This is a delicate question.

Some contemplative communities use maps of progress.

Stages.

Insights.

Experiences.

And criteria to identify attainments.

They can be useful.

But they can also become sources of confusion.

Because human experience is extraordinarily complex.

One person may interpret an intense experience as definitive awakening.

Another may undergo profound changes without using any spiritual category.

And two teachers may evaluate the same account differently.

The problem gets even harder when our interpretation is conditioned by expectations.

Imagine you've spent years studying a description of awakening.

You know the stages.

The signs.

The associated experiences.

And you deeply want to attain it.

When a transformative experience arises, it may be tempting to see precisely what you've been looking for.

That doesn't mean the experience is false.

But its interpretation deserves investigation.

Here we return to autosuggestion.

And to the importance of distinguishing what we experience from what we conclude.

I don't think we should discard every map.

They can provide useful references.

But neither should we turn them into infallible certificates.

A map can help us find our bearings. It can't replace the territory.

And our model, of course, can't either.

## When the Need for a Center Disappears

I want to share one of the transformations that has most influenced my understanding of the Dharma.

For a long time, my interpretation of experience needed a center.

Something to explain who was living through it all.

An observer.

A controller.

An identity.

Or perhaps a fundamental reality behind phenomena.

As I studied the conditioned mind, I began to understand that intentions could also be viewed as processes depending on causes and conditions.

This profoundly changed the way I thought about the self.

If decisions, thoughts, and intentions arise depending on conditions, perhaps we don't need an independent controller producing them.

We can keep using the word “I.”

Recognize our history.

And assume responsibility.

But perhaps we don't need to turn that self into an independent essence.

Later, I began reviewing other ideas.

Including some of my interpretations of nonduality and emptiness.

For quite a while, I'd viewed certain experiences as possible indications of a fundamental reality.

But gradually I began asking whether those conclusions were necessary.

What if nondual experiences were conditioned experiences too?

What if emptiness didn't require imagining some ultimate substance behind every phenomenon?

What if we didn't need to find a definitive center?

These questions changed my perspective profoundly.

Not because I'd discovered a superior metaphysical explanation.

But because some explanations stopped seeming necessary.

And something curious happened.

I began feeling less need to resolve certain questions.

Not because they were absurd.

Or because I'd proved they had no answers.

But because perhaps I didn't need to resolve them to relate to my experience.

I don't want to claim this proves I've attained any traditional stage of awakening.

That would be falling into precisely one of the traps we're trying to understand.

But I can say it was an important transformation in my way of interpreting what happens.

And perhaps that's a distinction worth preserving.

We can recognize that an insight has transformed our life without needing to turn it into a certificate of final liberation.

## When a Certainty Falls Away

Something has happened repeatedly along my path.

I find an explanation.

It seems extraordinarily convincing.

For a while it organizes my understanding of experience.

Then an observation arises that forces me to revisit it.

At first that could be uncomfortable.

Because our explanations can become objects of attachment too.

We identify with them.

Defend them.

And perhaps feel that questioning them means questioning ourselves.

But over time I've begun to value that process differently.

Revising an idea doesn't necessarily mean losing something valuable.

Sometimes it means we've learned.

And perhaps some of the most important transformations happen when we no longer need to defend certain certainties.

Not because every idea is equally valid.

Or because we should give up investigation.

But because we can learn to hold our explanations more flexibly.

This reminds me of a saying attributed to Socrates.

“All I know is that I know nothing.”

We don't need to take it literally.

We know many things.

And can learn many more.

But perhaps wisdom also involves recognizing how far our knowledge reaches.

And no longer turning its limits into a personal threat.

## Awakening Needn't Produce a Perfect Person

Here's a difficulty I want to raise.

When we picture an awakened person, we may construct an extraordinarily idealized image.

Always calm.

Always kind.

Always wise.

Incapable of making a mistake.

And probably able to find parking in any city without losing patience.

But we need to distinguish doctrinal claims about the extinction of unwholesome roots from other expectations we add ourselves.

Buddhist tradition attributes a radical transformation regarding greed, aversion, and ignorance to the arahant.

We shouldn't reduce those claims to an ordinary improvement in habits.

But neither do we have reason to conclude that every liberated person possesses unlimited knowledge of every subject.

Never forgets a name.

Is an expert in every human relationship.

Or never makes a practical mistake.

Spiritual liberation, as described by a tradition, isn't necessarily equivalent to physical, intellectual, or technical perfection.

And here I find an important idea for our book.

We don't need to turn the ideal of awakening into a fantasy of human perfection.

We can deeply value wisdom.

Compassion.

And the reduction of suffering.

Without constructing a supernatural character we must imitate in absolutely everything.

Because perhaps that perfect image becomes another source of suffering.

Especially when we use it to judge ourselves.

## What If Liberation Doesn't Look the Same for Everyone?

Now I want to ask a question.

If every mind depends on a different history of causes and conditions, should we expect all liberated people to be psychologically identical?

That doesn't seem a necessary conclusion.

We can imagine certain roots of suffering having ceased without every personal characteristic disappearing.

People may have different abilities.

Different knowledge.

Memories.

Conventional preferences.

And different ways of expressing themselves.

We needn't assume liberation implies one uniform personality.

But neither should we use that argument to empty traditional descriptions of their meaning.

If a teaching says a particular unwholesome root has been extinguished, we can't simply assert that its ordinary presence is compatible with that extinction.

We need to distinguish personal characteristics from the specific mechanisms the tradition regards as liberated.

From our model's perspective, perhaps we can investigate a hypothesis.

No two mental systems are exactly alike.

And so a profound transformation might manifest differently according to each person's conditions.

But that doesn't mean every behavior is compatible with every attainment.

Our model should help us ask better questions.

Not justify any response that suits us.

## Awakening and Love

There's something I don't want to lose sight of.

Throughout the book, we've proposed three trainings.

Love.

Concentration.

Investigation.

And perhaps we can now view them from another perspective.

Concentration develops stability and clarity.

Investigation may transform our understanding of the conditions generating suffering.

And love guides our relationship with ourselves and others.

But what happens when these abilities develop profoundly?

Perhaps certain forms of hostility weaken.

Perhaps we stop needing to defend our identity constantly.

And perhaps understanding others' suffering better makes compassionate responses easier.

We shouldn't assume understanding automatically produces love.

Some people can develop extraordinarily sophisticated theories of human beings and treat those around them terribly.

That's why I think love also needs to be cultivated through our actions.

But I still find a deep relationship between love and wisdom.

When we understand that people depend on causes and conditions, perhaps it becomes easier to abandon certain forms of contempt.

When we recognize our own vulnerability, perhaps we can treat ourselves more kindly.

And when we stop turning interpretations into absolute truths, perhaps we can listen better.

I don't know whether love and wisdom are two names for the same phenomenon.

It would probably be too simple to claim that.

But I find it hard to imagine truly liberating wisdom that doesn't profoundly transform our relationship with the suffering of others.

And perhaps that transformation is one of the most valuable things we can cultivate.

## Can Functional Liberation Exist?

We now reach one of the central questions of our book.

Can we construct a functional explanation of awakening?

Perhaps, at least partly.

We can consider certain processes that generate suffering.

Rigid identification.

Craving.

Aversion.

Self-reinforcing interpretations.

And habits that keep feeding those dynamics.

We can investigate how they arise.

What conditions sustain them.

And how certain practices help transform them.

We can also observe changes over time.

But there's something we must acknowledge.

Our model cannot, by itself, demonstrate the definitive liberation described in traditional teachings.

We can't verify all claims about rebirth.

Or establish a universal test of irreversibility.

Nor do we have an objective measurement certifying that all roots of ignorance have disappeared.

So perhaps we should distinguish functional liberation, defined by criteria we can investigate, from complete liberation as described by a religious tradition.

The first may allow us to study certain transformations.

The second includes claims our model can't fully resolve.

We needn't deny one to study the other.

But neither should we confuse them.

And I think that distinction could be very valuable for the future.

Perhaps we can investigate how mental tendencies change through contemplative training.

Compare experiences.

Study their conditions.

And develop increasingly accurate models.

Without deciding in advance what outcomes we must find.

## A Small Experiment: What Has Really Changed?

I want to suggest a different exercise.

We're not going to try to attain awakening.

Or evaluate whether we've broken any of the ten fetters.

We'll investigate something simpler.

Think of a situation that caused you suffering a few years ago.

Perhaps criticism.

An expectation.

A worry.

Or a difficult relationship.

Now ask yourself:

How did I respond before?

What interpretations arose?

What did I need to obtain or avoid?

And what happens today in similar situations?

Perhaps you'll discover some responses have changed.

Or perhaps you'll recognize similar dynamics still arising.

You don't need to judge the result.

Simply observe.

Then ask yourself a more interesting question.

What conditions contributed to that change, if it really happened?

Perhaps a life experience.

A conversation.

Meditation practice.

Learning a skill.

Or a change in circumstances.

You don't need to attribute every change automatically to the Dharma.

We also learn through countless experiences we don't consider spiritual.

Finally, ask yourself:

Has that change helped reduce unnecessary suffering?

Has it improved how I relate to others?

Am I more able to recognize my mistakes?

And am I still willing to learn?

These aren't sufficient criteria for certifying spiritual attainment.

But they can help us evaluate whether our path is producing changes we consider valuable.

And perhaps that's a sensible way to keep investigating.

## The Danger of Needing to Be Awakened

There's one final trap I want to mention.

We may become interested in awakening.

Study its stages.

Compare our experiences.

And develop a deep desire to attain one of them.

So far, perhaps we're simply expressing a sincere aspiration.

But something else may arise.

The need to prove we've arrived.

To turn our experiences into an identity.

To declare that we've understood what others still don't know.

And perhaps begin defending that conclusion with extraordinary intensity.

Here our model invites us to recognize an uncomfortable possibility.

The very idea of being awakened can become another object of attachment.

We can construct a new image of ourselves.

A spiritual identity.

And begin protecting it through precisely the dynamics we were trying to understand.

That's why I consider it especially important to maintain an attitude of radical honesty.

We can recognize transformative experiences.

Explain our interpretations.

And share what we've learned.

Without needing to become unquestionable authorities.

We needn't dismiss the changes we've experienced either.

It's not about denying our learning.

It's about distinguishing it from conclusions we can't yet justify.

And perhaps that distinction is one of the most important forms of humility.

## The Raft

There's a teaching of the Buddha I find especially beautiful.

We find it in the Alagaddūpama Sutta (MN 22).

The Dharma is compared to a raft.

Imagine you need to cross a river.

The bank you're on is dangerous.

The other seems safe.

There's no bridge.

But you can build a raft.

You gather materials.

Work.

And finally manage to cross.

What would you do afterward?

Carry the raft on your shoulders for the rest of your life?

Make it your most treasured possession?

Or recognize that it has served its purpose?

The teaching is clear.

The Dharma is a means of crossing.

Not something we should cling to indefinitely.

And here I find an extraordinary connection with our model.

Our model is a raft too.

A tool.

An interpretation.

A way of understanding certain relationships.

Perhaps it will help us investigate our experience.

Perhaps it will allow us to develop useful practices.

And perhaps it will even help transform some of the conditions sustaining our suffering.

But we don't need to turn it into an absolute truth.

If we find a better explanation, we can revise it.

If we discover that part of it doesn't work, we can change it.

And if a time comes when we no longer need it, perhaps we can let it go.

There's something funny about writing a book of several hundred pages only to end up explaining that perhaps we should learn to let go of it.

But I suspect it would be much worse to write it in an attempt to convince you that you need to carry it on your shoulders for the rest of your life.

## And Afterward?

Perhaps you're wondering what happens after awakening.

According to traditional teachings, an arahant continues living until the end of that life, but the roots fueling suffering and rebirth have been extinguished.

In Mahāyāna Buddhism, we find other ideals and explanations too, particularly those related to the bodhisattva path and compassionate activity.

We'll return to these differences in the next chapter.

But from our perspective, I want to ask a much closer question.

What happens when we no longer need to keep resolving our own identity?

When certain worries lose their importance.

When certain struggles stop being fed.

Does that mean there's nothing left to do?

Not necessarily.

We can keep learning.

Caring.

Sharing.

And participating in the world.

Perhaps without needing to turn every action into a demonstration of who we are.

We can write.

Teach.

Help.

Play with our children.

And enjoy the small experiences of our lives.

Not because we've discovered some metaphysical duty to save the world.

But because those actions may arise from our abilities, values, and the conditions we've cultivated.

Perhaps a valuable way to understand liberation is recognizing that we don't need to make ourselves the center of everything that happens.

We can participate.

Without needing to possess it.

And perhaps that allows love to find increasingly natural expression.

## Awakening

We've explored the four traditional levels of realization.

Stream-entry.

Once-returning.

Non-returning.

And arahantship.

We've also examined the ten fetters and their relationship to our functional interpretation of suffering.

We've recognized that some traditional claims, particularly those involving rebirth and irreversible liberation, can't be verified with our current model.

And that a transformative experience doesn't, by itself, demonstrate attainment of any of those stages.

But perhaps we've found some valuable questions.

What conditions perpetuate our identification?

How are certain forms of desire and aversion sustained?

What does it mean to transform a deeply ingrained habit?

And what would have to happen for some dynamics to stop being fueled permanently?

We don't have all the answers.

And perhaps we don't need them before starting to practice.

We can learn to recognize suffering.

Understand its conditions.

Cultivate love.

Develop concentration.

And investigate our experience.

Perhaps we'll discover profound transformations.

Perhaps some of our certainties will disappear.

And perhaps over time certain questions will stop seeming quite so important.

Not because we've proved we understand all of reality.

But because we've learned to relate to it differently.

I don't know exactly what it means to attain definitive liberation.

And it would be dishonest to claim I could recognize it with absolute certainty.

But I do believe we can learn to live with less unnecessary suffering and with greater love and wisdom.

And that it's worth investigating how far that learning might take us.

Perhaps awakening isn't about finally finding someone who controls our experience, but about understanding its conditions so deeply that we no longer need such a controller.

It's a hypothesis.

A personal interpretation.

And perhaps tomorrow we'll discover a reason to revise it.

Wonderful.

The path continues.

But there's something I don't want us to forget.

We don't have to wait for awakening before beginning to live better.

Or to love the people around us.

Or to do something good for the world.

We can begin now.

With our hands.

And with the conditions we have.

In the next chapter we'll broaden our perspective.

Because Buddhism isn't one single, perfectly uniform teaching transmitted unchanged for twenty-five hundred years.

There are different traditions.

Different interpretations.
