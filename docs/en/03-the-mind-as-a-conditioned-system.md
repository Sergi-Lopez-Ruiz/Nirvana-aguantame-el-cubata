# 3. The Mind as a Conditioned System

## A Debt to Culadasa

There's something I want to make clear before we continue.

This model didn't come out of nowhere. One of its main sources of inspiration is the Mind-System Model presented by Culadasa (John Yates) in *The Mind Illuminated*, usually known as TMI.

That book has had an enormous influence on my practice and on how I understand the mind.

TMI is fundamentally a meditation training manual. Its ten-stage structure is aimed at developing *śamatha*, a state of attentional stability, clarity, tranquillity, and equanimity that also supports the development of *vipassanā*: deep understanding of experience.

To explain how this training works, Culadasa introduces several models of the mind.

One of them, the Mind-System Model, suggests that we consider the mind not as a single, centralised entity but as a collection of interdependent processes.

Culadasa uses the idea of multiple sub-minds that process information, generate intentions, and communicate through consciousness.

There isn't a little managing director sitting inside our heads making all the decisions.

Which, considering some of the decisions I've made in my life, may come as a relief.

This model helped me enormously in understanding my own meditation experiences.

But over time, I began wondering about something.

What happens if we use that same perspective to explore other aspects of the Dharma?

What if the mechanisms that help us understand how concentration develops also allow us to investigate attachment, aversion, karma, the construction of the self, or even freedom from suffering?

Perhaps we aren't proposing a different model.

Perhaps we're looking at the same system from different angles, asking different questions to investigate how it works.

That, at least, is one of the possibilities I want to explore in this book.

I don't intend to attribute the conclusions I'll develop here to Culadasa. Some are my personal interpretations; others come from different Buddhist traditions; and others have emerged from trying to connect these teachings with my own experience.

But I want to acknowledge his influence.

And if you ever decide to delve more deeply into meditation practice, I recommend getting to know his work.

Now, let's get back to our conditioned system.

---

I have bad news.

You may not be quite who you think you are.

And I have even worse news.

Perhaps you aren't the one who decides what you'll want for breakfast tomorrow, either.

But don't worry. We're not going to take away your free will in the first few pages of the book. At least, not entirely.

Let's begin with something rather simpler.

Why do we do what we do?

Imagine someone insults you.

You might feel anger. Perhaps a sensation of heat in your body, muscle tension, and an almost irresistible urge to respond.

Or perhaps you couldn't care less.

Or, on a particularly inspired day, you might find it funny.

What determines your reaction?

The insult?

Obviously, it plays a part.

But it's not the only factor.

It also matters who insults you, what your relationship with that person is, what their words mean to you, how you're feeling at that moment, what experiences you've had before, and an enormous number of circumstances you probably don't even know about.

If you've slept badly for three nights, you may respond differently than you would after a wonderful holiday.

If the person insulting you is your five-year-old child, your reaction may be different from your reaction to a stranger.

And if the insult is in a language you don't understand, you probably have less reason to be offended.

Though depending on the tone, you might get angry anyway.

The interesting thing is that the same event can produce very different responses depending on the conditions in which it occurs.

This apparently simple observation is the starting point for our model.

## What Is a System?

As a computer engineer, I have a certain occupational bias.

When I try to understand something complicated, I like thinking in terms of systems.

A system is a collection of elements or processes that interact with each other. Its behaviour depends on those interactions, on how it is organised, on its history, and on the conditions in which it finds itself.

Think of something as everyday as a thermostat.

A thermostat measures the temperature of a room and, depending on its settings, switches the heating on or off.

When the room cools down, it turns the heating on. When the temperature reaches a certain level, it turns it off.

It's a simple system that responds to particular conditions.

Now imagine adding windows, people coming and going, changes in the outdoor temperature, several radiators, and a neighbour who insists on leaving the door open.

The system's behaviour starts getting more complicated.

And we haven't even added anyone arguing about what the ideal temperature should be.

Complex systems can display behaviours that we can't understand simply by studying each component separately. Their interactions generate new dynamics and sometimes results that are hard to anticipate.

The human mind is incomparably more complex than a thermostat.

But we can ask ourselves a question:

What if we tried to understand the mind as a system of interdependent processes?

Not as a machine with an operator sitting inside it, but as a dynamic collection of processes influencing one another.

Sensations, perceptions, memories, emotions, thoughts, intentions, and bodily responses.

All of them interacting, changing, and helping to determine what happens next.

## Actions and Reactions

We usually distinguish between things we do voluntarily and things that happen to us.

We consider deciding to get out of a chair voluntary, but feeling afraid when we hear a loud noise involuntary.

Yet let's look a little more closely.

Imagine you're quietly reading when a thought appears:

“I fancy a coffee.”

Where did it come from?

Perhaps you smelled coffee.

Perhaps you've been tired for a while.

Perhaps you usually drink coffee at this time.

Or perhaps someone mentioned coffee a few minutes ago, and your memory retrieved that association.

The fact is, the thought appeared.

You might then consider different possibilities.

Should I get up? Finish this chapter? Is it too late to have caffeine?

Maybe you remember that you slept badly yesterday and decide to drink water instead.

You'll experience that decision as something you've done yourself.

And there's no need to deny that experience.

But we can investigate which processes participated in it.

Desire, memory, deliberation, preferences, habits, and the decision itself can all be understood as events that depend on earlier conditions.

This brings us to an important hypothesis in our model:

What we call an action can also be understood as a conditioned response that helps create new conditions.

That doesn't mean all actions are automatic reflexes.

Nor does it mean they are inevitable or entirely predictable.

Some responses involve enormously complex processes of reflection, planning, learning, and deliberation.

But those processes are also part of the system we're trying to understand.

Instead of imagining an absolute divide between voluntary actions and reactions, we'll investigate the conditions that contribute to both kinds of events arising.

## A Mind That Changes Itself

Here comes one of the parts that fascinates me most.

Our mind's responses don't just depend on present conditions.

They can also modify future conditions.

Imagine that every time someone criticises you, you react angrily.

Criticism triggers anger. Anger influences your thoughts. Those thoughts may feed the anger even further. Eventually, you respond aggressively.

And perhaps that response strengthens your tendency to interpret criticism as a threat.

Next time, the process may repeat itself more easily.

But something different can happen, too.

Perhaps one day you recognise the anger before responding.

You notice the tension in your body, recognise the thoughts that are appearing, and remember that on other occasions you ended up saying things you regretted.

You decide not to respond immediately.

You might still be angry.

Very angry.

But this time something is different.

Anger doesn't completely determine your behaviour.

You've introduced other conditions into the system.

With repetition, that new way of responding can become a habit.

And habits, in turn, influence future responses.

We can describe processes in which results influence subsequent conditions using the idea of feedback.

Some dynamics reinforce themselves. Others help stabilise the system or change its behaviour.

Not all learning works in such a simple way, but the idea is useful: our history conditions our responses, and our responses help build our history.

It's a circular relationship.

And it has an extraordinary consequence.

If we can modify some of the conditions that influence our reactions, we can gradually transform our habits.

We don't need to control every process in our mind.

We don't even need to understand them completely.

Sometimes changing one apparently small condition can alter a much broader dynamic.

Sleeping better.

Stepping away from a situation that feeds a harmful habit.

Learning to recognise an emotion.

Practising a different response.

Or simply stopping punishing ourselves for making a mistake.

All these actions can introduce new conditions.

And those conditions are part of the system, too.

## Where Is the One in Charge?

At this point, you may be wondering something.

If my thoughts, emotions, intentions, and decisions depend on causes and conditions ...

Where does that leave me?

Good question.

And one that has kept philosophers, scientists, and contemplatives busy for centuries.

We usually have the sense that there's someone in charge.

An “I” who observes, thinks, decides, and acts.

But do we need to assume the existence of a controller independent of all those processes?

Think of a company.

A company can make decisions, hire employees, develop products, and make monumental mistakes.

It's perfectly reasonable to say, “The company has decided to change its strategy.”

Yet when we investigate how that decision was made, we find people, meetings, documents, interests, arguments, and organisational processes.

We don't need to imagine a tiny character called “The Company” living inside the building and pulling the strings.

Although, having worked in large organisations, I admit it would sometimes be a tempting explanation.

Perhaps we can view the “I” in a similar way.

As a way of organising and understanding certain processes in our experience.

That doesn't mean the self doesn't exist in any sense.

People exist. We have histories, relationships, responsibilities, and capacities for making decisions.

But perhaps the sense of being a separate, independent controller doesn't fully describe how our mental processes work.

We'll explore this question in much greater depth in another chapter.

For now, we don't need to decide exactly what the self is.

It's enough to recognise that many things we consider “ours” arise depending on conditions we haven't consciously chosen.

## Are We Condemned to React?

Here an important objection arises.

If everything is conditioned, does that mean there's nothing we can do?

Are we simply puppets of our circumstances?

Not necessarily.

In fact, our model suggests almost the opposite.

Think of a seed.

Its growth depends on many conditions: water, temperature, nutrients, light, and other circumstances.

The seed can't decide to become an oak through sheer willpower.

But that doesn't mean its development is independent of what happens around it.

If we change the conditions, we can change the outcome.

Something similar happens with us.

We don't freely choose every emotion, memory, preference, or thought.

But we can learn, reflect, ask for help, modify our surroundings, and develop new abilities.

And all that learning also influences our future responses.

Being conditioned doesn't mean being condemned to remain as we are. It means our capacity to change depends on conditions, too.

This opens up a very different perspective on personal development.

Instead of constantly asking why we can't behave as we'd like, we can investigate which conditions are favouring particular behaviours.

Perhaps we don't need more willpower.

Perhaps we need sleep.

Or to stop repeatedly exposing ourselves to something that harms us.

Or to spend months practising an ability we haven't developed yet.

Or to acknowledge that we're trying to solve alone a problem for which we need help.

It won't always be easy. Some conditions are deeply resistant to change, while others are beyond our reach.

But the possibility of influencing the system exists.

And that possibility will be fundamental to understanding the path of practice we'll explore later.

## Compassion as a Consequence

There's another implication of this model that I find especially important.

When we begin to understand our responses as conditioned processes, the way we judge ourselves can change.

Imagine you've spent years reacting with fear to certain situations.

Perhaps you've told yourself many times that you ought to be braver.

That you shouldn't feel afraid.

That there's something wrong with you.

But what happens if we begin investigating the conditions that helped create that fear?

Perhaps we find past experiences, thinking habits, bodily responses, or circumstances we'd never considered.

That doesn't make the fear disappear automatically.

But it can transform our relationship with it.

Instead of treating ourselves as an enemy to be defeated, we can begin to understand ourselves.

And that understanding can give rise to compassion.

The same happens when we observe other people.

Someone behaves unpleasantly, and our first reaction may be to judge them.

But what do we really know about the conditions that contributed to their behaviour?

Probably very little.

That doesn't mean we should accept any behaviour whatsoever.

Understanding the causes of aggression doesn't eliminate the need to protect those who suffer it, set boundaries, or demand accountability.

It means we can try to respond clearly without necessarily adding hatred to the situation.

Personally, this has been one of the most important transformations in the way I look at the world.

I've begun to see people—including myself—less as characters defined by their virtues and flaws, and more as extraordinarily complex beings with histories we barely know.

I don't always manage it.

Sometimes my mind still produces judgements at an admirable speed.

But it gets easier and easier to recognise them for what they are: new conditioned responses.

And sometimes that's enough to stop them having the final say.

## A Model, Not an Absolute Truth

Before we go on, I want to offer a warning.

We're building a model.

We haven't demonstrated that all mental activity can be fully explained using the concepts we've just introduced.

Nor have we solved the philosophical problem of free will, the nature of consciousness, or the relationship between mind and brain.

And we don't need to.

Our purpose is more modest.

We'll use the idea of conditioning to investigate our experiences, our reactions, and the possibilities for transformation within our reach.

If the model helps us understand something, wonderful.

If we encounter phenomena it doesn't adequately explain, we'll have to revise it.

In fact, there's something particularly amusing about all this.

The model itself is also a product of causes and conditions.

My experiences, my knowledge, the teachings I've studied, the people I've spoken with, and even conversations with an artificial intelligence have all helped shape it.

It didn't appear in a mind independent of the universe, free of influences, that has finally discovered how everything works.

How disappointing.

But perhaps that's good news, too.

Because we don't need a perfect explanation to learn something useful.

And recognising the limitations of our ideas can help us use them more wisely.

## A Little Experiment

Let's finish with a simple suggestion.

Next time you experience an intense emotional reaction, try asking yourself three questions:

What's happening?

Observe the bodily sensations, emotions, and thoughts that appear.

What conditions might be influencing this?

Perhaps you're tired. Perhaps something reminds you of an earlier experience. Perhaps you have expectations that aren't being met.

You don't need to discover every cause. That's probably impossible.

What conditions can I change?

Perhaps you can rest, change your surroundings, ask for help, or simply give yourself a few seconds before responding.

Don't turn the exercise into an obligation.

And if you forget to do it, don't worry.

Forgetting is a conditioned response, too.

We'll have plenty of opportunities to work with that.

## A New Way of Looking

We've introduced our first major hypothesis.

The mind can be understood as a dynamic system of interdependent processes that arise and change depending on causes and conditions.

Our experiences influence our responses.

Our responses help create new conditions.

And those conditions can favour the emergence of certain habits, emotions, thoughts, and intentions.

We aren't necessarily the independent controllers we sometimes imagine ourselves to be.

But neither are we systems incapable of transformation.

We can learn to recognise the conditions that influence us, develop abilities, and modify the way we respond.

Perhaps the freedom we're looking for doesn't consist in ceasing to be conditioned, but in transforming our relationship with those conditions.

And perhaps an important part of wisdom lies precisely in understanding what we can change, what we cannot change, and how to relate to both.

In the next chapter, we'll explore causes, conditions, and their consequences in greater depth.

Because saying that everything is conditioned is relatively easy.

What's truly interesting is discovering what that means when we begin observing our own lives.
