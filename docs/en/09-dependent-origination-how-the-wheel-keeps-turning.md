# 9. Dependent Origination: How the Wheel Keeps Turning

Imagine you're arguing with someone.

The other person says something you don't like.

You get angry.

You respond rather aggressively.

The other person gets angry too.

They answer even more harshly.

And within seconds, two people who probably love each other are having an argument neither of them wanted.

The curious thing is that if we asked them separately what happened, both would probably be convinced that the other person started it.

And perhaps both would have rather convincing arguments.

Welcome to the wonderful world of causes and conditions.

In previous chapters, we've proposed viewing the mind as a conditioned system.

We've seen that our experiences depend on many factors, that our responses can change subsequent conditions, and that some processes can reinforce themselves through repetition.

Now we're going to explore one of Buddhism's most profound teachings: dependent origination, known in Pali as *paṭiccasamuppāda*.

Its fundamental formulation can be expressed quite simply:

When this exists, that comes to be. With the arising of this, that arises. When this does not exist, that does not come to be. With the cessation of this, that ceases.

It almost sounds too obvious.

Things happen because particular conditions are present.

But the implications of this idea can be extraordinary.

Because if what we experience depends on conditions, understanding those conditions can help us understand how our suffering arises.

And perhaps even more importantly, how it can cease.

## A wheel that keeps turning

In Buddhism we find a well-known image: the wheel of *saṃsāra*.

Traditionally, *saṃsāra* refers to the cycle of birth, death and rebirth, associated with suffering and sustained by ignorance and craving.

But we can also use the image of a wheel to investigate the dynamics that repeat themselves in our everyday experience.

Imagine you're prone to worrying.

An unpleasant thought about the future appears.

That thought creates anxiety.

Anxiety makes you pay more attention to possible threats.

You find new reasons to worry.

And those reasons fuel your anxiety even more.

The wheel keeps turning.

It doesn't need someone outside to push it at every moment. The system's own dynamics help keep it moving.

Over time, certain patterns can become deeply rooted habits.

That doesn't mean all our worries are imaginary or unfounded.

It means that, as well as responding to real circumstances, our minds can develop processes that maintain or amplify particular experiences.

And here an interesting question arises:

What conditions keep our wheels turning?

## The twelve links

The Buddha presented an explanation of dependent origination through a sequence of twelve factors, or links.

Don't panic.

We're not going to turn this chapter into a Pali exam.

Although if you manage to remember all twelve names on your first attempt, perhaps we should consider entering you in a competition.

The twelve links are:

1. **Avijjā — Ignorance.** Failure to understand fundamental aspects of reality and suffering adequately, especially the Four Noble Truths.
2. **Saṅkhārā — Volitional formations.** Conditioned activities and constructions related to intention and action.
3. **Viññāṇa — Consciousness.** Forms of consciousness associated with different modes of experience.
4. **Nāmarūpa — Name and form.** The mental and material aspects that constitute individual experience.
5. **Saḷāyatana — The six sense bases.** Sight, hearing, smell, taste, touch and mind.
6. **Phassa — Contact.** The meeting of a sense faculty, its object and the corresponding consciousness.
7. **Vedanā — Feeling tone.** The pleasant, unpleasant or neutral quality of an experience.
8. **Taṇhā — Craving.** Compulsive desire to obtain, maintain or eliminate experiences.
9. **Upādāna — Clinging.** Appropriation of and attachment to what we desire, believe or regard as ours.
10. **Bhava — Becoming or existence.** The processes that condition a particular form of existence.
11. **Jāti — Birth.** The arising of a being in a form of existence.
12. **Jarāmaraṇa — Ageing and death.** Together with pain, sadness, lamentation and other forms of suffering.

In traditional interpretations, this sequence explains the continuity of *saṃsāra*. Some schools explicitly develop it across multiple lives, while other readings focus on its functioning within present experience.

We shouldn't pretend that all these interpretations say exactly the same thing.

Our aim is more limited: to explore what this teaching can tell us about the processes that sustain suffering.

And to do that, we'll pay particular attention to some of the relationships between its links.

## From feeling to suffering

Imagine someone makes an unpleasant comment about you.

You hear their words.

An auditory experience occurs.

You recognise what they've said.

And that experience acquires an unpleasant feeling tone.

So far, we can identify some elements of the traditional sequence: contact and *vedanā*.

Let's pause here for a moment.

*Vedanā* doesn't mean exactly the same thing as emotion.

It's a more elementary characteristic of experience: its pleasant, unpleasant or neutral tone.

For example, we can experience an unpleasant physical sensation without yet developing a complex emotion such as fear or anger.

This distinction will be fundamental when we study *vipassanā*.

Let's continue with our example.

You've heard an unpleasant comment.

Discomfort arises.

Then perhaps an intense wish appears that it had never happened.

Maybe you want the other person to take back their words.

Perhaps you need to prove them wrong.

Or maybe you feel an impulse to respond aggressively.

We've moved from the unpleasant feeling tone of an experience to a possible manifestation of *taṇhā*.

And now something interesting happens.

The initial experience may be over.

But the craving continues.

Now you need the situation to change.

You need the other person to apologise.

To admit that you're right.

Or, at the very least, for the universe to acknowledge that you've been treated unfairly.

If you begin identifying with that need, clinging may arise.

“I can't let people talk to me like that.”

“I'm not the kind of person who lets others treat me that way.”

“I have to prove who's right.”

The situation is no longer just about having heard unpleasant words.

It can now become part of a story about who you are, what you deserve and how others ought to behave towards you.

And that story can help keep the reaction going.

From our model's perspective, we can interpret this dynamic as a succession of processes conditioning one another.

Discomfort encourages certain responses.

Those responses generate new thoughts and emotions.

And those thoughts and emotions can reinforce the original dynamic.

The wheel turns.

But there's an important detail.

Not every unpleasant sensation has to turn into craving.

We can experience something unpleasant and acknowledge it without developing a struggle against it.

And that possibility is fundamental to understanding the Buddhist path.

## Ignorance: what aren't we seeing?

Now we reach one of the concepts that interests me most.

*Avijjā*, ignorance.

In Buddhism, ignorance doesn't simply mean lacking information.

We're not talking about someone who can't solve differential equations or doesn't know the capital of Mongolia.

In the ancient texts, it is particularly associated with not understanding the Four Noble Truths.

It's ignorance about suffering, its causes, its cessation and the path leading to its cessation.

But how might we view it through our model?

Let me suggest an interpretation.

Perhaps we can understand ignorance functionally as a configuration of conditioned processes that encourages the arising and perpetuation of dynamics that generate suffering.

It wouldn't simply be an empty space where knowledge is missing.

It would be a way of perceiving, interpreting and reacting that has developed and been reinforced over time.

Imagine someone who's learned to interpret any criticism as a threat to their personal worth.

That interpretation may be so habitual that they don't even recognise it as an interpretation.

They hear criticism.

Feel discomfort.

Anger arises.

They respond defensively.

And perhaps their response ends up producing new conflicts that reinforce the feeling of being surrounded by hostile people.

The system doesn't need someone to consciously decide to keep the problem going.

Its own responses can help reproduce it.

From this perspective, ignorance wouldn't simply mean not knowing the correct explanation.

It could also manifest as deeply entrenched habits that keep operating even when we know intellectually that they're harming us.

This would help explain something very common.

We can know perfectly well that a reaction hurts us and still keep repeating it.

Because understanding something intellectually doesn't necessarily mean we've transformed all the conditions that encourage that response.

## Wisdom can become a habit too

If certain conditioned processes encourage suffering, we can ask what happens when we develop different ones.

Imagine the same person who interprets every criticism as a threat.

But now they've spent some time learning to observe their reactions.

They recognise the bodily sensations that appear when they feel questioned.

They identify their defensive thoughts.

They've learned that criticism doesn't necessarily determine their worth as a person.

And they've developed the ability to listen without responding immediately.

One day, someone criticises them.

Perhaps discomfort still arises.

Perhaps even anger.

But other responses arise too.

Curiosity.

Attention.

The ability to recognise that the other person might be right.

Or simply the possibility of staying silent for a few seconds.

The system has changed.

Not because an independent controller has appeared, imposing its will from outside.

But because new conditions have developed that influence what happens.

Here we can introduce an interesting interpretation of wisdom.

Perhaps, besides being understanding, it can manifest as a collection of conditioned abilities and tendencies that allow us to respond in ways that don't perpetuate suffering.

That doesn't mean wisdom is merely another habit, or that our interpretation exhausts its Buddhist meaning.

But it allows us to explore an important possibility.

Ignorance and wisdom could manifest as different dynamics within the same conditioned system.

Not necessarily as two opposing substances.

Nor as two little characters fighting for control of our heads.

Although I admit that would make for a rather entertaining explanation.

Rather, as different tendencies that can influence our interpretations, intentions and responses.

And with practice, some of these tendencies can grow stronger while others lose their influence.

This leads us to an even more interesting question.

What would happen if the processes that feed suffering stopped receiving the conditions they need to continue?

## When the wheel is no longer fed

Think of a bonfire.

For it to keep burning, certain conditions are necessary.

Fuel.

Oxygen.

Enough heat.

If we stop adding fuel, the fire can gradually go out.

We don't need to convince each individual flame to stop burning.

We only need to change the conditions that sustain combustion.

We can use this image to explore certain mental dynamics.

Imagine you've developed a habit of feeding your anger through repetitive thoughts.

Every time you remember an unpleasant situation, you reconstruct it.

You imagine new arguments.

You recall other occasions when you felt unfairly treated.

And in this way, you help keep the reaction alive.

But perhaps you develop the ability to notice these thoughts without continuing to feed them.

The memories may still arise.

So may the unpleasant feeling.

But you no longer need to turn every appearance into a new story.

Over time, that dynamic might lose strength.

That doesn't mean all conditioning disappears immediately.

Some habits can remain present for a very long time.

Others may reappear when we're tired, stressed or in particularly difficult circumstances.

But we can begin to distinguish between the arising of a conditioned response and the additional conditions that help sustain it.

And here we find one of the most interesting possibilities of our model.

Perhaps we don't need to eliminate all our tendencies immediately in order to stop feeding some of the dynamics that generate suffering.

It's one thing for a habit still to arise.

It's quite another for each occurrence to reinforce it.

The Buddhist tradition envisions a far more radical liberation than occasionally interrupting a reaction. But this distinction can help us understand certain mechanisms of change that we can investigate through practice.

## And what about karma?

If our responses can influence future conditions, a natural connection appears with another Buddhist concept: karma.

In its traditional sense, *kamma* is fundamentally concerned with intentional action.

And intentional actions have consequences.

Through our model, we can investigate how particular intentions and behaviours contribute to developing future tendencies and habits.

If we continually respond with hostility, we may encourage patterns of hostility.

If we practise generosity, we may find it easier to act generously.

If we train attention and equanimity, we may change our usual ways of responding.

But we need to avoid oversimplifying.

Buddhist karma isn't simply any physical relationship between cause and effect, nor can it be reduced entirely to the psychological formation of habits.

It includes ethical dimensions and, in many traditional interpretations, consequences that extend beyond a single lifetime.

Our model doesn't need to resolve all these questions to explore something we can observe.

Our actions can help build the conditions of our future actions.

And that makes how we act a fundamental part of training.

In the next chapter, we'll study karma in greater depth.

## Is it really a chain?

So far, we've presented dependent origination through a sequence of twelve links.

But it's worth remembering that representations are tools.

A linear chain can be useful for explaining certain relationships.

However, our model of the mind considers processes that continually interact.

A thought can influence an emotion.

An emotion can change attention.

Attention can influence the thoughts that arise.

And those new experiences can change subsequent conditions.

That's why, from a systems-theory perspective, it's interesting to investigate circular relationships and feedback processes.

But we need to be careful here.

We're not claiming that the Buddha presented exactly our modern model of dynamic systems.

Nor can we assume the twelve links describe twelve psychological operations that always occur in a strict order every time someone gets angry.

In traditional texts, concepts such as birth, becoming and consciousness have meanings that we shouldn't simply replace with modern psychological interpretations.

Our proposal is to use dependent origination as inspiration for investigating systems of causes and conditions that can sustain suffering.

And to acknowledge that this perspective is an interpretation, not the only possible reading of the teaching.

## A small experiment

I'd like you to investigate an everyday reaction that repeats fairly often.

It doesn't have to be particularly intense.

Perhaps you get irritated when someone interrupts you.

Perhaps you keep checking your phone.

Perhaps you worry when someone takes a long time to reply to a message.

Choose a simple situation.

The next time it arises, try to observe how it unfolds.

What happened beforehand?

What pleasant or unpleasant feeling arose?

Did a desire emerge to obtain something or eliminate an experience?

Did you start constructing a story about what was happening?

Did that story change your emotional state?

Did you respond in a way that might encourage the dynamic to repeat?

You don't need to identify all twelve links.

In fact, trying to do so would probably be rather artificial.

It's enough to investigate some relationships.

And then you can ask yourself:

At which point could I introduce a different condition?

Perhaps you could recognise the unpleasant feeling sooner.

Perhaps you could stop feeding a thought.

Perhaps you need to rest.

Or perhaps you could respond with kindness to someone with whom you would normally come into conflict.

We're not trying to control absolutely everything that appears in our minds.

We're trying to better understand the processes that help sustain particular experiences.

And to discover where possibilities for change exist.

## A wheel that can stop turning

Dependent origination offers us an extraordinarily interesting way of looking at suffering.

Not as punishment.

Not as an essential defect in our nature.

Nor as something that must necessarily accompany us in exactly the same way forever.

But as a phenomenon that arises depending on causes and conditions.

And if those conditions change, the dynamics that depend on them can change too.

From the perspective of our model, we can explore how particular habits, interpretations, desires and reactions help sustain suffering.

But we can also investigate how attention, ethics, concentration and wisdom introduce conditions that transform these processes.

Perhaps liberation doesn't consist of finding a perfect controller who can dominate our minds.

Perhaps it consists of understanding certain dynamics so deeply that they stop receiving the nourishment they need to continue.

We don't yet know how far that transformation can go.

And we don't need to pretend we do.

But there is something we can begin to investigate.

The next time an unpleasant reaction arises, we can try to recognise its conditions.

The next time we catch ourselves feeding an old story, we can observe what happens if we stop.

And the next time we have an opportunity to respond with greater wisdom and love, we can explore what changes.

Because perhaps the most important aspect of dependent origination isn't simply understanding how the wheel begins to turn.

It's discovering that we don't have to keep pushing it.
