# 4. Causes, Conditions, and Consequences

Imagine waking up one morning in a bad mood.

Nothing extraordinary has happened.

Nobody has insulted you.

You haven't received any bad news.

And yet you have the feeling that the whole world has conspired to make your life miserable.

Noise bothers you.

Finding a cup out of place irritates you.

And when someone asks what's wrong, you answer:

“Nothing.”

In that very particular tone of voice that means exactly the opposite.

Now imagine we investigate a little.

Perhaps you slept badly.

Perhaps you've been worried about work for several days.

Yesterday you argued with someone.

And, to top it all off, you haven't had breakfast yet.

Where did your bad mood come from?

Perhaps there isn't a single answer.

And that is precisely what we're going to investigate.

**Our experience does not arise out of nowhere. It depends on conditions.**

But understanding this may turn out to be far more interesting than it sounds.

## A single cause is rarely enough

We're used to thinking in simple relationships.

One thing happens.

Then another thing happens.

And we conclude that the first caused the second.

Sometimes that explanation works well enough.

Drop a stone and it falls.

Heat water under the right conditions and it boils.

But when we try to understand human experience, these relationships tend to get more complicated.

Imagine someone makes an unpleasant remark.

One person gets angry immediately.

Another feels sad.

And a third barely pays any attention.

What happened?

The remark was the same.

But the responses were different.

Perhaps they had different expectations.

Different personal histories.

Different emotional states.

And different ways of interpreting what happened.

So we cannot fully explain those responses by looking only at the remark.

We also need to consider each person's conditions.

And here we encounter a fundamental idea.

**The same situation can produce different responses because it never occurs under exactly the same conditions.**

This doesn't mean that everything is unpredictable.

Nor does it mean that learning to understand our reactions is impossible.

It means we need to broaden our perspective.

Instead of always looking for a single cause, we can investigate sets of conditions.

And observe how they interact.

## The difference between a cause and a condition

In everyday life, we use these words rather loosely.

But for our model, it's useful to distinguish between them.

We can call a cause a factor that contributes to producing a particular outcome.

And a condition a circumstance that allows, facilitates, hinders, or modifies its occurrence.

This is a practical distinction, not a universal philosophical classification.

Imagine you want to light a campfire.

You need fuel.

Oxygen.

An appropriate temperature.

And certain circumstances that allow combustion to continue.

We could point to the spark as the thing that starts the fire.

But without fuel, there probably wouldn't be much of a fire.

And without oxygen, there wouldn't be one either.

So even when we can identify a triggering event, the outcome depends on multiple conditions.

Now let's return to our experience.

Someone criticises you.

Anger arises.

Perhaps the criticism acted as a trigger.

But what other conditions were present?

Maybe you were tired.

Perhaps you thought the criticism was unfair.

Or perhaps you'd been trying for a long time to prove your competence.

When someone questioned your work, you didn't merely interpret their words.

You may also have experienced a threat to your identity.

And that interpretation contributed to your reaction.

If we investigate the conditions, perhaps we'll discover possibilities we hadn't recognised before.

We cannot guarantee that nobody will ever criticise us again.

But perhaps we can transform our relationship with criticism.

## The mind as a network of relationships

In the previous chapter, we proposed looking at the mind as a conditioned system.

Now we can develop that picture.

Imagine a network.

Each process can influence other processes.

A bodily sensation encourages an emotion to arise.

That emotion influences our attention.

Attention selects particular thoughts.

And those thoughts can intensify the original emotion.

We're not necessarily talking about a linear sequence.

Several relationships may be operating simultaneously.

And some may reinforce one another.

For example:

Someone interprets another person's behaviour as rejection.

That interpretation gives rise to sadness.

Sadness makes it easier to recall other experiences of rejection.

Those memories seem to confirm the original interpretation.

And the conviction that they are being rejected grows stronger.

We have created a possible feedback loop.

But notice something important.

We don't need to imagine an independent controller who consciously decided to organise this entire dynamic.

We can investigate how different processes arise and influence one another.

From the perspective of our model, this is particularly interesting.

Because perhaps some of our suffering doesn't depend solely on an unpleasant experience.

It may also depend on the processes that keep feeding it.

And perhaps changing one of those processes can change how the whole system behaves.

## Conditions change too

There is an important consequence to this perspective.

If our experience depends on conditions, it can change when those conditions change.

That sounds obvious.

But perhaps we don't always live as though it were.

Sometimes we interpret our habits as permanent characteristics.

“I'm impatient.”

“I'm a nervous person.”

“I always react like this.”

Certain patterns may have repeated themselves for many years.

And they may be difficult to transform.

But repetition doesn't prove that they're unchangeable.

We can investigate what encourages them.

And what happens when we change some of their conditions.

Imagine you regularly argue with someone every day when you get home.

Perhaps you think the problem is simply your personalities.

But then you discover that the arguments happen especially when you're both tired and need to deal with household matters immediately.

What if we changed some of those conditions?

Perhaps we could rest for a few minutes.

Share certain responsibilities more fairly.

Or choose another time to discuss difficult matters.

That doesn't mean the problem will automatically disappear.

But perhaps the likelihood of conflict will change.

And here we find an important distinction.

**Transforming a response doesn't always require acting directly on that response. Sometimes we can change the conditions that make it more likely to arise.**

## The past influences us, but doesn't decide everything

Our habits develop through repeated experiences.

We learn ways of responding.

Of interpreting.

And of relating to others.

Some of these ways serve us well.

Others may have been useful in certain circumstances but later started causing difficulties.

Imagine that, as a child, you learned to avoid conflict because expressing disagreement could have unpleasant consequences.

Perhaps you developed a remarkable ability to recognise other people's emotional states.

But you may also have learned to hide your own needs.

Years later, that pattern may still appear.

Even though your circumstances have changed.

Not because some personal essence forces you to behave that way.

But because certain kinds of learning can persist for a long time.

Within our model, we can investigate these tendencies without turning them into identities.

But we also need to be careful.

Understanding that a behaviour has a history doesn't mean we can change it simply by explaining it.

Some patterns run deep.

They may be linked to traumatic experiences, medical conditions, or complex social circumstances.

And they may require specialised help.

We shouldn't turn the idea of conditioning into a promise of instant transformation.

But we can recognise a possibility.

Learning continues.

And perhaps some of the conditions present today will allow us to develop responses different from those we learned in the past.

## And what about our decisions?

Here comes a question you've probably been waiting to ask since the previous chapter.

If everything depends on causes and conditions, what happens to our decisions?

Do we actually decide anything?

Or are we merely watching processes unfold over which we have no influence whatsoever?

I'm not going to try to settle every philosophical problem concerning free will here.

But I think we can investigate something closer to home.

When we make a decision, what happens?

We can recognise desires.

Expectations.

Memories.

Evaluations.

And different possible courses of action.

Perhaps an intention arises.

Then we act.

But where did that intention come from?

It may depend on what we've learned.

On what we want to achieve.

On our habits.

And on our present circumstances.

Within our model, we don't need to imagine an independent entity that produces decisions from outside the system.

We can view decisions themselves as conditioned processes.

But this has an important consequence.

**The fact that our decisions are conditioned doesn't mean our actions have no consequences, or that we cannot learn to decide differently.**

Deliberation can also change our responses.

So can learning.

And developing certain values can influence future decisions.

We don't need to stand outside causality to take part in it.

## Consequences become conditions too

Imagine you react with hostility during an argument.

The other person responds defensively.

Afterwards, you both remember the encounter as an unpleasant experience.

And perhaps the next time you meet, the expectation of conflict is stronger.

What happened?

Our action produced consequences.

But those consequences can also become conditions for future experiences.

Here we find an extraordinarily important dynamic.

We don't merely react to a world that exists independently of our actions.

We also help change some of the conditions we'll encounter later.

If we treat someone with respect, perhaps we help build a relationship of trust.

If we continually respond with hostility, we may contribute to a relationship of mistrust.

Naturally, the outcomes don't depend on us alone.

Other people have their own conditions too.

But our actions play a part in shaping what happens.

And this idea will be fundamental when we investigate karma.

Because perhaps we can understand some of its practical implications through a very simple relationship.

Our actions have consequences.

And those consequences can influence our future actions.

We don't need to turn that observation into a complete explanation of the traditional doctrine of karma.

But we can recognise its importance.

**What we do now can help create the conditions for what happens next.**

## The freedom to change conditions

I'd like to suggest a possibility.

Perhaps our freedom doesn't consist in being able to decide absolutely anything, regardless of our circumstances.

Perhaps it consists, at least in part, in developing abilities that expand the range of responses available to us.

Imagine someone who automatically responds with anger to certain criticisms.

At first, they may not even recognise the reaction until after they've acted.

But through learning, they begin to identify some of the signs.

Bodily tension.

Defensive thoughts.

An unpleasant feeling.

Over time, perhaps they recognise the pattern sooner.

And can change their response.

Sometimes they'll manage it.

Other times they won't.

But perhaps their possibilities have changed.

Not because they've discovered a controller located outside the system.

But because the system itself has developed new abilities.

This fits with our three forms of training.

Love can change our intentions and the way we relate to others.

Concentration can foster stability and clarity.

And investigation can help us recognise processes we previously overlooked.

Within our model, all three forms of training play a part in transforming conditions.

We don't need to imagine an independent self magically intervening in the mind.

We can understand learning as part of how the mind works.

And perhaps that will allow us to abandon an unnecessary struggle.

The idea that we must control absolutely everything that happens inside us.

## Causes and responsibility

There's one misunderstanding I particularly want to avoid.

If we understand that a person acted under certain conditions, it may become easier to understand their behaviour.

But understanding is not the same as justifying.

Imagine someone harms another person.

We can investigate which circumstances contributed to that action.

Their history.

Their emotions.

Their habits.

Or the social conditions in which they live.

But none of those explanations erases the harm.

Or the need to protect the person who suffered it.

Nor does it remove the practical responsibility of the person who acted.

We can recognise the causes and demand change.

Set boundaries.

Make amends.

And change the conditions that encourage harmful behaviour.

In fact, understanding causes may help us prevent certain situations more effectively.

But it would be a mistake to use our model to make responsibility seem irrelevant.

Ethics is also part of the system of conditions.

And our responses to harm can help prevent it from happening again.

**Understanding causes doesn't replace responsibility. It can help us exercise it more wisely.**

## Dependent origination

Now we can introduce a fundamental concept of the Dharma.

*Paṭiccasamuppāda*.

Usually translated as dependent origination.

A classical formulation of this principle says, approximately, that when this exists, that arises; when this ceases, that ceases.

Here we find a way of looking at things that is deeply related to what we've been investigating.

Phenomena depend on conditions.

And certain experiences arise when those conditions are present.

But we need to be careful.

The Buddhist doctrine of dependent origination isn't simply a general claim that everything has causes.

It includes specific explanations of the arising of suffering and the continuation of *saṃsāra*.

In the chapter devoted to it, we'll investigate the traditional twelve links in more detail.

For now, I want to highlight one consequence.

If suffering depends on conditions, perhaps we can investigate what happens when those conditions change.

And perhaps certain forms of suffering can cease when the processes that sustain them stop operating.

That is an extraordinarily important possibility.

But we must also recognise that our model is an interpretation.

Not a complete translation of all the traditional teachings on dependent origination.

And certainly not definitive scientific proof.

## A small experiment: investigating a reaction

I'd like to suggest something simple.

Over the next few days, choose a reaction that comes up fairly often.

Not an especially painful situation.

Perhaps a little impatience.

A worry.

Or an irritated response.

When it happens, try to recognise five elements.

**First: the context.**

What was happening?

Where were you?

How were you feeling?

**Second: the experience.**

What sensations and emotions arose?

**Third: the interpretation.**

What meaning did you give to what happened?

**Fourth: the response.**

What did you do?

Or what did you feel the urge to do?

**Fifth: the consequences.**

What happened afterwards?

How did your response affect you and other people?

We don't need to find a perfect explanation.

Nor do we need to analyse absolutely every thought we have.

We're simply trying to recognise a few relationships.

Then you can ask yourself:

What small condition could I change?

Perhaps get more rest.

Prepare for a conversation.

Step away from a situation.

Or develop a different response through practice.

Observe what happens.

And remember something.

We're not trying to prove our model right.

We're investigating whether it helps us understand our experience better.

## Understanding in order to transform

We've explored causes, conditions, and consequences.

We've recognised that the same situation can produce different responses.

That our emotions and interpretations can participate in feedback loops.

And that the consequences of our actions can become new conditions.

We've also considered that our decisions themselves can be understood as conditioned processes.

Without that removing responsibility.

Or the possibility of learning.

Perhaps the most important lesson is quite simple.

**We cannot always choose what appears in our experience, but we can learn to transform some of the conditions that influence what happens next.**

And perhaps that's a useful way to look at our path.

Not as a battle against our mind.

But as a process of learning how it works.

We can recognise tendencies.

Understand their history.

And develop different conditions.

Sometimes the changes will be small.

Other times we may discover profound transformations.

But we don't need to guarantee every outcome before we begin.

We can investigate.

Learn.

And carry on.

In the next chapter, we'll explore one of the most fascinating constructions of our experience.

A story that has accompanied us for so long that perhaps we've forgotten it, too, depends on conditions.

Our identity.

The self.

And that strange feeling that there must be someone inside us who's in charge of absolutely everything.

I suspect a few surprises are in store.
