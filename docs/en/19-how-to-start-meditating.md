# 19. How to Start Meditating

Let's meditate.

At last.

After eighteen chapters discussing the mind, suffering, karma, emptiness, and nirvana, the time has come to do something extraordinarily complicated.

Sit down for five minutes.

And breathe.

Sounds easy, doesn't it?

After all, you've been breathing your whole life. You've probably become quite experienced at it.

Well, I have news for you.

You may discover that paying attention to your breathing for five minutes is considerably harder than breathing for five minutes.

Welcome to meditation.

## What does it mean to meditate?

When we speak of meditation, many people imagine somebody sitting cross-legged, eyes closed, wearing an expression of absolute peace.

Perhaps on a mountaintop.

Preferably with a sunset in the background.

But meditation requires none of those things.

In fact, the word *meditation* is used for quite different practices.

Some develop concentration.

Others cultivate loving-kindness and compassion.

Others investigate experience.

And still others use visualizations, recitations, or particular objects of attention.

In this book, we're going to start with something very simple.

Meditating will mean deliberately spending some time training certain capacities of our minds.

We don't need extraordinary experiences.

We don't need to empty our minds.

And we don't need to adopt any religious beliefs.

We're going to practice.

And observe what happens.

## Before we begin: a small warning

There is something I want to make clear.

Meditation can be valuable, but it is not a universal solution.

Not everyone needs to practice in the same way.

And not every technique is suitable in every circumstance.

Some people may experience anxiety, unpleasant memories, depersonalization, or other difficulties while practicing.

Long sessions, intensive retreats, and certain contemplative techniques can be particularly demanding.

So we will begin gradually.

Without forcing anything.

Without chasing extraordinary experiences.

And remembering that if a practice produces worrying effects, we can stop and seek professional guidance.

Especially if there is a history of psychological destabilization, it is wise to consult a mental-health professional before undertaking intensive practice.

Meditation shouldn't become a competition to see who can endure sitting still the longest.

Or a way to ignore our needs.

Remember our oxygen mask.

It applies here too.

## The first challenge: finding five minutes

When I first became interested in meditation, I discovered an extraordinary amount of information.

Books.

Methods.

Traditions.

Postures.

Breathing techniques.

And people who could explain with absolute certainty the one and only correct way to meditate.

But I consider something else far more important than choosing the perfect method.

Starting.

And you don't need an hour a day to start.

Not even half an hour.

Let's try five minutes.

Five minutes a day.

If that seems too much, start with two.

The important thing is to find an amount of time you can devote to practice sustainably.

From the perspective of our model, this makes sense.

We're trying to develop a habit.

And a habit needs conditions that encourage repetition.

Perhaps you could meditate after getting up.

Before bed, provided you're not too tired.

Or after your morning coffee.

Choose a time that suits you.

And, if possible, stick to it.

Not because there is a magical hour for meditation.

But because regularity can help establish the habit.

## Posture: you don't need to become a pretzel

Let's talk about posture.

Traditional pictures often show meditators in the lotus position.

Legs crossed.

Back upright.

An expression of absolute serenity.

And knees that look as though they were designed by an entirely different company from the one that made mine.

You don't have to sit like that.

You can use a chair.

In fact, for many people it is an excellent choice.

Sit comfortably and steadily.

Place your feet on the floor.

Let your back remain reasonably upright, without becoming rigid.

Relax your shoulders.

And rest your hands wherever feels natural.

You can close your eyes.

Or leave them open and gently rest your gaze on a point in front of you.

There is no perfect posture for everyone.

What matters is finding a position in which you can remain for a few minutes without unnecessary discomfort.

You don't have to stay completely motionless, either.

If you feel pain, adjust your posture.

If you need to move, move.

We're not training our ability to suffer in silence.

Life already gives us enough opportunities for that.

## First exercise: discovering where our attention is

Let's begin.

Sit comfortably.

For a few moments, acknowledge how you feel.

Perhaps you're calm.

Perhaps you're tired.

Perhaps you have a lot on your mind.

You don't need to change anything.

Now gently direct your attention to your breathing.

You can notice the sensations of air moving in and out through your nose.

Or the movement of your abdomen.

Choose whichever is easier for you.

You don't need to breathe in any special way.

Just let breathing happen.

And observe it.

Perhaps you notice the air coming in.

Going out.

The sensation changing.

For a few seconds, your attention may stay there.

But something else will probably appear.

A sound.

A thought.

A bodily sensation.

A memory.

Perhaps a question:

“Am I doing this right?”

That's fine.

When you notice that your attention has wandered, simply return to the breath.

And continue.

That's all.

Seriously.

There's no hidden trick.

You don't have to produce a special experience.

During these first few minutes, we're training two very simple capacities.

Directing attention.

And recognizing when it has moved elsewhere.

## The most important moment

I want to dwell on something I learned especially well from Culadasa and *The Mind Illuminated*.

Imagine you're observing the breath.

Suddenly a thought appears.

Then another.

And before you know it, you've spent two minutes imagining tomorrow's conversation with your boss.

Your first reaction might be:

“I've failed.”

“I can't concentrate.”

“I'm terrible at meditation.”

But let's look at what actually happened.

First, your attention wandered.

Then you recognized that you were distracted.

And now you can return.

That second moment is extraordinarily important.

Noticing that you've been distracted isn't failure. It's one of the capacities we're training.

That's why Culadasa suggests appreciating that moment of recognition.

Instead of getting angry, you can feel a little satisfaction.

“Ah, I noticed.”

And return.

This attitude can make practice more pleasant and sustainable.

From the perspective of our model, we're trying to encourage particular conditions.

If every distraction brings frustration, we may end up associating meditation with an unpleasant experience.

But if we learn to greet that moment of clarity with kindness, we can develop a different relationship with training.

And here we find a connection with our first principle.

Love.

Even while training concentration, we can learn to treat ourselves kindly.

## You don't have to stop thinking

There is a very common misconception about meditation.

The idea that we must empty our minds.

Many people start practicing and discover they can't eliminate their thoughts.

They then conclude that meditation isn't for them.

But perhaps the problem is the expectation.

In our initial practice, we're not trying to prevent thoughts from appearing.

We're learning to recognize them.

And to choose, when possible, where we direct our attention.

We can think during meditation.

We can get distracted.

We can remember things.

We might even discover that our minds seem especially busy when we try to calm them.

That doesn't mean we're meditating badly.

It means we're starting to notice processes that usually go unnoticed.

In fact, you may find that your mind produces an extraordinary number of thoughts.

Some useful.

Some interesting.

And some that seem to have been written by a creative department with absolutely no supervision.

You don't need to fight them.

You can recognize them.

And return.

## What if I get bored?

It's quite possible that boredom will appear during your first sessions.

A lot of it.

You may look at the clock.

You may wonder how much time has passed.

You may discover that five minutes can feel extraordinarily long when we're not being entertained.

This is interesting too.

Our minds are used to receiving stimulation constantly.

Conversations.

Screens.

Music.

Thoughts.

Plans.

Memories.

And when we deliberately stop feeding that activity, new sensations can appear.

Boredom.

Restlessness.

Impatience.

Our model invites us to investigate how we respond to them.

Perhaps the urge to do something else appears.

To get up.

To check our phone.

Or to abandon the practice.

We don't need to treat these reactions as enemies.

We can recognize them.

And if the discomfort is mild and manageable, perhaps remain for a few moments observing them.

But we don't need to turn five minutes into an endurance test, either.

We're learning.

Not punishing ourselves.

## What if emotions arise?

Sometimes, when we stop filling our attention with external stimuli, we discover emotions we hadn't clearly recognized before.

Perhaps sadness.

Worry.

Irritation.

Or a feeling that's hard to describe.

We don't need to interpret them immediately.

Much less find a profound explanation for every one of them.

We can recognize that they're present.

If they're mild, perhaps we can continue calmly with the breath.

If they intensify or become difficult to manage, we can open our eyes, look around, move, or end the session.

That's not a failure.

It's a way of looking after ourselves.

We don't need to force intense emotional investigation, especially while we're still learning to develop stability.

In fact, this is one reason I suggest beginning with simple concentration practice before going too deeply into the investigation of experience.

Not because there is a compulsory sequence.

But because stability can provide favorable conditions for investigating more carefully.

## The second training: cultivating a kind attitude

Let's now introduce a different exercise.

You don't have to do it in every session.

But I think it's worth learning from the beginning.

It's inspired by the Buddhist practice of *mettā*, loving-kindness.

Sit comfortably.

For a few moments, think of someone you love.

Perhaps a family member.

A friend.

Or even a pet.

Someone toward whom affection comes relatively easily.

Recognize that this person, like you, wants to be happy.

That they have difficulties.

That they want to feel safe.

And that they're probably trying to live as well as they know how.

You can now silently offer a simple intention:

“May you be well.”

“May you live in peace.”

“May you find well-being.”

You don't have to repeat the words mechanically.

Nor do you need to manufacture an extraordinary emotion.

Simply try to cultivate a sincere wish for their well-being.

Then you can try directing the same attitude toward yourself.

“May I be well.”

“May I live in peace.”

“May I learn to treat myself kindly.”

Perhaps it feels natural.

Or perhaps you discover that wishing yourself well is surprisingly difficult.

Either way, you don't need to force it.

We're exploring a capacity.

And, like any other capacity, it can develop through practice.

Remember what we learned from Erich Fromm.

Loving is an art too.

And we can train it.

## The third training: observing our experience

Once you're reasonably comfortable spending a few minutes observing the breath, you can introduce a small investigation exercise.

You don't need to wait until your concentration is perfect.

In fact, I suspect we'd be waiting quite a while.

Let's do something simple.

Sit for a few minutes and begin by observing your breathing.

Then gently broaden your attention.

Notice what's happening.

Perhaps a sound appears.

A bodily sensation.

A thought.

Or an emotion.

Try to notice its feeling tone.

Is it pleasant?

Unpleasant?

Neutral?

Now observe whether a response arises.

Do you want it to continue?

Do you want it to disappear?

Are you starting to build a story about what it means?

You don't need to analyze it too much.

Simply recognize a few relationships.

And if you become distracted or lose clarity, you can return to the breath.

This is a very simple introduction to contemplative investigation.

It isn't intended to replace complete vipassanā methods or guarantee profound insights.

But it can help you start recognizing some of the processes we've explained through our model.

## One month to begin

Now I'd like to suggest something practical.

For thirty days, devote a few minutes to exploring meditation.

You don't have to complete a perfect program.

There will be no exams.

No diplomas.

And, sadly, no official certificate of enlightenment either.

Let's use a simple progression.

**Week one: establish the habit.** Spend two to five minutes each day observing the breath.

The goal isn't perfect concentration.

It's learning to sit down, notice distractions, and return.

If you like, take a few seconds at the end of each session to appreciate that you've practiced.

**Week two: develop stability.** If practice feels comfortable, you can gradually increase to five or ten minutes.

Continue observing the breath.

Begin noticing when distractions, boredom, or restlessness arise.

You don't need to eliminate them.

Just learn to recognize them and return.

**Week three: bring in love.** Maintain your concentration practice.

And devote a minute or two to cultivating *mettā*.

You can begin with yourself or someone you love.

Don't try to manufacture an emotion.

Simply develop an intention of well-being.

**Week four: begin investigating.** If you feel comfortable, devote a few minutes to observing the sensations, thoughts, and emotions that arise.

Try to recognize when an experience feels pleasant or unpleasant.

And observe what responses follow.

Don't look for special experiences.

You don't need to confirm any theory.

Just investigate.

This program is a flexible proposal, not a universal recipe.

If five minutes are enough for you, you can stay with five minutes.

If you need a day off, rest.

And if you discover that practice isn't agreeing with you, you can adapt it or stop.

Our goal isn't to tick off thirty boxes on a calendar.

It's to discover whether we can develop a different relationship with our experience.

## How can I tell whether I'm making progress?

This is an interesting question.

And a slightly dangerous one.

Because our minds have an extraordinary ability to turn any activity into a competition.

At first you might measure how many minutes you can remain attentive.

Then you may start comparing sessions.

“I concentrated better yesterday.”

“I had too many thoughts today.”

“I was calmer last week.”

And, without noticing, you may end up building a new source of frustration.

But not all evaluation is harmful.

We can investigate whether our practice is developing useful capacities.

Perhaps you notice distractions sooner.

Perhaps you find it easier to return to the breath.

Perhaps you can stay with an unpleasant emotion for a few seconds without immediately reacting.

Or perhaps you discover that you're listening to people more attentively.

You don't need all those changes to happen.

And we shouldn't automatically attribute every improvement to meditation.

Life is complex.

Many conditions influence our experience.

But we can stay reasonably curious about what we're learning.

And, above all, observe whether our practice is helping us live with greater clarity and care.

Because if you can concentrate for an hour but then treat everybody terribly, perhaps we ought to review what we're training—and what else we need to cultivate.

## Meditation doesn't end when you stand up

There is something I want to emphasize.

Our practice doesn't have to be limited to the minutes we spend sitting.

We can draw on some of the capacities we develop throughout the rest of the day.

For example, while walking.

You can notice the sensations in your feet.

The movement of your body.

Sounds.

And how your attention shifts.

You can also practice while listening to someone.

Recognize when you're distracted.

And return to the conversation.

Or when a little irritation appears.

Perhaps you can notice bodily tension.

The unpleasant feeling tone.

And the thoughts beginning to accompany it.

You don't need to turn every moment of your life into a meditation session.

In fact, I'd rather you didn't.

We also need to enjoy ourselves.

Laugh.

Talk.

Play.

And do things without continually analyzing our experience.

But perhaps some of the capacities we train will begin to accompany us spontaneously in everyday life.

And that's exactly what we're aiming for.

Not to become experts at sitting with our eyes closed.

But to learn to live more consciously and carefully.

## Don't believe everything you experience

I want to end with a particularly important warning.

Meditation can produce surprising experiences.

Intense bodily sensations.

Changes in perception.

Moments of profound well-being.

Changes in our usual sense of identity.

Or experiences we can't explain.

Some may feel pleasant.

Others may be bewildering.

And some may be distressing.

We don't need to interpret these experiences immediately as signs of spiritual progress.

Nor should we assume they reveal extraordinary truths about the nature of reality.

Remember something we've repeated throughout this book.

An experience and our explanation of that experience are not the same thing.

If you discover something interesting, you can observe it.

Investigate its conditions.

Compare it with other experiences.

And keep its interpretation open.

Especially with extraordinary phenomena, it is wise to resist the temptation to turn them into certainties immediately.

Meditation doesn't make us immune to errors of interpretation.

Sometimes it can even give us very convincing experiences that call for particular caution.

We don't need to deny their value.

But neither do we need to believe everything our minds tell us about them.

## All you have to do is begin

We've reached the end of our first practical introduction to meditation.

And I want to remind you of something.

You don't need to become a different person before you begin.

You don't need a calm mind.

You don't need to know every Buddhist concept.

Or adopt any belief about nirvana.

You can begin with five minutes.

Sit.

Observe the breath.

Notice a distraction.

And return.

Then you can explore other capacities.

Cultivate love.

Develop stability.

Investigate your experience.

And discover what happens as these practices become part of your life.

Perhaps you'll find calm.

Perhaps you'll discover habits you hadn't noticed before.

Or perhaps you'll simply realize that your mind is considerably more restless than you imagined.

It doesn't matter.

We're not chasing a predetermined outcome.

We're learning to explore.

And if, after finishing this chapter, you decide to spend five minutes observing your breath, perhaps we've already achieved something important.

Not because you've taken the first step toward guaranteed enlightenment.

But because you've decided to investigate for yourself.

You don't have to believe meditation works. You can try it, observe what happens, and draw your own conclusions.

And now, if you don't mind, put the book down for a few minutes.

I'll see you when you return.
