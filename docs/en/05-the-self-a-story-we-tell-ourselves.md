# 5. The Self: A Story We Tell Ourselves

I'd like to suggest an experiment.

Close your eyes for a few seconds.

And ask yourself:

“Who am I?”

Perhaps your name comes to mind.

Your profession.

Your story.

Your memories.

The people you love.

And certain qualities you consider particularly important.

Perhaps you think of yourself as an intelligent person.

Kind.

Impatient.

Funny.

Or exceptionally good at finding bargains in the supermarket.

But now let me ask you something else.

Where, exactly, is this self?

In your thoughts?

In your body?

In your memories?

In your intentions?

Or is there something else—some sort of observer experiencing all of this?

You don't need an immediate answer.

In fact, this might be one of the most interesting questions we can investigate.

**What if the self were not an independent entity, but a construction arising through different processes in our experience?**

Let's explore that.

## The self we use every day

Before going any further, I want to clarify something.

I'm not going to try to convince you that you don't exist.

That would be quite a difficult task.

Especially because, if you didn't exist, I'd have to wonder who's reading this book.

And who's supposed to pay for it.

Our personal identity has an obvious purpose.

It allows us to recognise our history.

Maintain relationships.

Take responsibility.

And find our way in the world.

We use names.

We remember events.

We make plans.

And we acknowledge that certain actions have consequences for us and for other people.

We don't need to give all of that up.

The problem arises when we start imagining that behind these processes there must necessarily be a permanent, independent entity, separate from the conditions that make it possible.

A self that controls.

That decides.

That owns our thoughts.

And that remains essentially unchanged while everything else changes.

But do we really need that explanation?

From the perspective of our model, perhaps we can examine identity differently.

Not as a substance.

But as a collection of processes that produce a degree of continuity.

## The story we tell about ourselves

Imagine someone asks you what you're like.

You'll probably answer by drawing on experiences from the past.

Perhaps you'll say where you were born.

What you studied.

What you like.

And what difficulties you've been through.

You might talk about your achievements.

Your mistakes.

And the people who have influenced your life.

We're constructing a narrative.

A story that connects events and gives them meaning.

That story can be extraordinarily useful.

It helps us understand our personal continuity.

Explain our preferences.

And plan for the future.

But we should remember something.

A narrative is not a complete reproduction of everything that happened.

We select memories.

We interpret experiences.

And we establish connections between events.

What's more, our memories can change.

So can the way we understand the past.

Something we considered a failure for years may later become an important lesson.

An experience we thought was unfair may take on a different meaning when we learn about new circumstances.

Our personal story does not remain completely still.

It is continually being reinterpreted.

And perhaps that helps us understand something fundamental.

**Narrative identity is useful, but it doesn't have to be a definitive description of who we are.**

## The self changes with circumstances too

Here's something curious.

We don't always behave like the same person.

Perhaps you're calm at work.

But impatient behind the wheel.

Or very confident with your friends, yet extraordinarily shy around strangers.

We can display different abilities depending on our circumstances.

And some characteristics we consider part of our identity may emerge only in certain contexts.

From the perspective of our model, that makes a good deal of sense.

Mental processes depend on conditions.

And different situations can encourage different responses.

But now an interesting problem arises.

If our personality changes with circumstances, what exactly do we mean when we say we are a certain kind of person?

Perhaps we're describing tendencies.

Relatively stable patterns.

But not absolute properties.

Saying “I'm impatient” can be a useful way of recognising a habit.

But it can also become a sentence.

An explanation we use to justify continuing to behave in the same way.

“That's just how I am.”

Perhaps.

But what does that actually mean?

That you've reacted that way for a long time?

Or that some personal essence forces you to keep doing so?

The distinction matters.

Because a tendency may be difficult to change.

But we don't need to turn it into something immutable.

## The self as controller

We now arrive at one of the central questions in our model.

Our feeling that we control experience.

When we perform an action, we usually feel that we've decided to do it.

And that experience can be extraordinarily convincing.

But what happens if we observe carefully how our intentions arise?

Imagine you're sitting down.

And you decide to raise a hand.

It seems simple.

There's an intention.

Then the movement happens.

But try observing closely the moment that intention arises.

Did you first decide to decide to raise your hand?

And if so, what produced that earlier decision?

We could keep asking the same question indefinitely.

Our model offers another possibility.

We can regard intentions as processes that arise depending on conditions.

We need not imagine an independent controller producing each decision from outside the system.

This doesn't prove that we've definitively solved the philosophical problem of free will.

But it does challenge a familiar intuition.

The need for a separate self organising all our mental processes.

Perhaps the feeling of being in control is itself a phenomenon of experience.

And perhaps it depends on particular processes of interpretation and attribution.

That doesn't mean our actions take place without organisation.

Or that we cannot deliberate.

We're simply asking whether we need to imagine an independent controller to explain that organisation.

## Who observes our thoughts?

There's another particularly interesting experience.

We can observe our thoughts.

Perhaps you're meditating.

A worry appears.

You notice it.

And then you return to the breath.

It's easy to interpret this by saying there's an observer watching the thoughts.

A deeper self.

Located behind mental activity.

But perhaps we should examine that conclusion.

What have we actually observed?

A thought appeared.

Then recognition of that thought arose.

And perhaps an intention to direct attention back again.

From our model's perspective, we can view these events as different mental processes.

We don't have to assume that an independent entity is observing them from outside.

But we should also be cautious.

Introspection doesn't give us direct access to every mechanism of consciousness.

And failing to find an independent observer does not, by itself, prove that we've definitively settled the nature of subjectivity.

We're using a model to interpret experience.

Not presenting an absolute metaphysical proof.

Even so, I think the question is extraordinarily important.

**What if awareness of our thoughts didn't require a second self hidden behind the first?**

Otherwise we might end up constructing a considerable number of observers.

One that thinks.

Another that observes the thinker.

And perhaps a third whose job is to supervise the second.

A mental organisation worthy of a particularly bureaucratic corporation.

## The self and our emotions

Our identity also profoundly influences how we relate to emotions.

Imagine anger arises.

We might say:

“I'm angry.”

That's a perfectly reasonable description.

But we might also conclude:

“I'm an angry person.”

Or:

“If I feel this emotion, it means I'm a bad person.”

Here we've moved from recognising an experience to making a claim about our identity.

And that claim can have consequences.

Perhaps shame arises.

Guilt.

Or an intense need to defend our self-image.

From the perspective of our model, identification can help fuel certain dynamics.

An emotion arises.

We interpret it as an essential characteristic of the self.

And that interpretation produces new reactions.

But perhaps there's another possibility.

We can recognise that anger is arising.

Investigate its conditions.

And decide how to respond.

Without immediately turning it into a definition of who we are.

This doesn't remove responsibility for our actions.

If we hurt someone, we must acknowledge it and make amends.

But perhaps we can learn to distinguish behaviour from identity.

**We can take responsibility for what we do without turning every mistake into a personal essence.**

And perhaps that makes learning easier.

Because we're not trying to destroy a defective identity.

We're trying to transform particular conditions.

## The self that wants to be perfect

I'd like to share something personal.

For a long time I've recognised a strong tendency in myself.

The need to do things well.

To understand.

To improve.

And sometimes to try to become an ideal version of myself.

Perhaps this tendency has helped me learn.

Work.

And develop abilities I value.

But it can also cause difficulties.

Because there's always something to improve.

An error to correct.

An ability we haven't yet developed.

And a gap between who we are and who we imagine we ought to be.

If we identify deeply with that ideal image, we can turn our lives into an endless performance review.

It's never enough.

Something is always missing.

And we may end up using our personal development to fuel precisely the suffering we wanted to reduce.

From our model's perspective, this too can be seen as a conditioned dynamic.

An interpretation of who we ought to be.

Constant comparison.

And dissatisfaction whenever our experience fails to match that image.

But perhaps we don't need to abandon our aspiration to improve altogether.

We can learn.

Grow.

And acknowledge our mistakes.

Without making perfection a condition for loving ourselves.

And here we find a direct connection with our first form of training.

Love.

Not as a reward we grant ourselves once we finally behave perfectly.

But as a way of relating to ourselves while we continue to learn.

## We don't need to hate the self

I want to stop here because I think there is a common misunderstanding in certain interpretations of Buddhism.

When we hear about *anattā*, or not-self, we may imagine that we need to eliminate our identity.

Destroy the ego.

And become people who never think about themselves.

But perhaps that's yet another way of creating unnecessary conflict.

Our personal identity serves important functions.

It helps us organise ourselves.

Remember.

Relate to others.

And protect ourselves.

We don't need to despise it.

Or turn it into an enemy.

We can recognise that our self-image is a conditioned construction.

And still appreciate it.

In fact, perhaps we can develop a kinder relationship with it.

We don't need to let all of its interpretations determine our actions.

But neither should we deny that many of these tendencies arose in an attempt to protect us from real difficulties.

We can be grateful for what has helped us.

And change what is no longer useful.

Perhaps our relationship with the self can change profoundly without our having to declare war on it.

**We don't need to destroy our story. We can learn not to get trapped in it.**

## The five aggregates

Now we can connect our investigation with a fundamental teaching of early Buddhism.

The five aggregates.

*Pañcakkhandhā*.

Traditionally, they are used to analyse what we usually regard as a self.

They are:

1. Material form (*rūpa*).
2. Feeling or affective tone (*vedanā*).
3. Perception or recognition (*saññā*).
4. Volitional formations or activities (*saṅkhāra*).
5. Consciousness (*viññāṇa*).

This classification shouldn't be understood simply as a modern list of psychological components.

It belongs to a particular doctrinal framework.

But we can recognise an interesting intention behind it.

To investigate different aspects of experience without assuming that any of them necessarily constitutes an independent, permanent self.

Our body changes.

Our sensations arise and pass away.

Perceptions depend on conditions.

So do thoughts and intentions.

And consciousness, in early Buddhist explanations, is described in relation to particular conditions.

That brings us to the question.

Where do we find a personal essence independent of all those processes?

From the perspective of our model, we don't need to postulate one to investigate how experience works.

But let's be precise.

The teaching of *anattā* goes deeper than simply saying that our thoughts change.

And there are different philosophical interpretations of its scope.

Our model can help us approach it.

But it doesn't replace its full doctrinal complexity.

## Does the self exist or not?

Here comes a question you may be asking yourself.

So, does the self exist?

My answer is that we first need to clarify what we mean by “exist”.

If we're talking about a person with a history, relationships and a place in the world, we can perfectly well use the word “I”.

And it makes sense to do so.

But if we're talking about a permanent, independent entity that can control every mental process from outside causes and conditions, our model doesn't need to assume such an entity exists.

Perhaps this distinction is particularly useful.

Because it allows us to recognise conventional identity without turning it into a metaphysical substance.

We can say:

“I've made a decision.”

“I've made a mistake.”

“I love my children.”

And still understand our intentions and emotions as conditioned processes.

There's no necessary contradiction.

The word “I” can serve as a practical way of referring to a person.

Without our having to attribute to it all the properties our imagination might add.

And perhaps here we find a connection with the two truths we'll explore later.

The usefulness of conventional descriptions.

And the limits of turning them into assertions about independent essences.

## The self and suffering

Now we arrive at an important question.

Why might identifying with the self generate suffering?

Not because using a personal identity is necessarily harmful.

But because certain interpretations can make us particularly vulnerable.

Imagine you need to be regarded as intelligent.

Someone questions one of your ideas.

And you experience that criticism as a personal threat.

Perhaps you're no longer investigating whether the idea is right.

You're trying to defend who you are.

Or imagine you consider yourself an exceptionally good person.

When you make a mistake, you find it hard to acknowledge it.

Because doing so could call your identity into question.

So perhaps you start making excuses.

Denying what happened.

Or shifting responsibility onto others.

In both cases, identification can contribute to new dynamics of suffering.

But perhaps we can respond differently.

We can acknowledge that an idea might be wrong.

Without becoming worthless people as a result.

We can accept that we've made a mistake.

And go on loving ourselves.

And we can discover that some of the stories we've constructed about ourselves are no longer necessary.

From our model's perspective, this could reduce the influence of certain self-referential dynamics.

Not because the self has magically disappeared.

But because perhaps we've learned to relate differently to our interpretations.

## What if we don't need a centre?

There's an image that has stayed with me for a long time.

The search for a centre.

Something that organises our experience.

A fundamental identity.

An observer.

Or an essence that remains when everything else changes.

But perhaps we can question that need.

Imagine a city.

There are thousands of people.

Vehicles.

Buildings.

Services.

And processes that constantly interact.

The city functions through countless relationships.

We don't need to imagine a little director hidden in some building controlling absolutely every event.

Of course, authorities and coordination systems do exist.

But the organisation of the whole depends on innumerable processes.

Perhaps our experience can also be understood through distributed organisation.

That doesn't mean the mind literally works like a city.

It's a metaphor.

But it may help us let go of an intuition.

The idea that every organised system needs an independent controller at its centre.

Perhaps certain forms of organisation can arise through relationships between processes.

And perhaps the feeling of having a centre is itself one of those processes.

We don't need to prove that no functional centre of any kind ever exists.

We can recognise that some functions coordinate others.

But that doesn't necessarily imply an independent essence.

And perhaps that distinction could profoundly change how we understand ourselves.

## A small experiment: observing an intention

I'd like to suggest a simple investigation.

You don't need to reach any extraordinary meditative state.

Just sit for a few minutes.

And observe your experience.

Perhaps a thought appears.

Then a sensation.

Or an intention to move your body.

You don't need to produce anything special.

When you notice an intention, ask yourself:

What happened before?

Was there a sensation?

A desire.

An expectation.

Or a related thought.

Now see whether you can identify exactly how the intention appeared.

Perhaps you'll discover that you notice it only once it's already there.

Or perhaps you'll identify some preceding conditions.

We don't need a definitive conclusion.

Or to prove that free will doesn't exist.

We're simply investigating how certain processes in our experience arise.

And perhaps we'll discover something curious.

Our familiar sense of being in charge doesn't always give us direct access to all the causes of our actions.

That doesn't eliminate our ability to learn.

Or our responsibility.

But perhaps it helps us develop a less rigid interpretation of how we function.

## The self as a tool

I want to end by returning to a distinction.

Our identity can be an extraordinarily useful tool.

It enables us to keep commitments.

Learn from our experiences.

And build relationships.

But perhaps we don't need to make it the absolute centre of our existence.

We can recognise that our personal history has value.

Without assuming it completely defines who we can become.

We can use our abilities.

Without making them a permanent measure of our worth.

And we can acknowledge our difficulties.

Without assuming they constitute an essence we can never change.

From our model's perspective, perhaps the self can be understood as a conditioned construction that performs different functions.

Some useful.

Others perhaps sources of suffering.

We don't have to destroy it.

We can learn to relate to it better.

And perhaps when it no longer permanently occupies the centre of our attention, we'll discover that we have more room to notice other things.

The needs of the people we love.

The conditions we share.

And the opportunities we have to act with compassion.

Because perhaps one of the most interesting ways to develop wisdom is to stop turning our own identity into the fundamental explanation for everything that happens.

## A story we can learn to tell differently

We've investigated personal identity.

Our memories.

Intentions.

The feeling of control.

And our relationship with emotions.

We've also introduced the five aggregates and the Buddhist teaching of not-self.

From our model's perspective, perhaps we can view the self as a dynamic construction arising through conditioned processes.

Not as an independent entity existing outside them.

But we must remember something important.

This is an interpretation.

Not a definitive metaphysical demonstration.

And we don't need to turn it into a new belief we must defend.

We can use it to investigate.

See whether it helps us understand our reactions.

And notice whether it changes our relationship with suffering.

Perhaps we'll discover that certain stories about ourselves are no longer needed.

Or that we can learn to act without constantly defending our image.

And perhaps we'll learn to love ourselves differently, too.

Without needing to be perfect.

Without turning every mistake into a sentence.

And without despising the parts of our history that helped us get here.

Because perhaps the problem was never having a self.

Perhaps it arises when we mistake a useful tool for an essence that must be protected at all costs.

**We don't need to stop being someone in order to learn to live without making ourselves the centre of everything.**

And perhaps that will allow us to develop a freer relationship with our experience.

More open.

More compassionate.

And less caught up in the need to defend who we think we are.

In the next chapter, we're going to do something particularly important.

We're going to question our own model.

Because after using conditioning to explain the mind, emotions, intentions and our identity, perhaps we should ask ourselves something.

How far can we really trust this explanation?

And in particular, what happens when we try to use a conditioned model to understand a system of which it is itself a part?

I suspect we have a few interesting difficulties ahead.
