# 6. The Limits of the Model

We have a model.

The mind is a conditioned system. Experiences arise depending on causes and conditions, reactions influence what happens next, and through practice we can learn to transform some of these processes.

It sounds reasonable.

Even rather elegant.

And that's where the danger begins.

Because one of the most extraordinary abilities of our minds is to find explanations that fit what we already believe.

Once we adopt a way of interpreting the world, we start seeing it everywhere.

If we believe people are selfish, we'll find selfishness.

If we believe everything happens for a reason, we'll find reasons.

And if we believe everything is conditioned, we'll probably find causes and conditions even in the way our neighbour puts out the rubbish bins.

Finding an explanation convincing does not necessarily make it true.

So before we continue building our model, we're going to do something important.

We're going to be a little suspicious of it.

## The map is not the territory

Imagine you have a map of a city.

A good map can help you find your bearings, discover routes and reach places you didn't know.

But the map isn't the city.

It doesn't contain all its buildings, all the people who live there, the smells from its restaurants or the conversations taking place in its streets.

It's a representation that selects certain information and leaves out the rest.

And that's precisely what makes it useful.

A map that tried to represent absolutely everything in a city would be so complicated that it would probably stop being useful for finding our way.

Something similar happens with models.

Our model of the mind selects certain characteristics of experience and proposes relationships between them.

It invites us to observe how our reactions arise, which conditions might influence them, and how our responses help build our habits.

But it doesn't claim to represent every aspect of the human mind.

Nor does it necessarily explain every phenomenon we might experience.

And it certainly doesn't demonstrate how reality as a whole works.

It's a map.

And it would be rather absurd to start arguing with a mountain because it isn't where our map says it should be.

## An explanation is not a demonstration

There's an important difference between something being compatible with our model and our model actually explaining it.

For example, we can say that anger arises depending on causes and conditions.

That's a reasonable interpretation.

We can investigate whether tiredness, memories, expectations or certain habits play a part.

We might even change some of those conditions and observe whether our response changes.

But if we explain absolutely anything that happens by simply saying “it was conditioned”, we haven't learned very much.

We've found an answer that works for everything.

And answers that work for absolutely everything risk explaining nothing in particular.

For our model to be useful, we need to ask more specific questions.

Which conditions seem relevant?

Which relationships can we observe?

What changes might we expect if we altered certain circumstances?

Are there other possible explanations?

We won't always be able to answer these questions. Human experience is too complex and our powers of observation are limited.

But we should remain willing to try.

A good model should help us ask better questions, not become an automatic answer to every mystery.

## Our experience has limits, too

In earlier chapters we've talked about experience as our main field of investigation.

But observing our experience doesn't mean we can directly know all the processes that produce it.

We can feel anger without knowing all its causes.

We can experience an intention without knowing exactly how it arose.

We can notice a thought appearing without gaining access to every process that contributed to producing it.

And, of course, we can be mistaken about how we interpret what we experience.

I think this is particularly important when we talk about meditation.

Throughout my practice I've experienced states of concentration, joy and equanimity, as well as profound changes in my usual perception of the self.

Some of these experiences have transformed how I understand the mind.

But I've also learned something I consider fundamental.

The intensity of an experience doesn't guarantee that our interpretation of it is true.

We can experience something extraordinary and draw mistaken conclusions.

We might experience a profound sense of unity and conclude that we've discovered a universal consciousness.

Or experience a temporary disappearance of our usual sense of identity and conclude that we've proved the self doesn't exist.

Perhaps some of these interpretations are correct.

Perhaps not.

Experience alone doesn't always allow us to decide.

For a while, I myself attached considerable importance to certain interpretations of my non-dual experiences.

Over time I started to consider another possibility: that these experiences, too, were the result of particular conditions.

I didn't need to deny what I'd lived through.

I could simply stop giving it a meaning I wasn't in a position to demonstrate.

I admit that took a bit of the magic out of it.

But it also allowed me to keep exploring without having to defend a conclusion.

And, in a way, I find that much more interesting.

## We're not going to solve all of metaphysics

There are fascinating questions about the nature of reality.

Does free will exist?

What actually is consciousness?

Is there anything that exists independently of all conditions?

What happens after death?

Is there some ultimate reality beyond our ordinary experience?

Human beings have been asking these questions for a very long time.

And I don't intend to solve all of them before you've finished the next chapter.

Among other things, because I wouldn't know how.

Our model might help us reflect on some of them, but we mustn't confuse the implications of an interpretation with proof that the interpretation is true.

For example, considering our decisions to be conditioned doesn't, in itself, prove that free will is impossible. Much depends on what we mean by freedom.

Likewise, recognising the interdependence of phenomena doesn't automatically settle every philosophical question about emptiness.

And experiencing extraordinary meditative states doesn't necessarily demonstrate the existence of transcendent realities.

In this book, we'll try to keep a clear distinction between three things:

- What we can observe in our experience.
- The explanations and models we use to understand it.
- Claims about reality that go beyond what we can establish through those observations.

All three may be interesting.

But they aren't the same thing.

Nor are we going to dismiss scientific knowledge or other people's work just because we can't verify absolutely everything through our own experience.

We don't need to discover bacteria personally in order to wash our hands.

Fortunately.

## Understanding doesn't mean excusing

There's another danger that deserves special attention.

If we understand human behaviour as the result of causes and conditions, we might end up using that explanation as an excuse.

“I couldn't help it.”

“It's my conditioning.”

“I'm not responsible because my reactions are automatic.”

But our model isn't intended to eliminate responsibility.

Quite the opposite.

If our actions contribute to new conditions, then their consequences matter.

A great deal.

We can understand that someone has developed certain behaviours because of difficult circumstances and, at the same time, recognise that those behaviours can cause harm.

We can feel compassion for someone and set boundaries.

We can understand our own mistakes without failing to make amends.

Perhaps our usual concept of responsibility needs some nuance, but that doesn't mean we should abandon it.

In fact, understanding the conditions that encourage a behaviour can help us intervene more effectively.

Compassion shouldn't become indifference.

Nor should understanding become an excuse for inaction.

## The model is conditioned, too

And now we come to one of my favourite parts.

If our mind is a conditioned system, then this model, too, has arisen depending on causes and conditions.

My engineering background, personal experiences, the books I've read, my years of meditation practice and the conversations I've had have all helped shape it.

Among those influences is the Mind-System Model Culadasa presents in *The Mind Illuminated*, which we've already discussed.

And, of course, the conversations with artificial intelligence that have accompanied the writing of this book.

Perhaps someone with different training, a different history and different experiences would have constructed a very different explanation.

Even starting from similar observations.

That doesn't automatically invalidate our model.

But it reminds us that we're not looking at it from some privileged position, completely independent of the thing we're trying to understand.

We're using a conditioned mind to build a model of a conditioned mind.

And then we use that model to interpret the experience of that very same mind.

Wonderful.

At this rate we'll end up needing a model to explain why we need a model.

But there's an important issue behind the joke.

No explanation we construct is necessarily free from the limitations, expectations and conditioning of the people who construct it.

That's why we need to test our ideas against other perspectives, listen to different views and be ready to revise them.

Not because all interpretations are equally valid.

But because acknowledging that we might be wrong is an important condition for continuing to learn.

## What if we're wrong?

Imagine that in a few years we discover that parts of our model don't work as we thought.

Perhaps mental processes are more complex than we've assumed.

Perhaps certain relationships that seemed obvious turn out to be incomplete.

Perhaps we find more precise explanations.

What should we do?

Change the model.

It's that simple.

Well, perhaps it isn't quite that simple emotionally. Our minds are rather fond of being right.

And the more time we spend building an idea, the harder it may become to let it go.

But if our purpose is to understand experience better, there's little point in clinging to an explanation that's stopped being useful.

The model exists to help us.

We're not here to protect the model.

This will be particularly important when we explore concepts such as the self, karma, emptiness and awakening.

These subjects have generated interpretations, debates and disagreements for centuries.

I don't intend to resolve all these controversies once and for all.

I want to investigate what we can learn from them through our perspective, without forgetting that other ways of understanding them exist.

## A small experiment

This time I'd like to suggest something different.

Think of a belief you hold about yourself.

It can be something simple.

“I'm an impatient person.”

“I have no willpower.”

“I'm bad at relationships.”

“I'm too sensitive.”

Or even something positive.

“I always try to do the right thing.”

Now ask yourself:

Does this describe something that happens every single time?

In which situations does it seem true?

In which situations doesn't it?

What experiences may have helped create this belief?

And above all, what happens when you stop treating it as an absolute truth and begin regarding it as an interpretation you can investigate?

You don't have to reach any conclusion.

Perhaps you'll discover that your belief describes some of your habits fairly well.

Perhaps you'll discover numerous exceptions.

Or perhaps you'll end up even more confused.

That's fine.

Sometimes becoming less certain about something is a way forward.

## Let's continue

We now have our starting point.

We'll regard the mind as a conditioned system, investigate the relationships between its processes and explore how practice can transform our experience.

But we won't turn this perspective into an unquestionable doctrine.

Our model is not reality.

It doesn't explain everything.

It doesn't turn our experiences into proof of extraordinary claims.

And it doesn't exempt us from responsibility for our actions.

It's a tool for exploration.

Perhaps, over time, we'll have to modify it, expand it or even abandon parts of it.

And that would be perfectly fine.

Because the purpose of this book isn't for you to end up believing in our model.

It's to make you more curious about investigating your own experience.

The map is on the table. Now we can begin exploring the territory.
