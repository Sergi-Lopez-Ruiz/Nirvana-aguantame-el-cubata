# 29. What If Our Model Is Wrong Too?

I have bad news.

A good deal of what you've just read might be wrong.

I know.

We've spent twenty-eight chapters building a model of the mind, investigating suffering, reinterpreting Buddhist teachings, and proposing ways of learning to live better.

And now, just when it seemed we were beginning to understand something, I'm here to tell you that perhaps we've got it wrong.

As marketing strategies go, I admit it's somewhat questionable.

But there's an important reason.

If our model is meant to help us question our interpretations, it must be willing to be questioned too.

And perhaps this is one of the most important chapters in the book.

Because we're going to stop using our model to look at other things.

And look at the model itself.

## A Model Isn't Reality

Let's return to the beginning.

What have we tried to do?

We've proposed looking at the mind as a conditioned system.

A collection of processes that arise, interact, and change depending on causes and conditions.

We've investigated how our interpretations, intentions, sensations, and habits may contribute to the construction of our experience.

And we've suggested that certain dynamics may help perpetuate suffering.

We've also proposed three major forms of training.

Love.

Concentration.

Investigation.

Through them, we try to learn to relate better to our experience and develop conditions that favor wiser and more compassionate responses.

So far, I think we have a reasonably coherent framework.

But coherence doesn't prove that a model is correct.

We can construct extraordinarily elegant explanations that fail to describe adequately what we're trying to understand.

And our minds are particularly good at finding patterns.

Even when those patterns don't exist.

So we need to distinguish three things.

An explanation that is understandable.

An explanation that fits our experience.

And an explanation that accurately describes the mechanisms producing that experience.

They aren't necessarily equivalent.

A model can be useful for interpreting our experience without fully explaining how it works.

And perhaps we should remember that distinction every time we feel we've found a definitive answer.

## The Danger of Explaining Absolutely Everything

There's something that particularly worries me about overly general models.

They can end up explaining anything.

Imagine someone gets angry.

We say:

“It's conditioned.”

If they don't get angry:

“That's conditioned too.”

If they develop a profound understanding:

“It's a conditioned process.”

And if they completely reject our model:

“Naturally. Their rejection is conditioned too.”

Magnificent.

It seems we've built an explanation that can't be refuted.

But perhaps that's not a virtue.

If we use the word “conditioning” to explain absolutely every outcome, we risk explaining nothing specific.

Our model needs to identify relevant relationships.

It isn't enough to say that everything depends on causes and conditions.

We have to ask which ones.

How they interact.

What consequences they produce.

And which observations might make us revise our hypotheses.

For example, if we suggest that certain interpretations fuel an emotional reaction, we can investigate whether changing those interpretations changes the reaction.

Perhaps it does.

Or perhaps we discover that the reaction remains practically the same.

Then we'll need to ask which other processes are involved.

Perhaps bodily factors.

Deeply learned habits.

Environmental conditions.

Or mechanisms we don't yet understand.

We can't simply answer that everything is still conditioned.

That may be compatible with our general perspective.

But it doesn't help us identify which part of the explanation needs improving.

A good explanation should not merely help us understand what happens.

It should also help us recognize when we're wrong.

## What If Ignorance Doesn't Work the Way We Imagine?

Throughout this book, we've proposed an interpretation of ignorance that I find particularly interesting.

Ignorance might be understood as a configuration of conditioned processes that helps perpetuate certain dynamics generating suffering.

Not simply a lack of information.

But a way of functioning.

An organization of mutually reinforcing tendencies.

Perhaps attachment reinforces certain interpretations.

Perhaps those interpretations encourage new responses.

And perhaps those responses keep reproducing similar conditions.

From this perspective, wisdom could help transform the system.

But there are several difficulties here.

First, we don't know whether all the phenomena Buddhist traditions group under avijjā can be adequately explained by a single type of mechanism.

Second, there may be very different psychological processes contributing to different forms of suffering.

And third, some of the relationships we've proposed could be incomplete or mistaken.

Perhaps certain responses aren't sustained mainly by interpretations.

Perhaps others depend much more heavily on physiological or social mechanisms.

And some may arise through relationships we haven't yet learned to identify.

That's why I think we should avoid turning ignorance into some universal enemy responsible for absolutely everything.

That would be quite an irony.

We've tried to abandon the idea of an independent self controlling our experience.

And we could end up constructing another imaginary entity, called ignorance, that magically explains anything we don't understand.

We don't need to do that.

We can use the concept of ignorance as a tool.

And continually review what we're trying to describe with it.

## And What About Autosuggestion?

I want to pause at one of our more recent hypotheses.

Autosuggestion.

We've explored how expectations and interpretations may influence our experience.

How certain beliefs can reinforce sensations and emotions.

And how we can create dynamics in which what we expect contributes to experiences that seem to confirm those expectations.

I find the idea extraordinarily interesting.

Perhaps autosuggestion plays a part in some of the mechanisms that perpetuate suffering.

But we must be careful not to turn it into a universal explanation.

Not all suffering comes from a mistaken interpretation.

Not all pain can be changed through expectations.

And not all unpleasant emotions arise from stories we tell ourselves.

Our bodies can suffer.

We can become ill.

Lose someone.

Experience injustice.

Or go through difficulties we haven't chosen.

And those experiences have real consequences.

It would be profoundly unfair to use our model to suggest that people suffer because they don't know how to think correctly.

I don't want to write that book.

If autosuggestion plays an important role in certain dynamics, we need to investigate which ones.

Under what conditions.

And how far its influence extends.

Perhaps we'll discover that it's deeply involved in some cases.

And barely involved in others.

It's also possible that we're using one word to group different processes together.

Expectations.

Learning.

Selective attention.

Interpretations.

And physiological effects associated with emotional states.

We need to distinguish them.

Because an overly general explanation can cause us to lose precisely the details we wanted to understand.

## Our Knowledge of the Mind Is Limited

There's another fundamental difficulty.

We're trying to understand the mind using the mind.

And that poses an interesting problem.

We can observe our thoughts.

Recognize emotions.

Investigate sensations.

And develop introspective attention.

But we don't have direct access to every process producing our experience.

We can recognize an intention when it appears.

That doesn't mean we know all its causes.

We can experience a profound transformation.

That doesn't mean we understand exactly which mechanisms produced it.

And we can reconstruct an experience retrospectively.

But our memory is conditioned too.

It can be mistaken.

Or reorganize events in ways we find convincing.

I think this is especially important when investigating extraordinary meditative experiences.

We can develop states of concentration.

Nondual experiences.

Profound changes in our perception of identity.

Or extraordinarily intense bodily sensations.

But we need to distinguish between experiencing something and interpreting its meaning.

We don't have to dismiss such experiences.

They can teach us a great deal.

But neither should we automatically turn them into evidence for our theories.

Experience can be a source of knowledge without becoming an infallible authority.

And perhaps recognizing its limits is one of the most important forms of wisdom.

## What If Our Model Is Suggesting What We Should Find?

Here we encounter a particularly uncomfortable difficulty.

Our model can influence our experience too.

Imagine you've spent months studying the idea that intentions are conditioned processes.

Then you begin observing them during meditation.

And gradually you start noticing that they arise without your being able to identify an independent controller.

The experience can feel extraordinarily convincing.

But we need to ask a question.

Are we discovering something we hadn't previously noticed?

Or are we learning to interpret our experiences through the model's categories?

Perhaps both.

It won't always be easy to tell them apart.

And here autosuggestion appears once again.

A model can direct our attention toward certain phenomena.

It can encourage particular expectations.

And those expectations can influence what we perceive and remember.

That doesn't mean all our observations are false.

Science also uses models to guide investigations.

But precisely for that reason, it needs to test hypotheses against observations capable of challenging them.

Our project should do something similar.

We can use the model to formulate questions.

But we shouldn't treat every answer as automatic confirmation that we were right.

And perhaps the most important question is this.

What observations would we be willing to accept as reasons to change our minds?

If we can't answer that, perhaps our model is beginning to turn into an overly rigid belief.

## What If There Is No Single Explanation for Suffering?

Throughout the book, we've tried to identify general relationships.

Attachment.

Aversion.

Ignorance.

Conditioning.

But perhaps human suffering can't be reduced to one mechanism.

Consider different situations.

One person experiences physical pain.

Another is grieving.

Someone lives in poverty.

Another person is trapped in an abusive relationship.

And somebody else suffers because they're convinced they'll never be good enough.

We may find common elements.

But also enormous differences.

The conditions generating and sustaining these experiences are not identical.

And the appropriate responses need not be identical either.

Sometimes meditation may help.

In other cases, we may need medical care.

Psychological support.

Social change.

Protection.

Rest.

Or simply someone who will listen.

We shouldn't use a general theory of mind to replace every other form of knowledge and assistance.

Our model can offer a perspective.

But it doesn't need to be the only one.

And here I want to return to an idea we've explored before.

Perhaps certain general conditions contribute to multiple forms of suffering.

But specific mechanisms may also exist that require different interventions.

Our model should help us investigate those differences.

Not erase them.

## Responsibility Also Requires Care

There's one interpretation of our model that worries me especially.

We've suggested that our intentions are conditioned too.

And that perhaps we don't need to imagine an independent self controlling every mental process.

But what happens if someone uses that idea to justify their behavior?

“I didn't choose to hurt anyone. Causes and conditions simply arose.”

That may be an explanation of how we understand behavior.

But it doesn't erase its consequences.

Or our practical responsibility.

If an action causes harm, we must acknowledge it.

Protect those who may be affected.

Make amends where possible.

And change the conditions that make repetition more likely.

Causal understanding doesn't replace ethics.

It can help us develop ethics more intelligently.

But it can also be used to escape responsibility.

And we must avoid that.

That's why our first training is so important.

Love.

Not as an abstract emotion we use to feel spiritually superior.

But as a practical orientation toward care.

For ourselves.

And for others.

If our model makes us less responsible, less compassionate, or more indifferent to suffering, perhaps we should ask ourselves what we're doing wrong.

However extraordinarily elegant our explanations may be.

## The Paradox of a Conditioned Model

Now I want to suggest something I find particularly amusing.

If our model says the mind is a conditioned system, then the model itself is a conditioned product.

It didn't appear from a position outside experience.

It arose through reading.

Meditation.

Conversations.

Memories.

Expectations.

And countless interpretations.

It also depends on my training.

My culture.

The people who have taught me.

And the questions I've learned to ask.

There's no reason to imagine that my perspective is magically free of those conditions.

And this brings us to an important consequence.

Our model cannot use its own explanation of conditioning as a guarantee that it has somehow overcome all its conditioning.

That would be rather absurd.

But also very human.

We can discover an idea we find profound.

And begin to identify with it.

We build a new image of ourselves.

The person who understands how the mind works.

And perhaps we begin defending that identity.

Through exactly the kinds of dynamics we intended to investigate.

We don't have to do that.

We can develop a model.

Use it.

And allow it to change.

Without making it an extension of our identity.

## Things We Still Don't Know

I want to acknowledge some important limitations.

We don't know exactly how conscious experience arises.

We can't fully describe its mechanisms.

We don't know whether our interpretations of advanced meditative states adequately match every phenomenon described by the traditions.

Nor do we know whether the hypotheses we've proposed about pīti, brain networks, and feedback processes correctly explain those experiences.

We don't have a universal test for awakening.

Nor do we know how to establish scientifically that certain mental processes have disappeared irreversibly.

And we can't resolve every metaphysical issue raised by the different Buddhist traditions with this model.

We could go on listing uncertainties for many pages.

But perhaps we don't need to.

What matters is understanding that our model has limits.

And that these limits don't automatically make it useless.

We can understand some relationships without understanding all of them.

We can develop beneficial practices without possessing a definitive account of their mechanisms.

And we can learn to live better without solving every mystery of consciousness.

Perhaps the question isn't how to obtain a model without any uncertainty.

But how to work honestly with the uncertainties we have.

## The Danger of Falling in Love with Our Explanations

There's something I've learned during my life.

We can fall in love with an idea.

Especially when it helps us make sense of experiences that previously confused us.

Suddenly everything seems to fit.

The pieces fall into place.

And we experience extraordinary satisfaction.

But here's the danger.

We may begin defending an explanation because we like it.

Not because it's correct.

And the more effort we've put into developing it, the harder it can be to acknowledge its limitations.

Imagine you've spent years investigating a theory.

You've written hundreds of pages.

Built connections between concepts.

And explained to others why you think it works.

Then an observation appears that challenges a fundamental part of it.

What do you do?

You can ignore it.

Construct an additional explanation to protect your theory.

Or start questioning the credibility of the person presenting it.

You can also do something much harder.

Listen.

Investigate.

And perhaps change your mind.

We won't always need to abandon the entire model.

Sometimes changing one part will suffice.

Or recognizing that it only works under certain conditions.

But we must be willing to do it.

Because perhaps one of the subtlest forms of attachment is the need to be right.

And I suspect that writing a book about nonattachment while desperately defending every one of our ideas would be quite a contradiction.

## What If the Model Works?

After all that criticism, perhaps you're wondering whether I still consider everything we've built useful.

Yes.

I think it can be.

But perhaps we should reconsider what “working” means.

A model doesn't have to describe reality completely to be useful.

Think of a map.

It can help us find our bearings.

Find a route.

And recognize important relationships between places.

But it doesn't need to include every stone.

Every tree.

Or every ant crossing a road.

In fact, a map that tried to show absolutely every detail would probably be rather difficult to use.

Its value depends on what it enables us to do.

But that doesn't mean every map is equally good.

If the roads are wrong, we may end up somewhere else.

And if important dangers are missing, we may make harmful decisions.

So we need to evaluate our model using appropriate criteria.

Does it help us ask clearer questions?

Does it allow us to recognize conditions we hadn't noticed before?

Does it encourage useful practices?

Does it reduce certain forms of suffering?

Does it help us relate better to others?

And does it still work when we encounter experiences that don't fit easily with our expectations?

These are questions we can investigate.

Perhaps some answers will be positive.

Others won't.

We don't need to decide in advance.

## The Difference Between a Useful Explanation and a Definitive Truth

I want to emphasize one distinction.

Throughout the book, we've proposed functional interpretations of various Buddhist concepts.

Ignorance as a conditioned configuration.

Karma as processes involving intentions, habits, and consequences.

Liberation as the possible cessation of dynamics that perpetuate suffering.

And wisdom as the ability to recognize and transform certain relationships.

These explanations may prove useful.

But they don't necessarily exhaust the traditional meanings.

Nor do they demonstrate that the original interpretations were equivalent to ours.

Some Buddhist teachings include claims about rebirth.

About realms of existence.

About irreversible attainments.

And about phenomena our model cannot verify.

We can investigate them.

Compare them.

And acknowledge their differences.

Without pretending we've explained them all.

Because perhaps the mistake isn't using a new model.

It's forgetting that we're using a model.

And turning a provisional perspective into an absolute claim.

## Wisdom Can Be Wrong Too

There's another idea I want to revisit.

Throughout the book, we've spoken of wisdom as an operational remedy for ignorance.

But perhaps we should be careful with that formulation.

What exactly do we mean by wisdom?

Correct knowledge?

The ability to recognize certain dynamics?

Practical discernment?

A profound transformation of our relationship with experience?

We're probably grouping several different processes together.

And perhaps they don't all work in the same way.

We can understand a situation intellectually and respond harmfully.

We can also act with enormous compassion without a sophisticated philosophical explanation.

And we can develop extraordinary contemplative abilities while remaining mistaken about aspects of the world.

This invites us to distinguish different kinds of knowledge and skill.

Conceptual understanding can help us.

Experience can teach us.

And habits can transform our responses.

But we needn't assume that all these processes are equivalent.

Perhaps a future task for our model will be to explain better how they relate.

And what developing wisdom really means.

I don't want to use that word as a magical solution.

If we say that ignorance generates suffering and wisdom eliminates it, we still need to explain how.

Otherwise we've merely replaced a question with two rather lovely words.

## What If We Don't Need to Know Everything?

Something curious has happened along my path.

The more I've tried to understand experience, the more questions have appeared.

At first I thought progress meant finding answers.

Then I discovered that some answers generated deeper questions.

And eventually I began to recognize something that had previously made me uncomfortable.

Perhaps I don't need to resolve them all.

That doesn't mean I've lost my curiosity.

Quite the opposite.

I still find investigating the mind, consciousness, and contemplative practice fascinating.

But perhaps I can do so without turning every uncertainty into a personal problem.

Without needing a definitive explanation.

And without building an identity around what I think I've understood.

There's a particular freedom in recognizing that we may not know.

Not because ignorance is desirable.

But because we stop needing to pretend to have certainties we don't possess.

And perhaps that allows us to investigate more honestly.

We can formulate hypotheses.

Test them.

Revise them.

And learn.

Without each change of mind becoming a defeat.

Perhaps an important part of wisdom is needing fewer and fewer certainties in order to keep learning.

## Love as a Criterion, Not a Proof

I want to return to an idea that has accompanied us throughout the book.

Love.

We've talked about learning to love ourselves.

Cultivating goodwill.

Developing compassion.

And learning to relate better to others.

We've also suggested that love and wisdom can support one another deeply.

But perhaps we should ask something.

Can love prove that our model is correct?

No.

A person can misunderstand certain phenomena and still act with great kindness.

And somebody can construct an excellent scientific explanation while behaving cruelly.

Love doesn't replace investigation.

But perhaps it can help us evaluate the consequences of what we're doing.

If our model makes us more arrogant, perhaps something needs revisiting.

If we begin despising those who don't share our views, perhaps we're feeding a harmful dynamic.

And if we use our explanations to justify harm, perhaps we've forgotten a fundamental part of the path.

That doesn't mean a theory is true because it makes us feel good.

Nor that we should reject uncomfortable knowledge.

But we can remember what we're trying to achieve.

Understanding our experience better.

Reducing unnecessary suffering.

And learning to live with greater love and wisdom.

Perhaps we can't prove our model definitively correct.

But we can try to recognize whether it's contributing to those aims.

And when it isn't, ask ourselves why.

## What If Tomorrow We Discover We're Wrong?

Let's imagine a situation.

Tomorrow an extraordinarily solid study appears.

Or an observation we hadn't considered.

And we discover that an important part of our model is wrong.

Perhaps our hypotheses about autosuggestion prove too general.

Or certain emotional mechanisms turn out to work differently.

Perhaps we find a better explanation of meditative experiences.

Or discover that some of our interpretations of the Dharma don't represent the traditional teachings accurately.

What should we do?

Change the model.

As simple as that.

Of course, emotionally it might not be so simple.

We may feel disappointment.

Frustration.

Or sadness.

We've invested time and effort in building it.

But perhaps we can also experience something else.

Gratitude.

Because we've learned something new.

And because we now have an explanation that may be more accurate.

From our model's perspective, even resistance to changing our minds can be seen as a conditioned reaction.

But that doesn't mean we should justify it.

We can recognize it.

Investigate it.

And carry on.

Perhaps we don't need to defend our ideas as if they were part of us.

We can allow them to evolve.

And perhaps that's exactly the kind of relationship with knowledge we've tried to develop throughout the book.

## The Book Is a Construction Too

At this point, I want to acknowledge something.

This book is conditioned too.

It has arisen through my experiences.

My reading.

My conversations.

My interpretations.

And countless questions I still don't know how to answer.

It isn't a revelation.

Or a definitive teaching.

Nor does it claim to represent every Buddhist tradition perfectly.

It's an attempt to understand.

An exploration.

And an invitation.

Perhaps some of its ideas will be useful to you.

Others may not.

You might find better explanations.

Or discover mistakes I haven't yet recognized.

If so, wonderful.

I'd like you to challenge them.

Review them.

And use them only for as long as they help you understand.

I don't want this book to become another authority you have to accept.

Or an identity you need to defend.

In fact, if you finished it believing absolutely everything I've written without questioning it, I'd suspect I hadn't explained my own message particularly well.

Because perhaps one of the most important lessons I want to share is precisely this.

You don't have to believe me to learn from what we've investigated.

You can observe.

Practice.

Compare.

And decide what makes sense to you.

Without turning your experience into a universal truth.

And without giving up your ability to learn from other people.

## The Raft, Once Again

In the previous chapter, we recalled the metaphor of the raft.

A tool that helps us cross.

But one that we needn't carry on our shoulders forever.

I want to return to it one last time.

Because perhaps our model is a raft too.

We've built it with what we had.

Buddhist teachings.

Contemplative experiences.

Psychological models.

Philosophical questions.

And some personal ideas.

It might not be perfect.

It may have holes.

Poorly assembled parts.

And some fairly questionable engineering solutions.

But perhaps it can help us cross some difficulties.

And learn to understand our experience better.

I don't need it to be the world's best raft.

Or the only one.

Nor do I need everyone to want to use it.

Perhaps some people will find more suitable tools.

Or manage to build something better.

I'd find that wonderful.

Because in the end, we're not trying to win a competition between models.

We're trying to reduce suffering.

Learn.

And live better.

And perhaps, once we understand that, we'll discover we don't need to defend the raft.

We can help others build their own.

Or share what we learned while building this one.

Then perhaps we can leave it on the shore.

And keep walking.

We've reached the end of our investigation.

And we don't need to achieve an extraordinary spiritual attainment to recognize that we can do something good for another person.

We can begin now.

## The Final Experiment

I want to suggest one last exercise.

But this time you don't need to meditate.

Or observe your breathing.

Or investigate the conditions of your thoughts.

Simply think of a person.

Someone who may be going through a difficult time.

Or someone you love.

Or even someone with whom you've had a conflict.

Now ask yourself:

Is there something small I could do to contribute to their well-being?

Perhaps listen.

Apologize.

Offer help.

Set a necessary boundary.

Or simply share a few minutes of your time.

You don't need to do anything extraordinary.

Or prove you're a good person.

Simply act.

And then carry on living.

Because in the end, perhaps the Dharma isn't so much about accumulating extraordinary explanations as it is about learning to relate better to our experience.

And to the people who share it with us.

Throughout this book, we've tried to understand how suffering is constructed.

But I wouldn't want us to forget something important.

We can also help construct well-being.

And perhaps some of the most valuable conditions we can develop are precisely those that encourage love.

I don't know whether we've found the right model.

I don't know whether some of our hypotheses will survive future investigation.

Nor do I know how far contemplative training can really take us.

But there is something I want to keep doing.

Learning.

Loving.

And acting.

Not because I've found a definitive truth.

But because it strikes me as a good way to live while I keep investigating.

And perhaps tomorrow I'll change my mind about some things.

I hope so.

It will mean I can still learn.

If you've made it this far, perhaps you're expecting a definitive conclusion.

An explanation that brings all the teachings together.

A final answer to the question in the title.

Nirvana?

I'm not going to pretend I can settle in a few lines a question that has occupied countless people for more than two thousand years.

Learning to recognize suffering.

Understanding some of its conditions.

And developing ways of living that don't need to feed it constantly.

Learning to love.

To concentrate.

And to investigate.

Without making my experience the center of the universe.

Without needing a definitive metaphysical explanation.

And without forgetting that I can still be wrong.

Perhaps nirvana, as Buddhism describes it, is much deeper than our model can explain.

Perhaps some of our interpretations are wrong.

And perhaps we still have an enormous amount to learn.

We can carry on investigating.

But meanwhile, life goes on.
