# 17. Investigation: Learning to Understand (vipassanā)

I have a question.

Have you ever tried observing your thoughts?

I don't mean thinking *about* them.

You probably do that all the time.

I mean observing how they arise, how they change, and how they disappear.

Sounds simple.

Until you try it.

Because our minds have an extraordinary ability to turn any attempt at observation into another mental activity.

You try to observe a thought.

And another one appears, saying:

“I'm observing a thought.”

Then another:

“How interesting. I'm learning to observe my mind.”

And, if you're having a particularly inspired day:

“Perhaps I'm about to attain enlightenment.”

Congratulations.

You've gone from observing a thought to becoming a spiritual master in approximately four seconds.

Our minds are wonderful.

But that's precisely why we need to learn to investigate carefully.

Welcome to vipassanā.

## What is vipassanā?

Vipassanā is a Pali word often translated as insight or clear seeing.

In Buddhist practice, it is associated with developing a deep understanding of experience, especially characteristics such as impermanence, unsatisfactoriness, and not-self.

But I want to begin with a distinction.

Vipassanā is not simply thinking a great deal about our experience.

We can reflect on suffering for hours.

We can devise extraordinarily sophisticated theories about consciousness.

We can understand dependent origination intellectually.

And still react in ways that contradict everything we think we've learned.

Why?

Perhaps because knowing an explanation does not necessarily mean we've transformed the processes organizing our experience.

Here lies a fundamental difference between understanding an idea and directly recognizing a phenomenon while it occurs.

Imagine you know perfectly well that anger is a conditioned reaction.

You've read several books about it.

You know its possible causes.

You could even explain the physiological and psychological processes involved.

But somebody insults you.

And you get angry.

Very angry.

In that moment, all your knowledge may turn out to be surprisingly unhelpful.

Not because it's false.

But because knowing something does not guarantee that knowledge will be available, or sufficiently influential, when an intense reaction appears.

Vipassanā practice invites us to investigate our experience directly.

To recognize how sensations arise.

How thoughts emerge.

How emotions develop.

And how particular interpretations, desires, and reactions help sustain suffering.

We're not merely trying to build an explanation.

We're trying to develop an understanding capable of transforming how we relate to what happens.

## The nearest laboratory

As an engineer, I like to think we're working with a system we can study.

But there's an important difficulty.

We can't take our minds apart, put their components on a table, and examine them one by one.

And attempting to do so would probably be a terrible idea.

Our main field of investigation will be experience.

We can observe bodily sensations.

Thoughts.

Emotions.

Memories.

Intentions.

And the ways all these phenomena seem to relate to one another.

But there is something we must remember.

We don't have direct access to every cause of our experiences.

We can notice a thought appearing without necessarily knowing all the processes that produced it.

We can recognize an emotion without fully understanding where it came from.

And we can misinterpret what we're observing.

That's why I like to imagine vipassanā as an investigation requiring two qualities.

Curiosity.

And humility.

Curiosity invites us to observe.

Humility reminds us that our interpretations may be wrong.

And both are essential.

Because if we begin meditating convinced that we already have all the answers, we may end up finding exactly what we expected to find.

That wouldn't be particularly rigorous research.

## Discovering vedanā

One Buddhist concept has become extraordinarily important in my practice.

*Vedanā*.

It's often translated as *feeling* or *sensation*, although these translations can be confusing.

It doesn't simply mean a bodily sensation.

Nor is it a fully developed emotion.

Vedanā is the feeling tone of an experience.

It may be pleasant.

Unpleasant.

Or neutral.

Sounds fairly simple.

But notice what happens when you begin recognizing it.

Imagine you're sitting quietly and hear a loud noise.

An auditory experience appears.

Perhaps a bodily sensation too.

And an unpleasant feeling tone may arise.

Then irritation may appear.

“Who's making that noise?”

And perhaps a few seconds later, you're already forming a rather strong opinion about your neighbors' manners.

But what happened before that whole story?

There was an experience.

And that experience had an unpleasant feeling tone.

During my practice, I began paying particular attention to that moment.

Recognizing pleasant and unpleasant feelings before more elaborate interpretations developed.

And I discovered that it was an extraordinarily interesting field of investigation.

Because many of our reactions seem to organize themselves around these feeling tones.

When something feels pleasant, the desire to keep it may appear.

When it feels unpleasant, the impulse to get rid of it may arise.

And when it feels neutral, we may barely notice it.

Of course, the real relationships are far more complex.

But this observation offers a particularly useful starting point.

Perhaps we can learn to recognize the moment when an experience begins turning into a reaction.

And perhaps that ability can profoundly transform the way we respond.

## Between feeling and reaction

Let's return to the twelve links of dependent origination.

The traditional sequence contains a particularly important relationship.

Contact conditions *vedanā*.

And vedanā can condition *taṇhā*, craving.

We need to be precise here.

This does not mean every pleasant or unpleasant experience must generate attachment or aversion.

Indeed, Buddhist practice points to the possibility of experiencing sensations without becoming caught in those responses.

Imagine an unpleasant feeling appears.

Perhaps you normally respond by trying to eliminate it immediately.

But this time you manage to recognize it.

There is discomfort.

Simply discomfort.

You don't have to construct a story about how unfair it is.

You don't have to identify with it.

And you don't need to convince yourself it's pleasant.

You can recognize it.

Perhaps the sensation continues.

Perhaps it disappears.

Perhaps it changes.

But you've begun investigating a different possibility.

The arising of a feeling doesn't necessarily require us to feed every reaction that usually accompanies it.

This may seem like a small discovery.

But its consequences could be extraordinary.

Because much of our everyday experience includes pleasant, unpleasant, and neutral feeling tones.

And if we can transform our relationship with them, perhaps we can change many of the dynamics sustaining our suffering.

Not through a magical decision to stop feeling.

But by developing conditions that favor different responses.

## Observing without fighting

Here we run into a difficulty.

When we discover a harmful reaction, we normally want to eliminate it.

We notice anger.

And want to stop feeling angry.

We recognize fear.

And want it to disappear.

We discover attachment.

And become angry that we're still attached.

The mind can turn even the wish to be free from suffering into another source of suffering.

That's why I want to emphasize a distinction.

Investigating an experience does not necessarily mean trying to change it immediately.

We can observe anger without needing it to disappear.

We can recognize a worry without having to solve it right then.

And we can experience an unpleasant feeling without automatically making an enemy of it.

That doesn't mean we should remain passive in harmful situations.

If somebody is hurting us, we may need to act.

If there's a real problem, we may need to solve it.

And if an emotion becomes too intense, resting, seeking support, or changing activity may be wiser.

Observation doesn't replace self-care.

But when it is appropriate to investigate an experience, we can try to understand it without immediately adding a struggle against it.

And perhaps we'll discover that some reactions begin to change precisely when we stop treating them as enemies.

## The observer is part of the system too

Now we come to a question I find particularly interesting.

When we speak of observing experience, it seems there must be somebody doing the observing.

A separate observer.

A tiny scientist sitting inside our heads, watching mental events unfold.

But what happens if we investigate the feeling of being an observer as well?

We might find thoughts about ourselves.

Bodily sensations.

Intentions.

A particular perspective from which experience seems organized.

And perhaps we'll discover that this feeling changes too.

During my practice, I've experienced moments when the usual perception of being a separate observer changed profoundly.

Sometimes the distinction between the observer and the observed seemed to disappear.

For quite a long time, I interpreted those experiences as especially profound discoveries about the nature of reality.

But over the years, I began looking at them differently.

What if the very feeling of observing from a separate center is also conditioned?

What if experiences in which that center seems to disappear are other possible configurations of experience?

That doesn't mean denying their value.

But it does call into question some of the conclusions we might draw from them.

From the perspective of our model, we can investigate both the experience of being an observer and experiences in which that feeling changes or disappears.

We don't need to decide immediately which reveals the true nature of reality.

We can observe.

Compare.

Investigate.

And recognize that our interpretation is itself a conditioned process.

There's something rather funny about all this.

We may end up discovering that even the little scientist we imagined inside our heads was another phenomenon to investigate.

I hope he doesn't take offense.

## The trap of finding what we're looking for

Now I'd like to introduce a problem I consider particularly important.

Suggestion.

And, more specifically, autosuggestion.

Our experience doesn't depend solely on what happens around us.

It can also be influenced by our expectations, beliefs, and interpretations.

Imagine somebody tells you a particular meditation practice will produce a sensation of energy rising up your spine.

You begin practicing.

Paying close attention to your back.

Expecting that sensation to appear.

And one day you feel something.

A vibration.

A change in temperature.

A feeling that's hard to describe.

What happened?

There are several possibilities.

Perhaps you recognized a sensation that had previously gone unnoticed.

Perhaps the practice encouraged real changes in your bodily experience.

Perhaps your expectations contributed to changing your perception.

Or perhaps all these processes were involved.

The important thing is to understand that experiencing something doesn't automatically prove our explanation is correct.

And that matters especially in meditation.

When we study a tradition, we learn concepts, descriptions, and expectations about what we might experience.

Those ideas can help us recognize certain phenomena.

But they can also influence how we interpret what happens.

From our model's perspective, we might view autosuggestion as a set of processes through which our expectations and interpretations contribute to shaping later experiences.

This isn't an exhaustive scientific definition.

Nor does it mean that every extraordinary meditative experience is the product of autosuggestion.

But I think its role deserves investigation.

Because perhaps some of the dynamics generating suffering are connected to our ability to build and reinforce interpretations.

Imagine somebody doesn't reply to your message.

A thought appears:

“They're angry with me.”

Then you remember a recent conversation.

Perhaps you interpret some remark as a sign of rejection.

You begin to worry.

And that worry makes the original interpretation seem even more convincing.

The other person was simply asleep.

But you've already managed to construct a little tragedy.

Here we can observe how an interpretation may influence later experiences and then receive what looks like confirmation from them.

We don't need to claim that all suffering comes from autosuggestion.

But we can investigate whether processes of expectation, interpretation, and confirmation help perpetuate certain dynamics.

And perhaps vipassanā can help us recognize them.

## Metacognition: recognizing that we're interpreting

There's another concept that can help us understand this practice.

Metacognition.

In psychology, the term generally refers to our ability to know, observe, and regulate aspects of our own cognitive processes.

For example, we can think something.

But we can also recognize that we're thinking.

We can experience worry.

And recognize that our minds are making a prediction.

We can feel that an interpretation is absolutely true.

And simultaneously understand that it remains an interpretation.

This capacity isn't exclusive to meditation.

We use it constantly, to varying degrees.

But we can train it.

And here's a fascinating connection with our model.

Imagine you're dreaming.

During a dream you may experience extraordinary events and accept them as completely normal.

Perhaps you're flying.

Perhaps you're talking with someone who died years ago.

Or perhaps you've decided to ride a pink elephant to work.

And none of it seems especially strange.

But sometimes something different happens.

You realize you're dreaming.

The dream may continue.

The images still appear.

But your relationship with them changes.

You've recognized something important about the nature of the experience you're having.

That doesn't mean you've physically woken up.

Nor that you've eliminated all the processes constructing the dream.

But you no longer need to interpret what happens in exactly the same way.

I find this comparison fascinating.

Perhaps certain metacognitive capacities allow us to recognize interpretations we would normally accept without questioning.

And when that happens, our relationship with experience may change.

But we must avoid an excessive conclusion.

Not all autosuggestion can be eliminated through metacognition.

Nor does every experience of clarity show that we've stopped being influenced by our expectations.

In fact, we may become so confident in our ability to observe the mind that we end up even more vulnerable to our own interpretations.

That's why we need to keep curiosity and humility.

And remember that even the feeling of having understood something profoundly deserves investigation.

## Vipassanā isn't about seeking extraordinary experiences

There's a temptation I know quite well.

Once we discover that meditation can produce surprising experiences, we become interested in them.

We want to understand them.

Repeat them.

Go deeper.

And perhaps reach even more extraordinary states.

There's nothing necessarily wrong with that curiosity.

I've spent plenty of time myself exploring different forms of concentration and contemplative experience.

But I've learned to distinguish two questions.

What can I experience?

And:

What can I understand about what I experience?

They aren't the same thing.

We can reach an extraordinary state of concentration and still feed habits of suffering.

We can experience a profound sense of unity and continue responding with hostility to criticism.

And we can interpret an experience as a definitive revelation without sufficiently investigating the conditions that produced it.

That's why I believe vipassanā should be directed primarily toward understanding.

We don't have to keep chasing increasingly intense experiences.

We can learn an enormous amount by observing a little irritation.

An unpleasant feeling.

A desire.

Or a thought that appears when someone disagrees with us.

In fact, these ordinary experiences may be particularly valuable.

Because they're the ones we encounter constantly in everyday life.

And precisely where our habits most clearly reveal themselves.

## How does understanding transform our habits?

Let's return to our model.

If the mind is a conditioned system, we can ask how observation influences its functioning.

Imagine you've developed the habit of responding aggressively when someone interrupts you.

Usually the sequence unfolds very quickly.

You hear the interruption.

Discomfort appears.

An interpretation arises.

“They don't respect me.”

And you respond.

But through practice, you begin to recognize elements of that dynamic.

First you notice bodily tension.

Then you identify the unpleasant feeling tone.

You recognize the thought.

And discover an intention to respond aggressively.

That doesn't mean you can automatically prevent the reaction.

But now new conditions are present.

A greater capacity for recognition.

Memories of other experiences.

A willingness to investigate.

Perhaps also a previously cultivated intention to act with love.

And those conditions can influence what happens next.

Perhaps you respond differently.

Perhaps you still get angry.

Or perhaps you notice certain reactions occurring less frequently.

We shouldn't assume every change comes directly from observation.

Human learning depends on many factors.

But our model allows us to investigate how training attention, understanding, and habitual responses can gradually change particular dynamics.

I want to highlight something here.

Liberating understanding shouldn't be confused with an explanation that merely sounds convincing to us.

The important thing is to investigate what actually changes in the way we experience and respond.

And to do so without turning each change into proof that we've attained an extraordinary spiritual state.

## Wisdom and humility

There is something curious I've experienced on my journey.

The more I've investigated experience, the more I've had to revise some of my certainties.

For years I thought I understood certain phenomena in one way.

Then new observations appeared.

New questions.

And new interpretations.

At times, ideas that had mattered enormously to me no longer seemed necessary.

It wasn't always comfortable.

Our minds rather enjoy feeling they've finally found the definitive answer.

But I've also found a certain peace in recognizing that I don't need to have it.

We can learn something valuable without understanding everything.

We can use a model without believing it definitively describes reality.

And we can recognize that an experience transformed our lives without needing to give it a metaphysical explanation.

That doesn't mean giving up knowledge.

Or pretending that all interpretations are equally valid.

It means being willing to revise our conclusions.

And perhaps that willingness is an important part of wisdom.

We don't need to turn every discovery into a new certainty to defend. We can use it as an opportunity to keep learning.

## A small experiment

Let's try a simple practice.

I suggest devoting five minutes to observing experience.

If you've never meditated, you can start with a shorter period.

Find a comfortable position.

You can close your eyes or keep them open.

For the first moments, bring your attention to the breath.

You don't need to concentrate intensely.

Just recognize that you're breathing.

Then gently broaden your attention.

Observe what appears.

Perhaps a bodily sensation.

Perhaps a sound.

Perhaps a thought.

You don't need to follow it.

Or reject it.

Try to recognize its feeling tone.

Is it pleasant?

Unpleasant?

Neutral?

Then observe whether any response arises.

Do you want it to continue?

Do you want it to disappear?

Does a story about its meaning appear?

If you find yourself completely caught up in a thought, you can calmly return to the breath.

That's fine.

Now try investigating a distinction.

What are you directly experiencing?

And what are you interpreting about that experience?

For example:

“There's tension in my chest.”

That's a description of a bodily experience.

“I'm nervous because everything will go wrong tomorrow.”

That's an interpretation that includes expectations about the future.

You don't need to decide immediately whether that interpretation is right or wrong.

Simply recognize the difference.

And observe what happens.

Perhaps you discover something interesting.

Perhaps nothing special happens.

That's fine too.

We're not trying to produce an extraordinary experience.

We're learning to observe.

And if a lot of distress arises during the exercise, you don't need to persist. You can open your eyes, orient yourself to your surroundings, and end the practice.

For people with a history of psychological destabilization or intense episodes connected to contemplative practice, it is especially important to proceed gradually and seek appropriate guidance.

Investigation should be undertaken with care, not as a battle against our own minds.

## Bringing investigation into everyday life

There is something I consider fundamental.

I don't want vipassanā to become something we do only while sitting on a cushion.

Our experience continues when we stand up.

When we work.

When we talk with our children.

When we argue with our partners.

And when somebody cuts in front of us in the supermarket queue.

That's precisely where we may find extraordinary opportunities to investigate our habits.

Imagine somebody criticizes you.

Before responding, perhaps you can recognize the unpleasant feeling tone.

Observe the interpretation arising.

And ask whether you really need to follow it.

Perhaps you discover the criticism is valid.

Perhaps it's unfair.

Or perhaps you need more information.

What matters is that you're beginning to recognize processes that once could determine your response without you even noticing.

And this connects directly with our other two trainings.

Love helps us develop an orientation toward our own well-being and that of others.

Concentration encourages the stability and clarity needed to observe.

And vipassanā lets us investigate the processes contributing to our reactions.

These aren't completely independent activities.

They can reinforce one another.

And perhaps, with time, we'll find that understanding starts to show up in our everyday actions.

Not as a constant obligation to behave correctly.

But as a growing ease in responding with clarity and compassion.

## The final trap: turning observation into control

There's something I want to warn you about before finishing.

We can become obsessed with observing our experience.

Constantly monitoring our thoughts.

Analyzing every emotion.

Investigating every intention.

And end up turning practice into a form of permanent surveillance of ourselves.

A kind of inner police officer.

Complete with uniform, whistle, and a form for recording every incorrect thought.

I don't think that would be a good destination for our practice.

Observation should help us relate better to our experience.

Not develop a new need to control everything.

Sometimes we need to investigate.

At other times, simply live.

Listen to music.

Play with our children.

Enjoy a conversation.

Or sit quietly without trying to understand absolutely anything.

Not every moment needs to become an experiment.

And not every experience requires an explanation.

Perhaps an important part of wisdom is recognizing when investigation is useful and when we can stop.

Because our goal isn't to turn life into a permanent laboratory.

It's to learn to live it.

## Learning to see

We've reached the end of our introduction to vipassanā.

We've explored how to directly investigate the sensations, thoughts, emotions, and intentions arising in experience.

We've emphasized the importance of vedanā, the pleasant, unpleasant, or neutral feeling tone accompanying our experiences.

And considered how interpretations and expectations can help generate or sustain particular reactions.

We've also introduced a hypothesis worth exploring further: autosuggestion may participate in some of the processes through which our minds perpetuate suffering.

We don't yet know how far this explanation can take us.

Nor do we have to turn it into a universal answer.

But perhaps it can help us recognize something important.

Our experience isn't only what happens.

It also includes the interpretations we construct and the responses that follow.

And we can learn to investigate those relationships.

Without needing to control every thought.

Without chasing extraordinary experiences.

And without turning our conclusions into unquestionable truths.

Perhaps we'll discover that some things we considered inevitable were habits.

That certain interpretations weren't as solid as we imagined.

And that some responses can change as we learn to understand the conditions producing them.

Vipassanā isn't about seeing what we think we ought to see. It's about learning to look, even when what we find forces us to change our minds.

And perhaps that is one of the most valuable skills we can develop.

Not only for meditation.

But for life.
