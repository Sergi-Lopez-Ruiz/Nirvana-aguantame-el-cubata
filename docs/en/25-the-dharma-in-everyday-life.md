# 25. The Dharma in Everyday Life

I have a theory.

I think it is much easier to be an extraordinarily wise person when nobody is bothering you.

Imagine sitting peacefully in a garden.

The weather is lovely.

The birds are singing.

You have nothing urgent to do.

And you've spent twenty minutes enjoying a wonderful meditation.

Perhaps you feel profoundly calm.

Perhaps you feel grateful to be alive.

Perhaps you're even beginning to think you've understood some of Buddhism's deepest teachings.

Then you hear a voice.

“Daaaaad!”

You open your eyes.

One of your children is crying.

The other insists he hasn't done absolutely anything.

There's water on the floor.

And somebody has decided to turn the sofa into a training ground for an activity that apparently involves launching cushions at very high speed.

Welcome back to saṃsāra.

But perhaps we should ask ourselves something.

Have we really left our practice behind?

Or have we just found an extraordinary opportunity to continue it?

## Life Is Not an Obstacle to Practice

When we think of Buddhism, we often picture monasteries.

Temples.

Retreats.

People meditating silently for hours.

Perhaps a life removed from everyday concerns.

Of course, the monastic tradition has played a fundamental role in preserving and transmitting the Dharma.

And having time and favorable conditions for practice can be extraordinarily valuable.

But most of us lead rather different lives.

We work.

We care for our families.

We have commitments.

We pay bills.

We shop for groceries.

And we spend a surprising amount of time searching for things that somebody has left in incomprehensible places.

Does that mean we have fewer opportunities to develop wisdom?

Perhaps we have less time for certain formal practices.

But we also continually encounter situations in which we can investigate our habits.

A conflict.

An expectation.

A worry.

A moment of joy.

A difficult conversation.

A decision.

Our experience doesn't stop when we leave the meditation cushion.

Nor do the conditions we are trying to understand disappear.

From the perspective of our model, that is especially important.

If our reactions arise depending on causes and conditions, we can investigate them wherever they appear.

And perhaps some of the most relevant conditions are found precisely in everyday life.

The Dharma need not become an activity separate from our lives. It can help us transform the way we live them.

## The Family Laboratory

If you want to investigate your conditioning, I have a recommendation.

Have children.

Of course, that isn't a requirement for practicing the Dharma.

But I must admit that parenthood provides extraordinary opportunities to discover things about our minds.

For example, you can spend an entire morning reflecting on compassion.

Understand deeply that everyone acts depending on causes and conditions.

Develop a sincere intention to relate to others with love.

And then discover that your patience struggles to survive being asked where somebody's shoes are for the fourth time.

The interesting thing is that our children aren't necessarily trying to test our spiritual development.

They're simply living.

Learning.

Experiencing emotions.

And behaving according to the abilities and conditions available to them at each moment.

But we're conditioned too.

We have expectations.

Fatigue.

Learned habits.

Ideas about how children should behave.

And memories of the way we ourselves were raised.

When all those conditions meet, rather intense reactions can arise.

Here our model can help us broaden our perspective.

Instead of looking only at the behavior that bothers us, we can investigate what is happening throughout the system.

What does the child need?

What am I interpreting?

How am I feeling?

Is some condition intensifying the conflict?

Do we need to intervene immediately, or can we wait a moment?

These questions don't guarantee the right response.

But they can help us recognize possibilities we hadn't noticed before.

And, above all, remember something important.

Our children aren't projects to be turned into perfect versions of our expectations.

They're people learning to live.

Just like us.

## Raising Children Doesn't Mean Controlling Them

There's something I've struggled to learn.

When raising our children, we naturally want them to develop certain abilities.

Responsibility.

Honesty.

Respect.

Independence.

And the ability to put their toys away before somebody breaks a foot.

But perhaps we confuse teaching with getting them to behave exactly as we want, immediately.

We give an instruction.

We expect a response.

And when it doesn't happen, frustration arises.

From our model's perspective, perhaps we can look at it differently.

We're trying to encourage the development of certain habits.

And habits require learning.

Repetition.

Age-appropriate abilities.

Supportive conditions.

And sometimes an enormous amount of patience.

This doesn't mean abandoning boundaries.

Children need guidance, safety, and appropriate consequences.

But perhaps it's more useful to ask what they're learning from our responses than to focus exclusively on obtaining immediate obedience.

If we continually respond by shouting, perhaps we're teaching something different from what we intended.

If we set boundaries firmly and respectfully, we can offer another example.

We won't always succeed.

And when we make mistakes, we'll need to acknowledge them and make amends.

An apology matters, but it isn't enough if harmful behavior keeps recurring.

We must change the conditions that encourage it and make sure our children are protected.

Perhaps one of the most important lessons we can offer them is precisely this.

Mistakes don't disappear because we justify them. But we can acknowledge them, repair the harm, and learn to behave differently.

And perhaps our children will learn as much from that process as from any of our explanations.

## The Dharma at Work

Let's leave the family behind for a moment.

Although I suspect we'll return to it before this chapter ends.

Imagine working on a complicated project.

There are deadlines.

Responsibilities.

Dependencies.

And people who, inexplicably, don't always share your brilliant interpretation of how things should be done.

Someone questions a decision.

A colleague makes a mistake.

Your manager changes a priority.

And frustration arises.

What can we practice here?

First, recognize the experience.

Perhaps there's tension in the body.

An unpleasant feeling.

A defensive thought.

And an intention to prove we're right.

Then we can investigate.

Am I trying to solve a real problem?

Or am I defending my self-image?

Is there information I don't yet have?

What consequences might my response have?

And finally, we can remember our first training.

Love.

In a professional environment, we might not usually use that word.

But we can express it through respect, honesty, cooperation, and care.

Listening.

Sharing information.

Acknowledging mistakes.

Avoiding unnecessary humiliation.

And remembering that our colleagues are people too, with difficulties, responsibilities, and worries.

We don't always have to agree.

Nor should we avoid conflict.

Sometimes we'll have to make difficult decisions.

Assign responsibilities.

Or say that a piece of work isn't good enough.

But we can do that without turning every disagreement into a personal battle.

And here's something particularly interesting.

Understanding conditioning can help us move from looking for someone to blame to investigating causes.

If a project fails, it may be useful to understand which processes, decisions, and circumstances contributed to the outcome.

That doesn't eliminate individual responsibility.

But it can help us introduce improvements that prevent the same mistakes from recurring.

As an engineer, I must admit that this way of looking at problems feels rather natural to me.

When a machine keeps failing, we don't usually fix it by yelling that it ought to behave better.

We try to understand what's happening.

Perhaps we could extend a little of that patience to people too.

Although, I admit, machines are sometimes considerably more predictable.

## When Someone Treats Us Badly

So far we've discussed situations in which we can learn to respond better.

But what happens when someone treats us unfairly?

Should we understand their conditioning and accept whatever they do?

No.

And I want to emphasize that.

Love isn't permissiveness.

Equanimity isn't resignation.

And wisdom doesn't consist of finding explanations that justify every behavior.

We can recognize that someone depends on causes and conditions.

And at the same time set firm boundaries.

We can understand that someone is suffering.

And decide that we won't allow them to hurt us.

We can act to protect others without needing to hate the person causing the problem.

This distinction matters especially in situations involving abuse, intimidation, or violence.

Safety must come first.

Not trying to maintain a contemplative attitude while harm continues.

From our model's perspective, boundaries are also part of the conditions.

They can protect.

Change harmful dynamics.

And create opportunities for different behaviors to arise.

But we shouldn't assume our compassion will necessarily transform somebody else.

Their decisions and circumstances aren't entirely within our influence.

And recognizing that limit can also be a form of wisdom.

## The Dharma at the Supermarket

Let's return to one of our favorite situations.

You're shopping.

You're in a hurry.

And somebody cuts in front of you.

An unpleasant feeling appears.

Perhaps irritation.

Then a thought.

“People have no manners!”

This is an interesting moment.

Not because you need to become a miniature Buddha among the yogurts and vegetables.

But because you've just found an opportunity to investigate.

What actually happened?

Someone stepped in front of you.

That's what you observed.

Perhaps they did it deliberately.

Perhaps they didn't notice.

You don't know yet.

But an interpretation has already appeared.

And that interpretation may fuel your reaction.

What can you do?

You could kindly ask them to respect the queue.

You could decide it isn't worth arguing.

Or perhaps simply wait.

The appropriate response will depend on the circumstances.

Practice doesn't necessarily mean always choosing the most passive option.

It means recognizing that we have different possibilities for responding.

And perhaps learning not to feed an unnecessary inner argument.

The interesting thing is that we don't need to save these opportunities for extraordinary events.

Everyday life is full of them.

Small frustrations.

Expectations.

Misunderstandings.

And moments when our minds demonstrate an admirable capacity to construct suffering from surprisingly modest materials.

## Small Joys Matter Too

So far we've talked quite a lot about conflict and difficulty.

But everyday life is also full of pleasant experiences.

A conversation.

A song.

A meal.

A walk.

An afternoon with our children.

And perhaps we should learn to pay more attention to them.

Because practice isn't only about recognizing suffering.

It can also help us appreciate well-being.

Imagine having breakfast.

Perhaps you normally eat while checking your phone, thinking about work, or mentally planning the rest of your day.

But what if you spend a few moments really noticing what you're doing?

The taste.

The temperature.

The bodily sensations.

And perhaps a little gratitude for having food.

We don't need to turn breakfast into a forty-minute spiritual ritual.

We can simply be a little more present.

And perhaps discover that some ordinary experiences contain more well-being than we usually recognize.

Here we find a connection with muditā, appreciative joy, and with our relationship to pleasure.

We can enjoy without needing an experience to last forever.

We can appreciate what's pleasant without clinging to it.

And we can allow it to end without turning its disappearance into a tragedy.

We don't need extraordinary lives to find valuable moments.

Perhaps we need to get better at noticing some of those we already have.

## Knowing How to Rest Is Practice Too

There's something I particularly want to emphasize.

The importance of rest.

Sometimes we can turn personal development into an exhausting activity.

We want to meditate.

Exercise.

Eat properly.

Read.

Learn.

Be better parents.

Work efficiently.

Develop wisdom.

And, if there's any time left, perhaps sleep.

But from the perspective of our model, we should recognize something obvious.

Rest changes our conditions too.

Poor sleep can affect our concentration.

Exhaustion can encourage impulsive responses.

And sustained stress can make it harder to relate calmly.

We can't always solve these problems through meditation.

Sometimes we need to change our circumstances.

Reduce commitments.

Ask for help.

Rest.

Or recognize that we're trying to do too much.

Remember the oxygen mask.

Learning to care for ourselves isn't a selfish concession.

It can be an important condition for caring for others.

And perhaps one of the wisest forms of practice is recognizing when we need to stop practicing formally.

Not because we're abandoning the path.

But because we're caring for the conditions that allow us to walk it.

Sometimes the wisest practice is to close the book and go to bed early.

I admit that's a rather questionable commercial recommendation for an author.

## Don't Turn Every Moment into an Experiment

There's another difficulty.

We can become obsessed with observing our experience.

Noticing every sensation.

Analyzing every thought.

Investigating every intention.

And turning our lives into a permanent laboratory.

Imagine having dinner with friends.

Someone tells a joke.

And instead of laughing, you start investigating the arising of pleasure, the conditions of your emotional response, and the impermanent nature of the humorous experience.

The joke may have ended before you finish your analysis.

And your friends might decide not to invite you next time.

We don't need to do that.

We can learn to observe our experience.

And we can also learn to enjoy it without analyzing it constantly.

Attention and understanding don't have to become permanent surveillance.

In fact, perhaps one sign that we're developing useful abilities is that living clearly requires less effort.

We can listen.

Laugh.

Play.

And act.

Without turning every moment into an assessment of our progress.

The Dharma should help us live.

Not replace our lives with an endless investigation of them.

## The World Isn't Made Better by Ideas Alone

There's a sentence that has stayed with me for a long time.

The world isn't made better by our minds, but by our hands.

Of course, it's provocative.

The mind matters too.

Our ideas influence our actions.

Understanding can transform our intentions.

And learning to think clearly can be extraordinarily valuable.

But there's something I don't want to forget.

Understanding suffering isn't the same as relieving it.

We can develop a magnificent theory of compassion.

And help nobody.

We can understand deeply that everyone is conditioned.

And continue treating those around us badly.

We can talk about emptiness for hours.

And forget to listen to someone who needs company.

I don't think the Dharma should become a competition in sophisticated explanations.

Its value should also be expressed in our actions.

In how we treat our children.

In how we respond to someone vulnerable.

In how we use our authority.

In how we listen.

And in what we do when we have an opportunity to reduce suffering.

We don't need to save the entire world.

Or solve all its problems.

But perhaps we can help create slightly better conditions wherever we are.

A conversation.

A gesture.

A decision.

A boundary set respectfully.

Help offered without expecting a reward.

Small actions whose consequences we may never fully know.

And there's something especially beautiful here.

If our actions are conditioned too, we can try to develop conditions that increasingly favor compassionate actions.

Not through a permanent effort to become extraordinary people.

But through learning.

Practice.

And care.

## The Wisdom of Recognizing Our Limits

There's something I've learned over the years.

I can't solve every problem.

I can't control every circumstance.

And I can't prevent the people I love from experiencing difficulties.

For a long time, that reality made me uncomfortable.

Perhaps because I'd developed a considerable need to control what happened around me.

But over time I began to appreciate a different perspective.

I can try to understand conditions.

I can act.

I can offer help.

And I can accept that the outcomes don't depend solely on me.

That doesn't mean abandoning my responsibilities.

It means recognizing their limits.

And perhaps that distinction allows us to act with greater serenity.

We can help without needing to control the outcome entirely.

We can love without taking ownership of other people's lives.

And we can take responsibility without becoming absolutely responsible for everything that happens.

Here we encounter equanimity again.

Not as indifference.

But as the ability to act with care while recognizing that we live in an extraordinarily complex world.

And that our influence, although important, will always be limited.

## An Ordinary Day

Let's imagine a normal day.

You wake up.

Perhaps you spend five minutes observing your breath.

Then you make breakfast.

For a few moments, you listen to someone in your family without thinking of anything else.

You leave home.

At work, a problem arises.

You notice frustration.

You recognize it.

And you decide to investigate what's happening before responding.

Later, a colleague needs help.

You give them a few minutes.

At lunchtime, you enjoy a conversation.

Then fatigue appears.

And instead of demanding even more from yourself, you recognize that perhaps you need rest.

You return home.

Your children are restless.

You try to stay patient.

Sometimes you succeed.

Other times you make mistakes.

When that happens, you acknowledge the mistake and try to make amends.

Finally, evening arrives.

You haven't attained an extraordinary experience.

You haven't discovered a metaphysical truth.

And you probably haven't managed to make the universe behave exactly as you wished.

But perhaps you've developed some slightly more caring responses.

Perhaps you've listened better.

Or recognized a reaction before acting.

Or simply enjoyed a small pleasant experience without needing anything else.

Is that enlightenment?

No.

At least, we have no reason to claim it is.

But can it be part of a path that transforms the way we live?

I think so.

And perhaps we don't need to dismiss these small changes just because they aren't extraordinary.

Because our lives are made of precisely such moments.

## A Small Experiment: Choose Just One Thing

I want to finish this part of the book with a suggestion.

I'm not going to ask you to practice everything we've learned.

Or to start observing every thought.

Or to transform your whole life over the next week.

I'll suggest something simpler.

Choose just one everyday situation.

It might be getting up in the morning.

A conversation with your partner.

Your journey to work.

Putting your children to bed.

Or any other activity you do frequently.

Now ask yourself:

What small ability would I like to develop in this situation?

Perhaps listening better.

Noticing impatience sooner.

Responding with more care.

Maintaining attention.

Or stopping the feeding of an unnecessary worry.

You don't need to develop all these abilities at once.

Choose one.

Over the next few days, try to recognize the conditions that favor or hinder its appearance.

Perhaps you'll discover you need more rest.

Or to prepare better for a conversation.

Or to remember your intention before entering a difficult situation.

Then observe what happens.

You don't need to achieve an extraordinary transformation.

Simply learn.

And if one day you don't manage it, don't turn the mistake into another reason to punish yourself.

Acknowledge it.

Make amends if appropriate.

And carry on.

Because perhaps one of the great advantages of seeing our mind as a conditioned system is recognizing that we can introduce small changes which, over time, help transform certain tendencies.

We don't know exactly how far they may take us.

But we can investigate.

## The Path Continues

We've reached the end of this part of the book.

We've learned how to begin meditating.

To distinguish attention from awareness.

To observe how our habits develop.

To recognize the relationship between body and emotions.

To investigate our reactions.

To cultivate equanimity.

And to understand some of the obstacles that arise during training.

But perhaps the most important lesson is much simpler.

We don't need to turn the Dharma into an activity separate from our existence.

We can learn to practice while we live.

And live while we learn.

It's not about achieving mental perfection.

Or eliminating all our emotions.

Or constantly monitoring our thoughts.

It's about developing abilities that help us relate better to our experience.

To ourselves.

To the people we love.

And to the world.

Perhaps on some days we'll respond wisely.

At other times we'll make mistakes.

Sometimes we'll enjoy extraordinary experiences.

And much of the time our lives will be perfectly ordinary.

We don't need to despise any of those possibilities.

We can learn from them.

And carry on.

Because perhaps the path isn't about escaping the life we have to find a completely different one.

Perhaps it's about learning to live this very life more wisely and compassionately.

With its joys.

Its difficulties.

Its contradictions.

And its countless opportunities to learn.

The Dharma shouldn't turn us into spectators of our own lives. It should help us participate in them with greater love and wisdom.

And perhaps, when we understand that, we'll discover we don't have to wait for nirvana before doing something good for the world.

We can begin now.

With our hands.

In the final part of this book, we'll explore some particularly fascinating questions.

Deep states of concentration.

Awakening.

The different Buddhist traditions.

And, finally, the limitations of the entire model we've built.

Because we still have questions.

So many questions.

And perhaps that's exactly what makes continuing the investigation so interesting.

But before moving on, I suggest something.

Close the book.

Look around you.

Perhaps there's someone you can offer a smile.

Perhaps you need to rest.

Or perhaps you can simply enjoy a few moments of peace.

You don't need to do anything extraordinary.

Your life is already happening.

And that's where we can learn to live it.
