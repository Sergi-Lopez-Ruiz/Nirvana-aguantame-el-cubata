# 20. Attention, Awareness, and Habits

I have an extraordinary ability.

I can drive for several minutes while thinking about something entirely different.

Work. What I'm going to cook for dinner. A conversation I had three days ago.

And when I reach my destination, I discover that I can barely remember parts of the journey.

Of course, I'm still paying some attention to the road. Or at least I hope so.

But many of the operations involved in driving have become habits. They no longer demand the same deliberate attention they did when I was learning.

And that raises an interesting question.

If we can learn to perform complex activities without consciously attending to every detail, could we also learn to respond more wisely and compassionately without constantly having to remind ourselves?

Let's investigate.

## Attention: a small part of our experience

In the previous chapter, we learned to direct our attention to the breath.

But perhaps you noticed something curious. While you were paying attention to your breathing, the rest of the world did not disappear completely.

You could still hear certain sounds. Perhaps you were aware that your body was sitting down. And if someone had called your name, you would probably have heard it.

In *The Mind Illuminated*, Culadasa makes a distinction that helped me enormously in understanding this phenomenon: the difference between attention and peripheral awareness.

Attention selects certain contents of our experience for more detailed processing. Peripheral awareness, in this model, keeps broader information about what is happening available to us.

We can think of them as two complementary capacities. Attention allows us to examine something. Peripheral awareness helps us keep track of the context.

Think about a conversation. Your attention may be focused on the other person's words. But you might also notice their facial expression, changes in their tone of voice, and sensations in your own body.

If your attention becomes completely absorbed in finding arguments for your reply, you may lose some of that context. And that can have important consequences.

You can be extraordinarily focused—on entirely the wrong thing.

## What does it mean to be conscious?

We need to make a distinction here.

The Spanish word *conciencia* is used to describe several different phenomena. We may mean the fact that we have experiences, the ability to notice what is happening, our awareness of ourselves, or even our moral conscience.

These are not exactly the same thing.

When we speak of attention and peripheral awareness, we are using a functional model of how information available in our experience is organised. We are not definitively explaining what consciousness is in the philosophical sense, nor settling how subjective experience arises from processes in the brain.

Those questions remain subjects of research and debate.

For our purposes, we can begin with something much simpler. At any given moment, we experience certain contents. Some occupy the centre of our attention. Others remain available, but less prominent. And our ability to notice what is happening can vary.

Meditation allows us to investigate and train some of these capacities.

But we must remember that observing experience does not mean knowing every process that produces it. We may recognise a thought when it appears without knowing precisely how it was generated. And we may discover that a reaction has begun without noticing its earliest conditions.

Our ability to observe is valuable. But it is also limited.

## The moment we notice

Imagine that you're reading.

Suddenly, you start thinking about something else. Perhaps you remember a meeting. Then another conversation. And eventually you realise that you've spent several minutes without understanding a single word.

What happened?

For a while, your attention was occupied by thoughts unrelated to what you were reading. Then something different happened: you noticed that you were distracted.

In *The Mind Illuminated* (TMI), Culadasa places particular importance on our ability to recognise what is happening with our attention. A form of introspective awareness allows us to notice distractions, changes in our mental state, and other features of experience.

This capacity can be developed through training.

And here we encounter a crucial distinction. We don't need to monitor our minds constantly with some sort of internal supervisor. We can train processes that make the spontaneous recognition of certain situations more likely.

At first, several minutes may pass before we recognise a distraction. With practice, we may notice it sooner. In some circumstances, we may even recognise thoughts as they arise without completely losing contact with what we're doing.

From the perspective of our model, this is fascinating.

The ability to notice a distraction can itself become a learned tendency. We don't need to imagine an independent observer growing more and more powerful. We can investigate how the system itself develops a greater ability to recognise its own activity.

## Who decides to pay attention?

Here is a question we already know.

Who is directing attention?

We usually say, "I'm going to concentrate." We experience an intention arising and try to keep our attention on an object. But perhaps a more interesting thought appears, and our attention moves towards it.

We could interpret this as a small battle between our will and our distractions. But our model suggests another possibility.

We can regard attention as the result of processes that compete, cooperate, and influence one another. Certain stimuli become relevant. Intentions arise. Habits favour particular responses. And our attention shifts according to those conditions.

This idea is closely related to Culadasa's model of the mind. In it, intentions and conscious contents need not originate from one central controller. We can understand them through interactions among different mental processes.

And here's something especially interesting. When we meditate, we're not trying to impose a magical will on the entire system. We're introducing conditions that can favour a different organisation of attention.

The intention to return. Recognising distractions. The satisfaction of noticing them. Repetition.

Gradually, new tendencies can develop. Attention that initially demands effort may become easier to maintain steadily through training.

## Habits: our little autopilots

Imagine learning to drive.

At first, every operation requires attention: checking the mirrors, controlling your speed, steering, watching the signs—and remembering that other drivers aren't always kind enough to behave as you expect.

It's exhausting.

But after years of practice, many of those operations become more automatic. You've developed habits. And that's extraordinarily useful.

Habits allow us to carry out complex activities without consciously deliberating about every tiny detail.

But they have one important characteristic: not all of them are beneficial.

We can learn to respond aggressively in certain situations, worry whenever uncertainty appears, interpret criticism as a threat, or check our phones whenever we feel a moment of boredom.

In our model, habits are learned tendencies that favour particular responses under certain conditions. They can become so familiar that we barely notice them happening.

Yet here lies an important possibility. If our habits were learned, some of them can also change through new experiences and training.

That won't always be easy. Some habits are deeply rooted. Others are linked to difficult experiences or circumstances that are hard to change. But the system can learn, and that learning can transform our future experience.

## We don't need to decide everything

Let me suggest something.

Imagine having to decide consciously, every morning, every detail of your behaviour.

How to walk. How to use cutlery. How to pronounce every word. How to interpret every gesture. How to respond to every sound.

You probably wouldn't get out of the house before dinnertime.

Our ability to develop automatic responses is an extraordinary advantage. The problem is not that habits exist. The problem arises when some habits cause suffering and continue operating in ways we don't know how to recognise or change.

That's why I think we should be careful with a fairly common idea about meditation: that a highly trained person should perform every action through completely conscious decisions.

That doesn't strike me as a particularly reasonable—or necessary—goal.

Perhaps an important part of training is precisely to develop healthy habits that don't need constant supervision.

Imagine that, initially, you have to remind yourself deliberately to listen to others without interrupting. You make an effort. You get it wrong. You recognise your mistake. And you try again.

Over time, listening may become a much more natural response. You no longer need to remind yourself constantly. You simply listen.

And perhaps something similar can happen with patience, generosity, or the ability to recognise some of our reactions.

Not because we've stopped being conditioned, but because we've transformed some of the conditions that influence our behaviour.

## Training our tendencies

There's something I consider particularly important.

When we meditate, we're not developing only a skill to use while sitting still. We may be training tendencies that influence our everyday lives.

Think of a distraction. During meditation, a thought appears. We notice it and return to the breath. We repeat the process many times.

But what are we really learning?

Among other things, perhaps we're developing a greater ability to notice when our attention has been carried away by some content.

Now imagine that we're having a conversation. Someone says something upsetting. A defensive thought appears, and our attention begins to get caught up in it.

Perhaps the capacity we've trained in meditation helps us notice sooner. There's no guarantee, but it's a possibility worth investigating.

Something similar may happen with other qualities. Practising *mettā* can help develop responses of goodwill. Concentration may favour stability. Investigating experience can help us recognise interpretations and reactions.

From our model's perspective, these practices are ways of changing the system's tendencies. Not because every session necessarily produces a profound change, but because repetition, context, and learning can contribute to new capacities.

## A more available mind

I'd like to introduce an idea that I find particularly useful.

When we talk about a trained mind, perhaps we shouldn't imagine a mind that never experiences intense emotions. Nor one incapable of becoming distracted.

Perhaps it is more useful to talk about *availability*—being able to respond to what is happening.

Imagine walking peacefully when you hear a loud noise. Your body may react. Perhaps you startle.

That doesn't necessarily mean you've lost all ability to pay attention. You may quickly recognise what happened, orient yourself, and respond appropriately.

But if you become trapped in an intense reaction, it may take longer to recover a clear understanding of the situation.

We can investigate how attention, equanimity, and mental stability affect that availability.

We don't need to claim that a trained person is immune to being startled or can never become overwhelmed by an emotion. But perhaps we can develop a greater capacity to regain stability and respond to events.

In our model, this could be understood as a tendency of the system to maintain or recover a functional organisation that favours clarity.

A kind of balance—not a motionless state, but a dynamic adaptation to circumstances.

Perhaps that's more helpful than imagining enlightenment as a mind permanently frozen into a serene facial expression.

## Attention can get things wrong, too

We need to remember something important.

Paying close attention doesn't guarantee that we understand correctly. We can concentrate intensely on a mistaken interpretation. We can observe our emotions and attribute causes to them that we don't actually know. And we can become very confident in our introspective abilities without recognising our own expectations.

In earlier chapters, we talked about autosuggestion. Our experience can be influenced by what we expect to find.

If we're convinced that a certain sensation means spiritual progress, we may pay particular attention to anything that confirms this interpretation and overlook what contradicts it.

Attention doesn't automatically remove our biases. Sometimes it can even reinforce them when guided by rigid expectations.

That's why we need to complement concentration with investigation and critical openness. And perhaps with something we learned in our first training: love.

When we don't need to prove we're right, recognising our mistakes becomes easier. And when we stop punishing ourselves for being wrong, we can investigate more honestly.

The three trainings meet again.

## When the self tries to take charge of everything

Here's a situation I know rather well.

You discover a habit you dislike. You decide to change it. And a new image of yourself appears: a person who shouldn't still have that habit.

You begin monitoring yourself, analysing every reaction, checking whether you're making progress, and getting angry when you discover you haven't transformed it yet.

Curiously, you may end up developing a new habit of surveillance, control, and judgement.

You tried to solve one problem. And built another.

Our minds have an admirable talent for this sort of thing.

But perhaps we can do it differently. We can recognise a habit, investigate its conditions, introduce new responses, and allow learning to unfold gradually.

We don't need to consciously control every process or constantly supervise every thought.

In fact, perhaps an important part of practice is learning to trust the capacities we've developed. Not through blind faith, but because we've trained responses that can arise more and more naturally.

We don't need to be permanently in charge of our minds to learn how to care for them.

## A small experiment: observing a habit

Let's investigate something ordinary.

Choose a simple habit. Perhaps you check your phone too often, interrupt other people, eat when you're bored, or catch yourself thinking about work throughout your free time.

It doesn't have to be especially harmful. The idea is to observe how it works.

For a few days, try to recognise three elements.

**The situation.** What was happening before the habit appeared? Were you tired, bored, worried? Had something happened that favoured that response?

**The intention.** What did you seem to be trying to achieve? Obtain a pleasant experience? Remove an unpleasant sensation? Resolve a worry? Avoid something?

**The consequence.** What happened afterwards? Did you get what you wanted? Was there relief? Did it last? Did your response make the habit more likely to appear again?

We don't need to identify every cause. We probably couldn't. We're simply investigating a few relationships.

Then try something different. The next time similar conditions arise, make one small change.

If you check your phone when bored, perhaps you can spend a few seconds recognising the boredom first. If you interrupt someone, perhaps you can practise listening until they've finished. If you're tired and irritation appears, perhaps you can rest before continuing a difficult conversation.

Don't look for an extraordinary result. Simply observe what changes.

And remember: the same response won't necessarily work every time. We're studying a complex system, not programming a machine that will obey any instruction.

## Absolute control is not the goal

At this point, you may be wondering what it really means to develop a trained mind.

Being able to direct our attention at all times? Recognising every intention before acting? Eliminating all automatic responses?

I don't think we need to frame it like that. In fact, I suspect trying to control absolutely all our mental processes would be extraordinarily exhausting. And perhaps impossible.

Our minds continue operating through innumerable processes that we don't consciously control. Some are essential to life. Others are useful. And some can cause difficulties.

Training doesn't need to eliminate that complexity. It can help us change our relationship with it: recognise certain responses, develop healthy habits, reduce harmful patterns, and expand our ability to act with clarity and compassion.

In our model, we might say we're trying to transform the system's tendencies, not replace it with a perfect controller.

And here's an idea I find especially beautiful.

Perhaps a wise mind isn't one that must consciously decide every right response, but one in which wise responses have become more natural.

That doesn't mean wisdom can be reduced entirely to automatic habits. Deep understanding, discernment, and the ability to respond to new situations remain essential.

But it does invite us to reconsider what we're trying to develop.

Not permanent surveillance. A transformation of our capacities.

## Learning to trust what we've learned

When we learn to ride a bicycle, at first we have to pay close attention. It's hard to keep our balance. We watch the pedals. We worry about falling. And we probably end up on the ground a few times.

But after practising, something changes. We no longer have to think constantly about staying upright. Balance becomes much more natural.

That doesn't mean we're incapable of falling. It means we've developed a capacity we didn't have before, one we can use without analysing every movement.

Perhaps training the mind has something in common with this kind of learning.

At first, we need to remember our intentions, notice distractions, practise new responses, and try again many times.

Over time, some capacities may become integrated into our ordinary way of living. We can listen better, notice certain emotions sooner, regain stability more easily, and respond more kindly in situations we used to find difficult.

Not because we've become perfect beings. But because we've learned.

And perhaps that learning is one of the most practical expressions of wisdom.

After all, we don't want to spend our whole lives studying how to ride a bicycle. We want to learn to ride one—and then go out and enjoy the journey.

The point isn't to watch every movement of our minds forever. It's to learn to live in a way that requires us to struggle against them less and less.

And perhaps, when some of our healthiest responses begin to arise naturally, we'll discover that training has started to fulfil its purpose.

In the next chapter, we'll return to something we've mentioned many times: the body, emotions, and pleasure. Our experience doesn't happen only in our thoughts, and understanding that relationship may open new possibilities for living better.
