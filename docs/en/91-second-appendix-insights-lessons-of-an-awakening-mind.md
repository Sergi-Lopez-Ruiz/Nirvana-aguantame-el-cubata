# Second Appendix — Insights: Lessons from a Mind That Is Awakening

*Notes for “Nirvana? Hold My Beer!” — October 31, 2026*

> **Status of this text:** a personal model under development. It combines reported experiences, later interpretations, and hypotheses that still need to be tested. It is not intended as a scientifically proven account of awakening or a universal checklist of requirements.

## 1. What exactly is an insight?

For a long time I associated *insights* with revelatory experiences: moments when something suddenly makes sense and changes the way we see things. Now I think we should be more precise.

**An insight would be an understanding—sometimes sudden, sometimes gradual—of how experience or the mind works.** But understanding and its consequences are not the same thing.

We can distinguish four levels:

1. **Observation.** I notice a phenomenon: anger arises before I have decided to become angry.
2. **Understanding.** I recognize a pattern: the reaction arises from causes and conditions, not from a separate “self” that deliberately manufactures it.
3. **Learning.** That understanding changes the system's expectations and responses. When another reaction arises, there may be less identification or resistance.
4. **Habit or trait.** The change gradually becomes spontaneous and may persist outside meditation, even under less favorable conditions.

An intense experience can bring about an understanding without transforming habits. An intellectual understanding can be correct without reaching our automatic responses. And learning can become profound without ever being accompanied by a spectacular experience.

That is why I propose reserving **insight** for the understanding itself, and calling the process through which the system's functioning changes **integration** or **reconditioning**. This does not make insights less valuable; it helps explain *how* they might be valuable.

In systems language:

**Experience → observation → new model → revision of expectations → new responses → consolidation (or not).**

A word of caution: talking about “structural changes” is useful as a metaphor, but it does not demonstrate that a specific anatomical change has occurred in the brain. We can speak more accurately of functional changes and learning; their stability is an empirical question.

## 2. Culadasa and the sub-minds: do they have lives of their own?

In *The Mind Illuminated*, Culadasa presents the mind as a system of numerous **sub-minds**, or relatively autonomous processes. They process information in parallel, have specialized functions, and can generate competing intentions. In his model, consciousness acts as a space in which information is shared and coordination is facilitated. Unification of mind reduces conflict between these impulses.

Here I see a difference in language—and perhaps in the level of explanation—between his model and ours:

| Culadasa (simplified) | Our proposed functional model |
|---|---|
| Sub-minds with functions, goals and intentions | Conditioned processes with tendencies and mechanisms for selecting responses |
| Sub-minds that cooperate or compete | Loops and processes that reinforce, inhibit or conflict with one another |
| Consciousness facilitates their coordination | Conscious information can alter interactions between processes |
| Unification of mind | Less conflict between tendencies; greater stability and availability of attention |

Must we consider each sub-mind to have a “life,” a will or a point of view of its own? **It is not necessary in order to use the model.** Speaking of the intentions of a sub-mind may be a functional abstraction, just as we speak of a thermostat's “goal” without attributing a tiny inner self to it.

But we should not oversimplify either: if our model denies the **relative autonomy, specialization and competition of processes**, it would lose explanatory power. A possible point of convergence is this:

> The mind is not a single agent that produces intentions; it is an organization of processes that produce, negotiate and enact tendencies. Intentions are events within the system, not evidence of a separate owner.

In this way, we can make use of the richness of Culadasa's model without turning its sub-minds into metaphysical entities. We should also accept that **neither model is a definitive description of the brain**.

## 3. Is my current calm “samatha on steroids”?

In recent days I have recognized a calm I had experienced before. The subjective difference is that it now seems more readily available, less dependent on sitting in meditation.

I would not describe it simply as pleasure, or as the total absence of conflict. A different description fits better:

**“My mind isn't jumping around, searching for something to latch on to.”**

When something unpleasant arises, I recognize it quickly as unpleasantness. It does not seem necessary to judge the reaction or fight its presence. I can choose not to act on its impulse. And, at least for now, that acceptance feels spontaneous.

One hypothesis is that **two mechanisms** are combining:

- **Samatha:** the attention and stability I have cultivated allow the mind to settle.
- **Integrated insights:** certain habitual interpretations—“this shouldn't happen,” “this defines me,” “I need to resolve this to be okay”—may no longer generate so much agitation.

If there are fewer internal disturbances that need suppressing, calm might be maintained with less effort. “*Samatha on steroids*” would be a colloquial image, **not a technical category or evidence of a meditative attainment**.

This could generate a feedback dynamic:

**More equanimity → less reactivity to *vedanā* → less *taṇhā* → less agitation → more favorable conditions for equanimity.**

The direction of causation, the relative importance of each element, and the stability of the process remain open questions. Even the impression that *vedanā* has “become neutral” could mean different things: that its hedonic tone is less intense, that reactions to it have diminished, or both.

There is a biographical detail I do not want to erase or dramatize: **I remember intense calm before my first episode of mood destabilization**. I now notice differences, and I feel calm and aware of this. Both observations matter. The similarity warrants caution, but does not prove that the earlier situation is repeating itself; nor does it make my present state a guarantee of attainment. Maintaining sleep and routines, avoiding abrupt increases in practice, and watching for a reduced need for sleep, unusual acceleration, grandiosity or impulsivity are sensible precautions. If those signs appear, it is wise to speak promptly with a healthcare professional and someone close to me.

## 4. A map of the insights I have described

This is a **provisional reconstruction** of understandings I have spoken about over time. Not all came as sudden revelations; some developed over months or were reinterpreted afterwards. Dates indicate when I described them, not necessarily when they occurred.

### A. The body, emotions and attention

**1. The body participates in the creation of emotions.**

By learning to access *pīti*, and later to evoke it voluntarily, I understood how profoundly a bodily state can transform emotional experience even when nothing relevant has changed outside. Emotions are not merely abstract narratives. The body plays a constitutive role in how they feel.

*Related observation:* being able to evoke *pīti* is a trained ability or state, not in itself a liberating insight.

**2. Bodily reactions can precede conscious explanations.**

Sometimes an emotion seems to begin as a physical reaction that we then interpret, label and turn into a story. Attending to the body makes it possible to recognize part of that process before being swept away by the interpretation.

**3. Calm and concentration change which processes can be observed.**

*Samatha* practice makes sensations, changes and patterns visible that would normally be obscured by discursive activity. It is not necessarily that a new reality appears; the resolution of observation may simply improve.

**4. Equanimity can be learned too.**

I came to understand that equanimity need not be an essence or a special property of a self: it can be understood as a conditioned response that can be cultivated. I extended that intuition to other attitudes, such as loving-kindness and compassion.

### B. The self, intentions and conditioning

**5. I cannot find an independent “self” behind experience.**

During an early understanding, associated with my first episode of destabilization, I could not find a separate owner of what was happening. There were causes and conditions. Over time, I became better at distinguishing a powerful experience of selflessness from an integrated understanding of how the self functions.

**6. The “self” exists as a model, not necessarily as an owner.**

Identity, personal history and self-image have real functions. The mistake arises when I reify this collection of processes and treat it as an independent entity that must direct everything.

**7. I can love the self without always obeying it.**

For years I used images such as the **tiger** and the **protective older brother**. The ego, or learned character, does not have to be destroyed or hated: it did what it could to protect me. It can be understood, thanked and given a less central place. What once seemed an enemy can become an ally.

**8. The center of experience and identity are not the same thing.**

I gradually distinguished bodily perception, the point of view from which experience seems to be organized, and narrative identification. All three can vary. The feeling of “being here, at the center” does not demonstrate the existence of an independent observer.

**9. Peace arises when I stop claiming everything as mine.**

I described this as no longer “taking ownership” of experience: when the self stops claiming center stage, certain tensions dissolve. We do not need to erase the functional self for it to stop dominating our interpretation.

**10. Intentions are conditioned too.**

The most radical formulation was: **“Actions are conditioned reactions.”** Thoughts, emotions and intentions themselves also emerge from a history of causes and conditions. Conscious decision-making need not be understood as the intervention of an entity outside the system.

**11. *Saṅkhāras* are conditioned constructions; we cannot live without constructing.**

The mind creates models, categories, expectations and narratives. The aim would not be to abolish all mental construction—something our model does not even consider compatible with everyday life—but to understand which constructions are useful and which fuel unnecessary suffering.

**12. Conditioning can itself be reconditioned.**

If habits are learned, they can also be changed. The path need not be a struggle of the self against the mind, but the cultivation of conditions that weaken some tendencies and strengthen others.

### C. Impermanence, emptiness and the limits of models

**13. Impermanence is not an exception: it is how processes work.**

Things we treat as fixed entities change. Even “life,” “death” and “person” may be useful names we give to processes. Reifying them creates some of the tensions associated with *anicca*, *anattā* and *dukkha*.

**14. Suffering grows when I turn expectations into demands.**

It is not only that things change; the conflict between what happens and what my mind demands should happen matters too. Recognizing that mechanism creates some distance from reactivity.

**15. Experience does not need a metaphysical center.**

For years I sought different foundations: the self, a center of observation, emptiness as an essence, or non-duality as ultimate reality. More recently I have seen that perhaps none of those foundations is necessary. **There is experience, and that is enough** to begin investigating.

**16. Non-dual experiences are conditioned too.**

They may be profound, transformative and useful, but having a non-dual experience does not in itself demonstrate the existence of a non-dual metaphysical reality. I therefore reconsidered an interpretation that had previously convinced me. I jokingly called it “the death of the magic.”

**17. Emptiness can be understood as interdependence without turning it into a substance.**

Seeing that we cannot find a separate essence does not mean discovering a new thing called “Emptiness” underneath everything. The two truths help distinguish the usefulness of everyday language from the absence of independent identities.

**18. Even the model of the conditioned mind is conditioned.**

It is another way of looking at experience, not a guarantee of absolute certainty. Wisdom should include the ability to revise its own formulations. **An ever-decreasing need for little, definitive certainties.**

### D. Fear, compassion and conduct

**19. Understanding a fear can reveal what it was protecting.**

During an episode of panic, I described the symbolic transformation of “monsters” into jewels. Behind my fear of losing my children, the love I felt for them became clear. Understanding the source of a reaction allowed me to thank it instead of fighting it.

**20. Forgiveness and gratitude can replace the inner war.**

By viewing my responses as conditioned, I have been able to look at certain mistakes and defense mechanisms with greater compassion. This does not erase responsibility for their consequences; it allows me to accept that responsibility without adding self-hatred.

**21. People are far more complex systems than their visible behaviors.**

When looking at a child whose behavior causes conflict, or recognizing cruel group dynamics, I became more keenly aware of how personal history and suffering may lie behind reactions. Understanding does not mean approving every behavior or giving up boundaries. It can mean responding with firmness and compassion at the same time.

**22. Wisdom can express itself as love in action.**

I have gradually brought ethics (*sīla*), care and the understanding of conditioning closer together. I am not interested only in being someone who considers themselves “good,” but in acting in ways that reduce actual suffering. Hence my phrase: **“The world needs fewer people who think they're good, and more people who do good.”**

### E. Seeking, equanimity and wisdom becoming spontaneous

**23. *Taṇhā* may begin before explicit desire: in the feeling of insufficiency.**

Thirst would not simply mean wanting something; it could be the constant impulse to find whatever would make present experience sufficient. Temporary relief sometimes feeds a new search. This is a functional hypothesis about *taṇhā*, not a settled doctrinal equivalence.

**24. *Vedanā* can arise without having to govern our response.**

I now observe unpleasantness arising and recognize it very quickly. It is possible to feel the signal without concluding that I must act on its behalf. Recent experience does not determine whether there has been a real neutralization of hedonic tone or, above all, less reactivity to it.

**25. Acceptance can shift from conscious effort to a spontaneous response.**

Lately I do not feel that I have to persuade myself to accept every reaction. I recognize it as conditioned, do not condemn it for appearing, and can refrain from following its impulse. This suggests that equanimity may have become, at least in some circumstances, a more habitual response.

**26. Ignorance that is recognized may cease to direct the system.**

A mistaken interpretation need not stop arising altogether for it to lose its power. If a reaction or narrative is recognized as a conditioned construction, it may cease to receive automatic authority. **Recognizing ignorance is already an expression of wisdom.**

**27. Wisdom itself must recognize its fallibility.**

Even the most refined understanding can become another identity: “Now I really have understood.” Further learning would involve recognizing even that tendency without needing final certainty. The mind need not defend its model as though the model were the mind itself.

## 5. Other important hypotheses I would not yet call consolidated insights

We should not confuse the understandings above with proposals we are still exploring:

- **Autosuggestion and external suggestion.** Mantras, hypnosis, dreams and internal narratives may share mechanisms through which expectations and experience are influenced. The hypothesis that autosuggestion is a particularly important mechanism in perpetuating suffering deserves investigation, but it is not demonstrated by the experiences described.
- **Lucid dreaming and metacognition.** Recognizing “I am dreaming” offers an intriguing analogy with recognizing “I am believing my own construction.” The analogy does not prove that the mechanisms are identical.
- **The threshold of equanimity.** Perhaps a sufficiently trained system reaches a mode of functioning in which it stops constantly feeding the search. We do not know whether a clear threshold exists or a gradual transition, or how long it would last.
- ***Nirvāṇa* as functionally liberated functioning.** We might attempt to describe it through the absence of certain mechanisms of suffering, without deciding in advance what “unconditioned” means metaphysically. This is our interpretation, not the definitive translation of the traditional concept.

## 6. Are these the “necessary” lessons for an awakened mind?

Perhaps we should change just one word: not necessarily **necessary**, but **candidate lessons**. We have no evidence that every person must undergo these same experiences, in this order and with the same metaphors. Indeed, if systems differ, it is reasonable to expect different trajectories.

Even so, the collection allows us to propose a functional map:

| Capacity being cultivated | Possible learning | Observable change to look for |
|---|---|---|
| **Sensitivity** | Noticing the body, *vedanā* and early reactions | Recognizing discomfort and impulses sooner |
| **Causal understanding** | Seeing thoughts, intentions and emotions as conditioned | Less automatic attribution to a self that owns them |
| **Disidentification** | Understanding the self as a useful model | Less need to defend each personal story |
| **Equanimity** | Allowing sensations and reactions without rejecting them | Less secondary struggle and less impulsivity |
| **Non-attachment** | Seeing the thirst for experience to be different | Less compulsive seeking of relief |
| **Compassion** | Understanding our own and others' conditioning | Clearer boundaries with less cruelty |
| **Metacognition** | Recognizing that our own interpretations are constructions | Greater capacity for revision and correction |
| **Integration** | Turning understanding into habitual responses | Changes that persist in everyday life |

We are not proposing a compulsory ladder. These capacities interact and reinforce one another: attention facilitates understanding, understanding supports equanimity, equanimity supports attention, and compassion helps us avoid turning mistakes into new causes of suffering.

The decisive question would not be: “Have I had the right insights?” It would be:

**Which processes continue to generate suffering, what feeds them, and what learning allows them to stop being fed?**

I would add another, no less important question: **how is all this reflected in my behavior towards others, especially when I am tired, frustrated or under pressure?** Feeling peaceful does not guarantee acting well; this is where the model meets real life.

## 7. State, learning and prudence

To study this proposal, we need to separate three variables that are often confused:

- **State:** how much calm, clarity, pleasure or equanimity I feel right now.
- **Learning:** what I have understood and which new responses are available.
- **Stability:** how much the change lasts over time, outside practice and when circumstances change.

We can note, for instance, whether I am sleeping normally, have my usual everyday energy, can interrupt a train of thought without difficulty, recognize disagreement, keep my commitments and respond to others more carefully. Not as a spiritual test or obsessive monitoring, but as a way of avoiding the confusion of intensity with integration.

With a history of episodes of mood disturbance, **less sleep without tiredness, racing thoughts, unusual impulses, a sense of mission or extraordinary expansiveness** deserve early attention, even when accompanied by well-being. Practice can continue in moderation; intense states are not a reason to rapidly increase meditation, alter treatment or make major decisions. If warning signs arise, contacting a healthcare professional is sensible.

None of this invalidates the value of learning or forces us to interpret every calm state as a problem. It simply prevents subjective experience from being treated as proof of stability, diagnosis or awakening.

## 8. A possible provisional definition of an awakened mind

> **An awakened mind might be a conditioned system that has learned to recognize its own constructions and reactions, not to grant them automatic authority, and not to needlessly feed processes that produce suffering; a system whose functioning becomes progressively more attentive, equanimous, compassionate and capable of correcting itself.**

The sentence is not meant to demonstrate that this is the canonical definition of awakening. It merely expresses our research program.

If insights are understandings and understandings can become learning, then the path would not necessarily consist of collecting extraordinary experiences. It would consist of **transforming the mind's habitual way of functioning**.

Perhaps the current calm is, in part, a consequence of old mechanisms finding less and less fuel. Perhaps it is a particularly favorable state that will change. Or perhaps both.

We do not need to decide today.

**We shall see.**
