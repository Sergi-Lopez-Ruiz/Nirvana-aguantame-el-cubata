# 28. Theravāda, Mahāyāna, and Vajrayāna: Different Paths, Possible Meetings

Imagine you're trying to reach the other side of a river.

You've spent a long time studying how to cross it.

You've watched the current.

Learned to build a raft.

And finally found a way across.

But when you reach the other side, you discover something surprising.

Other people have taken different routes.

Some have built other kinds of boats.

Others have followed the river until they found a bridge.

And some insist the most important thing wasn't crossing at all, but understanding the nature of the river.

Of course, there are also people who insist their boat is the only authentic one.

And perhaps someone is selling an advanced raft-building course for the modest price of three thousand euros.

Welcome to Buddhist traditions.

For more than two thousand years, different communities have studied the Buddha's teachings.

They have developed practices.

Philosophical systems.

Rituals.

And extraordinarily diverse ways of understanding liberation.

Some appear complementary.

Others present important differences.

And certain claims can be difficult to reconcile.

But I want to ask you a question.

What if we could learn from different traditions without needing to prove that they all say exactly the same thing?

Let's investigate.

## Three Major Families

To begin, we can distinguish three major families of Buddhism.

Theravāda, often translated as “Teaching of the Elders.”

Mahāyāna, “Great Vehicle.”

And Vajrayāna, “Diamond Vehicle” or “Vajra Vehicle.”

This classification is useful, but it's also a simplification.

Theravāda is a particular historical tradition that preserves the Pali Canon.

Mahāyāna includes numerous philosophical and contemplative schools, including Zen, with considerable differences among them.

And Vajrayāna developed within the doctrinal horizon of Mahāyāna, incorporating specific tantric methods.

So we're not talking about three completely independent systems.

Or three stages everyone must pass through in succession.

Historically, these traditions have developed through long and complex processes.

They've shared teachings.

Debated.

Incorporated new texts and practices.

And influenced one another.

We should remember something else.

Buddhist teachings haven't been transmitted over twenty-five hundred years as though someone locked a document in a safe and nobody touched it again.

There have been interpretations, debates, translations, and doctrinal developments.

That doesn't mean every teaching is arbitrary.

But it does require us to distinguish history, tradition, and interpretation.

And perhaps that distinction is especially important for our project.

Because we're not trying to find an institution to tell us what to believe.

We want to understand.

## Theravāda: Understanding Suffering and Its Causes

My own path has been particularly influenced by teachings associated with early Buddhism and Theravāda.

The Four Noble Truths.

Dependent origination.

The five aggregates.

The Noble Eightfold Path.

The jhānas.

And different ways of training attention and understanding.

All these provided extraordinarily useful tools for investigating my experience.

In Theravāda, we find an important emphasis on understanding suffering and extinguishing its causes.

The ideal of the arahant occupies a central place.

A person who has completed liberation through the extinction of greed, aversion, and ignorance.

We also find a highly developed monastic tradition, practices of concentration and investigation, and a vast body of teachings on ethical conduct.

But I want to avoid a common oversimplification.

Theravāda is sometimes presented as an individualistic path.

As though its practitioners were only interested in saving themselves.

That doesn't seem fair to me.

Generosity, compassion, goodwill, and ethical living are fundamental parts of this tradition.

We don't need to pit two caricatures against each other.

A selfish Theravāda.

And a compassionate Mahāyāna.

The actual differences are much more interesting.

What did I learn from this perspective?

Perhaps most importantly, I learned that suffering could be investigated.

I didn't need simply to accept it as an inevitable feature of my personality.

I could observe the conditions feeding it.

My interpretations.

My reactions.

My desires.

And the way certain dynamics reinforced one another.

This perspective fit extraordinarily well with my background as an engineer.

When a system has a problem, we try to understand its causes.

It's not enough just to complain.

We investigate.

And perhaps find ways to change what maintains the problem.

Of course, the human mind is much more complex than a machine.

But the approach felt familiar to me.

Understanding suffering through its causes and conditions was one of early Buddhism's most important contributions to my own path.

Although, later, other questions began to arise.

## Mahāyāna: Broadening the View

Mahāyāna introduces an extraordinarily wide range of teachings and practices.

Among its best-known characteristics is the bodhisattva ideal.

A person oriented toward complete awakening for the benefit of all beings.

Here an especially important word appears.

Bodhicitta.

We can approach it as the aspiration to awaken for the benefit of others.

But it isn't simply being kind.

It includes a profound commitment to the liberation of all beings, together with the cultivation of wisdom and compassion.

And I must admit I find this idea especially beautiful.

Because it raises a fundamental question.

Why do we want to develop wisdom?

Only to suffer less ourselves?

Or also to help reduce the suffering of those around us?

From our model's perspective, these possibilities need not exclude each other.

Learning to care for ourselves can help us care for others better.

And caring for others can also transform some of our tendencies.

Love and wisdom can support one another.

But there's an important nuance.

We shouldn't reduce traditional bodhicitta to a simple psychological intention to do good.

In Mahāyāna, it's part of a much broader liberating ideal.

Our interpretation can draw inspiration from it.

But not replace it entirely.

## The Bodhisattva and the Arahant

Here we find an important doctrinal difference.

Theravāda gives a central place to the arahant ideal.

Mahāyāna emphasizes the bodhisattva path toward complete Buddhahood.

It isn't simply that one group seeks liberation while the other seeks to help.

The traditional debate includes questions about the nature and scope of realization, the capacities of a Buddha, and the relationship between wisdom and compassionate activity.

In some Mahāyāna interpretations, arahantship is regarded as a realization that doesn't exhaust all the possibilities of Buddhahood.

From certain Theravāda perspectives, that interpretation isn't accepted.

So there are real differences.

We don't need to erase them.

But perhaps we can ask a useful question from our model's perspective.

If a person deeply reduces the dynamics generating their own suffering, how might that affect their relationship with others?

And if they actively cultivate compassion for other beings, how might that transform their own tendencies?

Perhaps we'll find a mutually beneficial relationship.

But we shouldn't conclude that both traditional ideals are identical.

We can recognize connections without eliminating differences.

And perhaps that's the beginning of our bridge.

## Emptiness: When We Begin Questioning Our Explanations

One of Mahāyāna's most important philosophical developments is its reflection on emptiness.

Śūnyatā.

Especially in the Madhyamaka tradition associated with Nāgārjuna.

Emptiness concerns the absence of inherent or independent existence in phenomena.

And here there's something I consider fundamental.

Emptiness doesn't mean nothing exists.

Nor does it mean everything is an illusion without consequences.

We can recognize that phenomena depend on conditions and lack an independent essence without denying their conventional existence.

This connects deeply with dependent origination.

Things arise through relationships.

And we needn't attribute to them a self-sufficient nature.

But I have something to admit.

For quite some time, some of my interpretations of emptiness were connected to nondual experiences.

In certain meditative states, the familiar separation between observer and observed could change profoundly.

And it was tempting to interpret that as a revelation of reality's true nature.

But over time I began to question that conclusion.

Why should an extraordinary experience demonstrate a metaphysical truth?

Couldn't it be a conditioned experience too?

And, especially, do we need to find an ultimate reality in the form of a substance or universal consciousness in order to understand emptiness?

From Nāgārjuna's perspective, emptiness itself mustn't become an independent essence.

And that seems extraordinarily important to me.

Because our minds have an admirable skill for turning any explanation into a new thing to cling to.

Even emptiness.

We can begin by saying that phenomena lack essence.

And end up attributing an absolute essence to emptiness.

We've managed to build a new metaphysics while explaining why we didn't need one.

Quite ingenious.

That's why I think this tradition has an especially valuable lesson for us.

It's not enough to question our ideas about the self. We also have to learn to question our ideas about what we think we've discovered once we've stopped looking for it.

## The Two Truths

In Madhyamaka, we encounter an especially important distinction.

Conventional truth.

And ultimate truth.

We shouldn't imagine them simply as two separate worlds.

One false.

And one true.

Conventional truth allows us to talk about people, actions, causes, responsibilities, and relationships.

Ultimate investigation questions whether these phenomena possess an inherent, independent nature.

The relationship between the two has received various interpretations.

But perhaps we can draw a useful lesson.

We can recognize that a person has no independent essence and still treat them as a real person in the conventional world.

We can understand that our intentions are conditioned and still take responsibility for our actions.

And we can recognize that our interpretations are constructions without concluding that every interpretation has equal value.

Our model also operates on a conventional level.

It proposes relationships.

Processes.

And functional explanations.

It shouldn't present itself as a definitive description of ultimate reality.

And here I find an important connection.

Perhaps one danger of constructing models is forgetting that they're constructions too.

Tools for understanding.

Not eternal substances hidden behind phenomena.

## Yogācāra: Investigating How We Construct Experience

Another major philosophical tradition within Mahāyāna is Yogācāra.

Its teachings include developments concerning consciousness, perception, and the ways our experience is conditioned.

We encounter concepts such as cittamātra, often translated as “mind-only,” and ālayavijñāna, storehouse consciousness.

These ideas have received different interpretations and generated complex philosophical debates.

We shouldn't simplify them by saying Yogācāra merely claims that the external world doesn't exist.

But they're especially interesting for our project for one reason.

They invite us to investigate how experience depends on processes of perception, interpretation, and conditioning.

And here there's a connection with something we've repeatedly explored.

Our experience isn't a transparent copy of reality.

It's influenced by our expectations.

Our habits.

Our memory.

And numerous processes we don't directly recognize.

This doesn't prove Yogācāra's doctrinal claims.

But it does allow an interesting dialogue with contemporary models of cognition.

And especially with our investigation of autosuggestion.

Because perhaps certain mental constructions don't merely interpret our experience.

They also help shape what we experience next.

But we have to distinguish a psychological hypothesis from a metaphysical claim.

That our perception is conditioned doesn't prove the entire universe is a creation of our mind.

We can learn from Yogācāra without immediately resolving all its philosophical controversies.

## Buddha-Nature: A Beautiful and Dangerous Idea

Another concept appears in various Mahāyāna currents.

Tathāgatagarbha.

Often associated with Buddha-nature.

Some explanations use metaphors of something pure hidden beneath impurities.

Like a jewel covered in mud.

Or a mirror that needs cleaning.

I understand the appeal of these images.

They can express confidence in our capacity for transformation.

And perhaps help people who believe their minds are fundamentally damaged.

But they can also lead to problematic interpretations.

We can imagine a pure, permanent, independent essence hidden inside us.

A sort of Buddhist soul.

And here our model encounters difficulties.

If we see the mind as a conditioned system, do we need to posit a pure mental substance to explain the possibility of liberation?

Not necessarily.

We can interpret some teachings on Buddha-nature as statements about the potential for awakening.

But that doesn't exhaust their traditional interpretations.

There are important debates about their ontological meaning and relationship with emptiness.

We shouldn't pretend they're settled.

Personally, I find it more useful to think that we can develop transformative capacities without imagining an immutable essence.

But that's my interpretation.

Not a definitive translation of every teaching on Buddha-nature.

And perhaps acknowledging that is precisely a way of keeping our investigation open.

## Zen: When Thinking Isn't Enough

There's another Mahāyāna tradition I particularly want to mention.

Zen.

And I have to admit that there's something in its way of teaching I find extraordinarily appealing.

Its insistence on practice.

On action.

On returning to what we're doing.

And, in certain schools, on using questions or situations that challenge our usual way of thinking.

Zen developed historically from Chinese Chan Buddhism and acquired particularly well-known forms in Japan.

It includes different schools, such as Sōtō and Rinzai, with differing methods and emphases.

Seated meditation, or zazen, has a fundamental place.

But Zen also values ordinary activities.

Walking.

Eating.

Working.

Cleaning.

And paying attention to what we're doing.

This connects with an idea we've repeated throughout the book.

The world isn't made better by the mind alone, but by our hands.

We can build extraordinary explanations of suffering.

But then we have to live.

Listen.

Respond.

And act.

In some Zen teachings, this connection between practice and everyday life is particularly important.

It isn't necessarily about abandoning thought.

But about recognizing that intellectual understanding can't replace experience or action.

And here we find another connection with our model.

We can understand perfectly how a habit works.

But that doesn't mean we've learned to transform it.

To learn to ride a bicycle, at some point we have to get on it.

Writing a treatise on balance isn't enough.

Although we'd probably sell more books that way.

**Kōans: when the mind runs out of answers.** There's another aspect of Zen that fascinates me.

Kōans.

In certain schools, especially Rinzai, stories, dialogues, and questions are used to challenge a student's usual conceptual frameworks.

One of the best-known examples is the question:

“What is the sound of one hand?”

Our mind tries to solve it.

Looks for an explanation.

A clever answer.

A philosophical interpretation.

But perhaps the question isn't designed for an ordinary conceptual response.

In traditional practice, working with a kōan may require intensive contemplative investigation and guidance from a teacher.

It's not simply solving a riddle.

Nor should we assume every kōan serves the same purpose.

But there's something I find extraordinarily interesting about them.

They can expose the limitations of the mental framework we're using to understand a situation.

And here I want to share a personal association.

Humor.

Especially the crude, unexpected, sometimes downright coarse humor that abruptly breaks our expectations.

Imagine someone starting a solemn speech.

Using profound words.

Building an atmosphere of immense importance.

And just when we expect a transcendent conclusion, delivering an utterly absurd response.

Perhaps even a remark so vulgar that keeping a straight face becomes impossible.

We laugh.

But what happened?

For a few seconds, the interpretation we were constructing stopped working.

We expected one thing.

Something else appeared.

And our system had to reorganize the meaning of what was happening very quickly.

Some humor works through precisely that incongruity.

The disruption of an expectation.

And perhaps, from a functional perspective, that has something in common with certain effects kōans can produce.

I'm not claiming that a kōan is simply a joke.

Or that a burst of laughter is equivalent to liberating insight.

Zen practice has a depth and context we can't reduce to a punchline.

But perhaps we can recognize a partially shared mechanism.

An unexpected interruption can reveal that we were interpreting experience through an assumed framework.

And, for a moment, that framework may lose its authority.

**What if humor could also wake us up from our interpretations?**

I want to take this speculation a little further.

Imagine someone completely caught up in an argument.

They defend a position.

Build arguments.

And begin identifying with the need to be right.

Then an unexpected remark appears.

A joke.

Perhaps even a ridiculously absurd response.

And both people start laughing.

For a few seconds, the argument loses its importance.

The interpretation that seemed so solid stops completely dominating experience.

And a possibility opens up to see the situation differently.

Of course, that doesn't always happen.

Humor can also humiliate, distract, or reinforce prejudice.

And a joke doesn't necessarily solve the problem that caused the conflict.

But perhaps, when appropriate, it can help interrupt certain dynamics of identification.

From our model's perspective, this is fascinating.

Because we've repeatedly discussed how interpretations can fuel reactions.

An expectation encourages an emotion.

The emotion reinforces the interpretation.

And our attention becomes trapped in that dynamic.

What happens if an unexpected intervention suddenly changes the interpretive framework?

Perhaps some of those relationships weaken.

Even if only temporarily.

We have no grounds to conclude that humor produces the same type of understanding as contemplative investigation of a kōan.

But I find the analogy useful.

And very much in keeping with the spirit of this book.

Because perhaps some of our deepest certainties occasionally need something more than another philosophical explanation.

Perhaps they need somebody to make us laugh at them.

Including our own.

**Breaking the framework isn't enough.** Here we need to add a warning.

An experience that breaks our usual interpretive framework doesn't guarantee that the next interpretation will be better.

We may abandon a mistaken belief.

And immediately replace it with something even more extravagant.

We can also experience something surprising and give it an unjustified meaning.

That's why I think we need to combine two abilities.

The flexibility to question our mental frameworks.

And the discernment required to evaluate what we construct afterward.

From our model's perspective, both can be fundamental.

We need to recognize when an interpretation fuels suffering.

But also investigate what happens when we stop using it.

Not every change of perspective implies wisdom.

And not every disorienting experience leads to profound understanding.

Even so, I find something very valuable in Zen.

Its ability to remind us that we can spend a tremendous amount of time trying to solve problems conceptually when they may require a different relationship with experience.

And its insistence on returning to practice.

To what we do.

To everyday life.

Perhaps one of the most useful lessons we can draw from this tradition is quite simple.

We can use concepts to understand, but also need to learn when to stop turning them over in our minds and begin to live.

## Vajrayāna: Working with What's Already Present

We come to a tradition that can be especially fascinating.

And also especially easy to misunderstand.

Vajrayāna is part of the Mahāyāna world, but incorporates specific tantric methods.

Among them are practices involving mantras.

Visualizations.

Meditative deities.

Rituals.

And various ways of working with energy, attention, and perception.

Within their own traditional frameworks, these practices have complex doctrinal foundations and often require initiation, transmission, and guidance from a qualified teacher.

We can't simply reduce them to psychological techniques.

But perhaps we can investigate some of their characteristics through our model.

In certain tantric practices, the meditator deliberately cultivates forms of perception, symbolism, and identification different from the usual ones.

I find that extraordinarily interesting.

Because so far we've investigated how our interpretations condition experience.

Tantra seems to explore, among many other things, the possibility of deliberately using imaginative, emotional, and symbolic processes within a contemplative path.

And here a fundamental question arises.

If our experience is deeply conditioned by our mental representations, can we use those representations to transform the conditions generating suffering?

Perhaps some tantric practices can be partially understood from this perspective.

But we must remember it's a functional interpretation.

Not a comprehensive explanation of Vajrayāna.

## Mantras and Suggestion

I want to pause now at something that has especially interested me along my path.

Mantras.

We find them in various religious and contemplative traditions.

In Vajrayāna, they have specific meanings depending on their ritual and doctrinal context.

But we can also investigate them functionally.

Repeating certain syllables or phrases may influence our attention.

Our breathing.

Our level of arousal.

And perhaps the expectations and emotions accompanying practice.

Here we find a connection with our investigation of suggestion.

If someone assigns extraordinary meaning to an experience, that meaning may influence how they interpret it and what they expect to experience next.

And those expectations can participate in subsequent experiences.

That doesn't mean every effect of mantras can be reduced to autosuggestion.

Or that traditional practices lack value because some of their components involve it.

Suggestion can be part of normal human processes of learning, perception, and emotional regulation.

But we must recognize its risks too.

Under certain circumstances, repetitive, intense, expectation-laden practice may increase absorption and subjective conviction in particular interpretations.

And if those interpretations become rigid, we may attribute meanings to our experiences that we haven't verified.

That's why I think we need to be particularly careful.

We can investigate.

But we don't need to automatically believe every explanation that appears during an extraordinary experience.

Meditation doesn't turn our interpretations into infallible truths.

And the more intense an experience is, the more important it may be to remember that distinction.

## Transforming Emotions or Stopping Feeding Them?

Here we find a particularly interesting difference in emphasis.

Various early Buddhist teachings pay great attention to abandoning unwholesome states and extinguishing dynamics of desire and aversion.

In certain tantric interpretations, by contrast, there is talk of transforming emotions or energies ordinarily considered obstacles.

That doesn't simply mean expressing any emotion without limits.

Nor justifying hostility, impulsiveness, or compulsive desire.

Traditional tantric practices are embedded in specific ethical and doctrinal frameworks.

But perhaps we can find a common question.

What do we do when an intense emotion arises?

Do we try to reduce the conditions feeding it?

Do we learn to recognize its impermanence?

Do we investigate its relationship with identification?

Or can we change how that energy participates in our experience?

From our model's perspective, these possibilities need not exclude one another.

An emotion can involve different processes.

Bodily sensations.

Interpretations.

Impulses.

And learned responses.

Perhaps we can learn to relate differently to some of them.

For example, intense bodily arousal doesn't necessarily have to become aggression.

We can recognize it.

Understand its context.

And develop a different response.

But we shouldn't conclude that every harmful emotion can be safely transformed through visualization or a meditative technique.

There's no universal guarantee.

And some intensive practices can be destabilizing.

Especially when they involve sleep disruption, demanding breathing exercises, or extraordinary interpretations of one's identity.

That's why I consider it important to distinguish conceptual exploration from advanced tantric practice.

The latter requires preparation, discernment, and proper guidance.

We don't have to experience everything we find fascinating.

## And What About Nonduality?

I need to pause here.

Because this is one of the subjects on which my understanding has changed the most.

For a long time, certain nondual experiences seemed extraordinarily important to me.

The usual distinction between observer and observed could disappear or change profoundly.

And it seemed to reveal something fundamental.

But over time I began asking a question.

What if that experience were conditioned too?

What if we didn't need to attribute independent metaphysical existence to it?

That doesn't mean the experience was false.

The experience happened.

What I began questioning was my interpretation.

In different Mahāyāna and Vajrayāna traditions, we find teachings about nonduality.

But their meanings aren't always equivalent.

They may concern the relationship between wisdom and appearance.

The absence of inherent separation between subject and object.

Or much more specific doctrinal insights.

We shouldn't reduce all these teachings to one particular perceptual experience.

Nor assume an experience of non-separation automatically proves a metaphysical claim.

From our model's perspective, we can investigate how representations of subject and object change.

But that doesn't definitively settle the nature of reality.

Perhaps the most important lesson I've drawn from my experience is something else.

We can learn a great deal from a nondual experience without turning it into an absolute description of the universe.

And perhaps that attitude is compatible with some traditional teachings.

Although it may also be in tension with interpretations making stronger claims.

We don't need to conceal that tension.

We can investigate it.

## Rigpa, Luminosity, and Primordial Mind

Some expressions appear particularly in certain Tibetan teachings.

Rigpa.

Luminosity.

Primordial mind.

Nature of mind.

These are complex concepts with specific meanings depending on school and context.

For example, rigpa has a central place in certain Dzogchen teachings.

It shouldn't simply be translated as “ordinary awareness” or automatically identified with an experience of tranquility.

But I admit some of these expressions pose a difficulty for me.

When I hear about a primordial mind, I can begin imagining a fundamental substance existing behind all conditioned processes.

And perhaps project ideas from my own cultural background onto these teachings.

A kind of absolute foundation.

Something that remains.

Something that ultimately resembles far too closely what I used to seek under other names.

But I need to be fair here.

Not all traditions that use these expressions posit an independent mental substance.

In some doctrinal frameworks, luminosity and emptiness are deeply connected.

And teachers insist precisely that what is recognized shouldn't be reified.

So I don't want to reject these teachings simply because certain expressions remind me of metaphysics I no longer need.

I want to understand what they're trying to point to.

And distinguish their traditional claims from my own associations.

Perhaps we'll find some interpretations compatible with our model.

Others perhaps not.

And some may require experiences and conceptual frameworks we don't yet understand sufficiently.

That's fine.

We're not obliged to settle every issue in Tibetan Buddhism before finishing this book.

Although I must admit that, given our track record, I wouldn't rule out our trying.

## The Teacher, Authority, and Discernment

Vajrayāna places particular importance on the relationship with a teacher.

In some contexts, that relationship includes commitments and devotional practices.

Traditionally, proper transmission is considered essential for certain methods.

We can understand why it matters.

Advanced practices can be difficult to interpret.

An experienced teacher can help contextualize phenomena the student doesn't understand.

And a relationship of trust may support learning.

But I want to introduce a warning.

Spiritual authority is conditioned too.

Teachers are people.

They can make mistakes.

They can misinterpret certain experiences.

And in some cases they can abuse their position.

That's why I think every teaching relationship should include clear ethical standards.

Respect.

Transparency.

Boundaries.

And the ability to question what we don't understand.

Any teaching that demands we completely abandon our critical judgment deserves special caution.

I'm not suggesting we should systematically distrust every tradition.

I simply believe wisdom shouldn't depend on giving up our ability to recognize harm.

And from our model's perspective, there's another reason.

Relationships of authority can profoundly influence our expectations and interpretations.

That's why we must care for the conditions we create around practice.

Especially when working with intense psychological experiences.

## The Bridge Metaphor

Now I want to return to an image that's been with me for some time.

Imagine a lake.

On one shore, people practice within one tradition.

On the other, people have developed different teachings and methods.

Each group sees the landscape from a different perspective.

And perhaps each has built maps that are useful from its own position.

Now imagine a bridge.

Not a bridge designed to prove both sides are identical.

Nor to force everyone to abandon their traditions.

A bridge that allows people to meet.

Listen.

Compare experiences.

And understand how different perspectives may illuminate different aspects of a problem.

Perhaps that's what I'd like to try to build with this book.

A way of studying Buddhist teachings through a functional model.

Not to replace them.

But to facilitate dialogue.

Theravāda can offer extraordinarily precise tools for investigating the dynamics of suffering.

Mahāyāna can broaden our understanding of compassion, emptiness, and the limitations of our interpretations.

Zen can remind us of the importance of practice, action, and questioning the conceptual frameworks we use to understand.

And Vajrayāna can invite us to investigate how certain symbolic, imaginative, and contemplative practices transform our experience.

These are illustrative emphases.

Not exclusive characteristics of each tradition.

They all contain much greater richness.

And important internal differences too.

But perhaps we can learn from them without constructing a definitive hierarchy.

A bridge needn't prove that every shore is the same. It only needs to allow us to learn to see them from more than one place.

## Can We Find a Shared Language?

Here a possibility especially interests me.

Perhaps some discussions between traditions could benefit from functional language.

For example, when we speak of ignorance, we can investigate which interpretations and tendencies perpetuate suffering.

When we speak of concentration, we can study how attentional stability develops.

When we speak of emptiness, we can examine the difficulties of attributing independent essences to phenomena.

When we speak of kōans, we can investigate how certain processes call our familiar interpretations into question.

And when we speak of emotional transformation, we can investigate how our responses depend on conditions.

That doesn't mean every traditional concept can be reduced to a psychological process.

Some doctrines contain cosmological, ontological, or soteriological claims that don't fit directly into our model.

Nor should we assume that using the same vocabulary means describing exactly the same phenomenon.

But a shared language might help us ask clearer questions.

What are we experiencing?

What interpretation does each tradition propose?

What practical consequences does it have?

What can we observe?

What remains beyond our reach?

Perhaps this approach will help us find connections.

And also recognize irreconcilable differences.

I don't regard that as a failure.

Quite the opposite.

A useful dialogue doesn't need to end with everyone thinking exactly alike.

Perhaps understanding better where we agree will be enough.

And where we disagree.

## The Temptation to Build a New Tradition

There's something I particularly want to avoid.

After studying different teachings, we may be tempted to pick what we like best.

A little Theravāda.

Some Mahāyāna.

A few Zen kōans.

Some Vajrayāna practices.

And finally announce that we've discovered the definitive system that surpasses all its predecessors.

That would be a rather efficient way of turning curiosity into a new form of arrogance.

I don't want to do that.

My model isn't intended to replace twenty-five hundred years of contemplative traditions.

Nor to demonstrate that their teachers were wrong because I've found a more modern explanation.

What I'm trying to do is simpler.

Understand some of the teachings I've received.

Relate them to my experience.

And propose a framework that allows me to investigate them coherently.

Of course, that framework can be wrong.

And perhaps some traditional explanations are more profound than I can currently comprehend.

We must leave that possibility open.

But I also don't think respecting a tradition means accepting every claim without examination.

We can be deeply grateful for a teaching.

And question some of its interpretations.

We can learn from a teacher.

And recognize their limitations.

And we can develop a personal understanding without needing to proclaim we've surpassed those who taught us.

Perhaps gratitude and criticism are perfectly compatible.

In fact, I think they may need each other.

## Tradition Is Conditioned Too

Here's an interesting consequence of our model.

If we view the human mind as a conditioned system, perhaps we should recognize that traditions themselves also depend on causes and conditions.

They develop in cultural contexts.

Use particular languages.

Respond to certain needs.

And change over history.

That doesn't mean all their teachings are false.

But it does mean their forms of expression, institutions, and interpretations are deeply connected to the circumstances in which they arise.

A concept may acquire different meanings in different contexts.

A practice may be interpreted through religious, philosophical, or psychological frameworks.

And a teacher may understand a teaching through the conditions of their own life.

Perhaps recognizing this can help us be more humble.

Not only about other traditions.

But our own too.

Because our model is another conditioned product.

It arose through reading.

Experiences.

Conversations.

And numerous attempts to understand.

It isn't a viewpoint outside history.

Nor an independent observer capable of judging every tradition from an absolute position.

It's one more interpretation.

Which might prove useful.

And which must, precisely for that reason, remain open to revision.

## Which Path Should We Choose?

At this point, perhaps you're wondering which tradition you should follow.

I don't think there's one universal answer.

People have different needs.

Different abilities.

Personal histories.

And conditions that may make certain practices more suitable than others.

Perhaps you'll find early Buddhist teachings especially useful.

Maybe you'll be deeply inspired by the bodhisattva ideal.

Perhaps you'll be drawn to Zen's direct practice.

Or perhaps you're interested in certain contemplative practices of Tibetan Buddhism.

It's also possible that no tradition fully fits your questions.

That's all right.

But I think we can establish a few simple criteria.

A practice should help you develop understanding.

Relate better to your experience.

And reduce unnecessary suffering where possible.

It should also allow you to recognize your limits.

Question interpretations.

And act responsibly.

Not every practice will suit everyone.

And some advanced techniques require specific preparation, guidance, and safety conditions.

We don't have to try everything.

Or prove we're capable of attaining every state described by the traditions.

Perhaps recognizing what we're trying to cultivate matters more.

And why.

## A Small Investigation: Four Perspectives

I want to suggest a conceptual exercise.

Think of an everyday situation that causes you suffering.

For example, an argument with someone you love.

Now try viewing it through four perspectives inspired by some of the emphases we've explored.

**First perspective: the conditions of suffering.**

What happened?

What sensations arose?

What desires, rejections, or interpretations are fueling your reaction?

What could you stop reinforcing?

**Second perspective: compassion and emptiness.**

How might your understanding change if you stopped treating yourself and the other person as rigid identities?

What causes and conditions might be affecting you both?

How could you respond in a way that reduces suffering?

**Third perspective: step outside the framework and act.**

Are you trapped in an interpretation that seems beyond question?

Can you view the situation from an unexpected angle?

And what concrete action could you take now, rather than continuing to argue mentally with what happened?

**Fourth perspective: transform your relationship with experience.**

Can you recognize the bodily and emotional energy accompanying the situation?

Can you change how you interpret it?

Can you develop a different response without needing to deny what you feel?

These questions are inspired by the themes we've associated with different traditions.

But they aren't a formal traditional practice from any of them.

And in particular, they aren't equivalent to tantric practice or traditional kōan work.

They're simply four ways of investigating a problem.

Perhaps you'll find some questions particularly useful.

Others perhaps not.

The important thing isn't proving that a tradition is right.

It's learning to understand your experience better.

And perhaps developing a wiser response.

## Different Paths, Possible Meetings

We've explored three major Buddhist families and some of their schools.

Theravāda.

Mahāyāna, including Zen.

Vajrayāna.

We've spoken about liberation, compassion, emptiness, consciousness, and tantric practices.

We've also explored how certain teachings can challenge our conceptual frameworks and remind us of the importance of action.

And we've recognized important differences.

Not all traditions describe exactly the same goal.

Not all share the same philosophical interpretations.

And some claims are difficult to reconcile.

But perhaps we don't need to resolve every difference in order to learn.

We can recognize connections.

Investigate their foundations.

And remain open to what we don't yet understand.

From our model's perspective, perhaps we can find common ground.

Experience depends on conditions.

Certain dynamics generate suffering.

And we can develop abilities that transform our relationship with them.

But we must remember that this formulation is our functional proposal.

Not an exhaustive definition of Buddhism.

Nor a claim that all traditions share exactly the same ideas.

Perhaps a useful bridge isn't one that merges every shore into one.

But one that allows us to cross differences without needing to destroy them.

And perhaps that's especially important in a world where so many people seem convinced that, for them to be right, someone else must necessarily be wrong.

We can learn to listen.

Recognize what we don't know.

And value contributions from different perspectives.

Without abandoning our judgment.

Without treating all ideas as equivalent.

And without needing to defend a spiritual identity.

Perhaps wisdom isn't about finding a tradition with all the answers, but learning to investigate honestly enough to recognize what each can teach us.

And perhaps, when we learn to do that, we'll discover something especially important.

We don't need to turn the Dharma into another reason for division.

We can use it to understand better.

Reduce suffering.

And learn to relate with greater love and wisdom.

To ourselves.

To those who think as we do.

And, especially, to those who don't.

In the next chapter we'll carry out one final investigation.

But this time we won't question a tradition.

Or a meditative experience.

Or a philosophical interpretation.

We'll question our own model.

Because if we really want to build a useful tool for understanding the Dharma, we must be willing to recognize where it works.

Where we still don't know.

And where we may be wrong.

And if we discover that some of our ideas don't stand up to investigation, we'll have to do something quite simple.

Change our minds.

I suspect the world would be a considerably nicer place if we all learned to do that a little more often.
