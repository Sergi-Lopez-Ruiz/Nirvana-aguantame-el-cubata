# 22. Investigating Our Reactions

Have you ever done something and, a second later, wondered why on earth you did it?

Perhaps you replied aggressively. Said something you didn't mean to say. Interrupted someone. Or reacted in a way that, just five minutes earlier, you'd have considered completely absurd.

And then an extraordinarily interesting question arises:

"Why did I do that?"

It seems simple. But perhaps it's one of the most important questions on our entire journey.

Because it takes us straight to the heart of our model.

What happens when a reaction arises before we've had a chance to understand it? And, above all, what can we learn so that something different happens next time?

## We don't always decide before we act

We normally imagine our actions following a simple sequence.

First we think. Then we decide. Finally, we act.

But everyday experience shows that things often happen differently.

Someone frightens us, and we jump. Something feels unpleasant, and we pull our hand away. We hear criticism, and we respond defensively.

Sometimes we notice our reaction when it has already begun. Or even after it has ended.

That doesn't mean every action is an automatic reflex or that conscious decisions are unimportant. But it does invite us to question the idea that clear, conscious deliberation always precedes every behaviour.

In our model, actions can be understood as the outcome of multiple conditioned processes.

Perceptions. Sensations. Memories. Expectations. Emotions. Habits. Intentions. And external circumstances.

All of them may contribute to what we eventually do.

We don't need to remove intention from the explanation. But we don't need to imagine it as an order issued by an independent controller either. Intentions also arise through causes and conditions.

That idea can be uncomfortable. We're used to thinking of ourselves as the absolute authors of our decisions. But perhaps it can also help us understand our behaviour better—especially when we don't like it.

## The birth of a reaction

Imagine you're working.

You've spent hours solving a complicated problem. You're tired. You're hungry. And someone interrupts you to ask a question you've already answered three times.

Suddenly, irritation appears.

Then an interpretation: "They don't respect my time."

And almost immediately, an unpleasant response: "I've already explained it to you!"

The other person falls silent. A few seconds later, you realise you overreacted.

What happened?

We can try to reconstruct some of the conditions: tiredness, accumulated frustration, the interruption, the interpretation that you were being disrespected, the unpleasant feeling, a defensive habit, and a response that arose very quickly.

But perhaps there's more.

Maybe you would have answered differently if you'd been rested. Perhaps you'd have felt patience if a small child had asked the question. Or perhaps the same irritation wouldn't have appeared if you'd interpreted the interruption as a genuine request for help.

The interesting thing is that the stimulus alone doesn't explain the whole reaction.

Conditions matter. And our interpretation of the situation may be one of them.

## We don't react only to what happens

I'd like to suggest an idea.

Often, our response depends not only on an event, but also on what that event means to us.

Imagine someone doesn't greet you.

Perhaps they didn't see you. Perhaps they're worried. Perhaps they're in a hurry. Or perhaps they really are angry with you.

You don't necessarily know the explanation.

Yet a thought may appear: "They're ignoring me."

That thought may trigger sadness, irritation, or insecurity. You may respond coldly. The other person notices your behaviour and perhaps starts treating you distantly too.

Within minutes, an initial interpretation that we never checked can help create a real conflict.

Our minds have an extraordinary ability to make some of their predictions begin to resemble reality.

Here we find a connection with the autosuggestion we've started investigating. Expectations may alter perception, emotion, and action. And the consequences of our actions may reinforce our original expectations.

But we must be careful.

Not every reaction is based on a conscious interpretation. Some physiological responses and learned habits can activate very quickly, before we're even able to put a story into words.

And some interpretations are correct. We don't want to convince readers that every interpersonal problem is a product of their imagination.

What we're investigating is simpler: between an event and our response, processes may intervene that we don't immediately recognise. Learning to observe them can help us understand our reactions.

## Speed matters

There's something I consider particularly important.

Not all reactions happen at the same speed.

Some develop slowly. Perhaps we spend hours worrying about a situation, or feed irritation throughout an afternoon. In such cases, there may be many opportunities to notice what is happening.

But other reactions are extraordinarily fast.

A word. A gesture. A sensation. And the response has already begun.

That can be frustrating, especially when we've developed considerable intellectual understanding of our habits.

We may know perfectly well that we don't want to respond aggressively—and still do so. Afterwards we can see everything with absolute clarity. But too late to prevent the first response.

I think we need to distinguish two capacities.

The ability to recognise a reaction. And the ability to change the conditions that favour its appearance.

The first helps us understand what happened. The second may help different responses arise in future situations. Perhaps both are necessary.

Recognising a reaction after it happened can be useful for learning. But it doesn't erase its consequences.

If we've hurt someone, we need to deal with that. And if a reaction is particularly intense or dangerous, we may need concrete preventive measures rather than relying solely on noticing it in time.

## A reaction is not an identity

There's a human tendency I find especially interesting.

When someone behaves badly, we may conclude they're a bad person. If they lie, we turn them into a liar. If they act selfishly, we decide they're selfish.

And when we make a mistake, perhaps we do exactly the same thing.

"I'm a disaster." "I'm a bad person." "I'll never learn."

But our model allows us to investigate another perspective.

A behaviour arises under certain conditions. It may be connected to deep habits. It may happen repeatedly. It may have serious consequences.

And yet we don't have to turn it into a permanent essence that completely defines a person.

That doesn't eliminate responsibility. Harmful behaviour must be recognised as such, and where necessary there must be boundaries and consequences.

But we can distinguish a person from their behaviour; recognising that someone has caused harm from claiming that causing harm is their entire identity.

This can be especially important for our first training: love.

It allows us to understand without having to justify. Correct without humiliating. And learn without turning every mistake into a final verdict—including our own.

## The danger of using conditioning as an excuse

Here we need to make something fundamental clear.

If our actions are conditioned, does that mean we're not responsible for them?

No. At least that's not the conclusion I want to defend in this book.

The fact that a behaviour has causes doesn't remove its consequences. And understanding those causes doesn't mean we should allow the behaviour.

Imagine someone drives dangerously because they're tired. Tiredness may help explain their behaviour. But it doesn't make that behaviour safe. Nor does it remove the responsibility to take steps to avoid driving in those conditions.

The same is true of our reactions.

We may understand that we responded aggressively because we were exhausted, worried, or caught in an old habit. But if we've hurt someone, the harm is still real.

We can apologise. Listen. Repair what can be repaired. And take concrete steps to reduce the likelihood of it happening again.

Perhaps we need more rest, to avoid certain situations when we're highly agitated, to learn emotional regulation skills, to ask for help, or to make specific agreements with people around us.

In our model, responsibility doesn't require imagining a will completely independent of all conditions. We can understand it as part of our ability to recognise consequences, learn, and act on future conditions.

Understanding why we did something doesn't justify it. It gives us information to help us avoid repeating it.

And when there's a risk of harming someone—especially someone vulnerable—protecting that person must take priority.

Not producing a particularly elegant philosophical explanation.

## The effort of not reacting

Imagine you're angry.

You recognise the anger and decide you don't want to respond aggressively.

What can you do?

Perhaps you try to hold yourself back, keep your mouth shut, breathe deeply, and wait for the intensity to subside.

In some circumstances that can be very useful. But you may also discover that it's exhausting, especially when you're tired or the conditions keep feeding the reaction.

Our model helps us understand why. We're trying to alter a response that's already been activated, and perhaps multiple processes are still favouring it.

That's why I think it's important to distinguish containing a reaction from transforming the conditions that generate it.

Containing it may be necessary to prevent immediate harm. But it may not be enough to change the habit over the long term.

We can learn to recognise what situations favour it, which interpretations intensify it, what bodily signals arise, and which conditions we can change *before* we find ourselves in a difficult situation.

We won't always succeed. But perhaps some reactions will become less frequent when we stop feeding certain conditions.

And that may be far more sustainable than constantly trying to win a battle against ourselves.

## The solutions aren't always where the problem appears

Let's imagine something else.

For weeks, you've been feeling irritable. Almost everything bothers you. Concentrating is difficult. And you find yourself reacting impatiently to things that hardly affected you before.

Perhaps you conclude that you need to work on your anger and start analysing each episode.

But what if the problem is largely related to your sleep? Or to a work situation that keeps you under constant pressure? Or to personal difficulties for which you need support?

Sometimes a reaction is merely the visible expression of more general conditions.

From our model, this opens a very interesting possibility. Changing a general condition can influence many specific responses simultaneously.

Sleeping better may support attention and reduce irritability. A safer relationship may ease certain defensive reactions. Reducing sustained stress may change how we experience many situations. And developing a kinder attitude towards ourselves may lessen patterns of judgement and frustration.

Of course, these relationships aren't always simple. And we can't always change external conditions.

But it may be useful to ask where we're trying to intervene.

Are we trying to extinguish every little flame? Or can we recognise something that keeps feeding the fire?

## The pause: a new condition

Many teachings on emotional regulation recommend pausing before responding.

Counting to ten. Breathing. Walking away for a moment. Returning when the emotion has become less intense.

Simple advice. But our model gives us an interesting way to understand it.

A pause introduces different conditions into a sequence that used to unfold very quickly. Perhaps it allows some activation to subside, a broader perspective to appear, or a previously cultivated intention to be remembered.

A pause doesn't guarantee a wise response. But it may increase our chances of recognising what's happening.

And that can be valuable.

There is, however, a difficulty: when a reaction is very intense, we may not be able to remember to pause.

That's precisely why it can help to practise in simple situations. A minor annoyance. An interruption. A moment of impatience.

We can develop the habit of noticing early signals and associating them with an alternative response.

For example: "When I notice my voice starting to rise, I'll stop the conversation for a moment."

We're not relying on a magical will. We're trying to train a response that may become more accessible.

And perhaps, over time, some pauses will happen more naturally.

## Repairing also changes conditions

There's something I don't want us to forget.

Sometimes we'll get it wrong. Respond in a way we didn't want to. Say something hurtful. Or discover too late that an emotion has swept us away.

What do we do then?

Perhaps our first reaction is to punish ourselves, feel ashamed, or create a story about what terrible people we are.

But that doesn't necessarily help repair the harm. It may even make an honest assessment harder.

We can try a different response: recognise the behaviour, accept its consequences, listen to the person affected, apologise when appropriate, and make concrete changes.

I want to emphasise one thing.

Apologising isn't the same as repairing.

An apology can matter. But the other person may need more: safety, time, a change in behaviour, or reasonable assurance that we're taking steps to prevent it happening again.

And that takes action.

In our model, repair can also help transform future conditions. It may change our relationships, help us understand consequences, and support more responsible habits.

Not because an apology automatically erases harm. But because we can learn from what we've done.

Compassion for ourselves doesn't require minimising our mistakes. It can help us acknowledge them without constantly defending ourselves.

And perhaps that honesty is one of the most important conditions for change.

## When the mind invents a justification

Here's another curious phenomenon.

Sometimes we react—and then construct an explanation.

We become angry, respond aggressively, and seconds later begin justifying our behaviour.

"They deserved it." "They were provoking me." "I had no other choice." "Anyone would have reacted the same way."

Perhaps some of these statements contain elements of truth. But they can also serve to protect our self-image.

Here is another connection with autosuggestion. A later interpretation can change how we remember an event. And if we repeat it enough, it may eventually seem completely obvious to us.

That doesn't mean every justification is false. But we can learn to investigate them.

For example: Am I trying to understand what happened, or prove I was right? Am I considering the consequences of my actions? Is there information I'm avoiding? Can I accept that my reaction was harmful without concluding that I'm a bad person?

These questions may be uncomfortable. But they can open new opportunities for learning.

Perhaps one of the subtlest ways to feed a habit is to justify it repeatedly after it appears. And one of the most important ways to change it may be learning to recognise it honestly.

Without hatred. But without excuses either.

## The role of our three trainings

Let's return to our three trainings.

Love. Concentration. Investigation.

How can they help us change our reactions?

**Love** can provide direction. We want to reduce suffering, care for ourselves, and treat others with dignity. When an intense reaction arises, that orientation may help us remember what kind of response we want to cultivate.

**Concentration** may provide the stability needed to notice what's happening. We can learn to observe distractions, emotions, and some impulses before becoming completely caught up in them.

**Investigation** can help us understand the conditions feeding the reaction: sensations, interpretations, habits, and circumstances we may need to change.

And interestingly, we can also use these capacities *after* we've reacted.

Love helps us repair without despising ourselves. Concentration supports the stability needed to review what happened. And investigation allows us to learn from the experience.

We don't always need to use them in the same order. Nor do they guarantee we'll stop making mistakes. But they can help transform our tendencies.

Perhaps that's enough to begin.

## A small experiment: investigating a reaction

Let's try something practical.

Think of an everyday reaction you'd like to understand better. Don't choose a traumatic event or a particularly painful situation.

It could be mild irritation, impatience, repeated worry, or a response that appears when someone disagrees with you.

Now try to reconstruct what happened.

**First: the conditions.** How did you feel beforehand? Were you tired? Hungry? Had something unpleasant happened? Were you already worried?

**Second: the experience.** What happened? What bodily sensations do you remember? What thoughts arose? What pleasant or unpleasant feeling tone accompanied the experience?

**Third: the interpretation.** What meaning did you assign to the situation? Did you think someone was attacking you, that something was unfair, or that you had to defend yourself? Could there be other interpretations?

**Fourth: the reaction.** What did you do? Was there a recognisable intention? Was the response immediate? Did you have any chance to pause?

**Fifth: the consequences.** What happened next? Did your reaction solve the problem? Create new difficulties? Reinforce an interpretation?

Finally, ask yourself: What specific condition could I change to favour a different response next time?

You don't need the definitive explanation. Perhaps it's enough to recognise that you need rest before a difficult conversation, that certain interpretations intensify your discomfort, or that you can practise pausing when you notice a bodily signal.

If your reactions could cause harm, especially aggression, don't rely only on introspection: establish safety measures and seek appropriate help.

We're not looking for a justification. We're investigating how to learn.

## Change isn't always linear

There's something worth remembering.

We can practise for a long time, develop concentration, understand particular habits deeply—and still discover that an old reaction has returned.

That can be disappointing, especially if we've created an image of ourselves as people who should have overcome certain difficulties by now.

But learning isn't always linear. Conditions change. So do our capacities. And some responses can reappear under particularly demanding circumstances.

From our model, this isn't terribly mysterious. A habit can depend on many conditions. Some may have changed, while others remain. And certain circumstances may favour responses that haven't appeared for a long time.

That doesn't prove all previous progress has vanished. But it doesn't guarantee the problem is solved either.

We need to observe what happens, recognise the consequences, and continue working with relevant conditions.

Without turning every difficulty into a personal tragedy. And without using impermanence or conditioning to minimise harmful behaviour.

Learning also means recognising that some things still need attention.

## We don't need to defeat our minds

I'd like to finish with a personal reflection.

For a long time, I thought changing meant fighting against certain parts of myself: eliminating my defects, controlling my emotions, mastering my thoughts, becoming someone who would never make particular mistakes.

But I've begun to understand training differently.

Perhaps we don't need to defeat our minds. Perhaps we need to learn to care for them.

Recognise the conditions that favour certain responses, develop healthy habits, and change what we can.

That doesn't mean resignation. We can hold ourselves to very high standards in our actions, especially when they affect other people's well-being. But we don't need to turn those standards into self-hatred.

We can learn to distinguish responsibility from punishment; recognising a mistake from constructing an identity around it; working to change from despising what we haven't transformed yet.

Perhaps that distinction will help us learn better.

When we're no longer busy defending our image, we can observe more honestly. And when we no longer need to punish ourselves constantly, perhaps we have more energy available to repair and change.

We aren't responsible for having chosen every condition that shaped us. But that doesn't free us from the responsibility to care for the conditions we help create from now on.

We don't need an independent will to understand why our actions matter. Actions have consequences. And we can learn.

## A different reaction

We've explored how our reactions arise; how sensations, interpretations, habits, and intentions contribute to behaviour; and how certain dynamics reinforce themselves.

We've also seen that our responses aren't all equally accessible to conscious observation. Some arise slowly; others extraordinarily quickly. And sometimes we only understand them after they happen.

But even then, we can learn. Investigate their conditions. Recognise consequences. Repair harm. And develop new responses.

We don't need to eliminate all automatic reactions. Many are useful. Others are part of our emotional life.

We want to reduce the patterns that generate unnecessary suffering and cultivate capacities that help us relate to the world more wisely.

Sometimes we can intervene directly. At other times we'll need to change more general conditions. And perhaps we'll discover that some important transformations happen precisely when we stop fighting every individual reaction.

Not because we've given up on change, but because we've learned a more intelligent way to change.

Perhaps the freedom we're seeking isn't the ability to control every reaction, but the capacity to develop conditions that allow more and more of our responses to express the love and wisdom we want to cultivate.

And when we fall short, something important remains to be done.

Recognise it. Take responsibility. Learn. And continue.

In the next chapter we'll explore a capacity closely connected with all this: equanimity.

Perhaps one of the deepest changes we can develop is learning to experience what happens without immediately turning it into a struggle.

And for a mind that has spent a lifetime trying to get the universe to behave according to its preferences, that may be truly revolutionary.
