# 24. Obstacles to Practice: Learning to Recognise What Holds Us Back

I have news for you.

If you've started meditating and discovered that your mind doesn't behave quite as you expected, you're not the first person this has happened to.

In fact, I suspect we've spent about two and a half thousand years discovering much the same thing.

You sit down. Close your eyes. Decide to observe your breath.

And before thirty seconds have passed, you're already thinking about work, dinner, an unpleasant conversation, or whether it's really worth spending five minutes on such a boring activity.

Perhaps you get sleepy. Perhaps you become angry because you can't concentrate.

Or perhaps a particularly interesting doubt appears: "What if I'm wasting my time?"

Welcome to the wonderful world of meditation obstacles.

The good news is that we don't need to invent an explanation for every one of them. Buddhism has been studying them for a long time.

The even better news is that our model may help us understand them in a particularly practical way.

Because perhaps obstacles aren't simply enemies we have to defeat. Perhaps they're conditioned processes that we can learn to recognise and, in some circumstances, transform.

Let's investigate.

## The five hindrances

Buddhist teachings describe five major hindrances, known as *nīvaraṇas*:

1. **Sensual desire (*kāmacchanda*):** attraction to pleasant sensory experiences that draws the mind away from practice.
2. **Ill will (*byāpāda*):** rejection, irritation, or hostility towards what we're experiencing.
3. **Sloth and torpor (*thīna-middha*):** a lack of energy, clarity, or mental alertness.
4. **Restlessness and remorse (*uddhacca-kukkucca*):** agitation and worry that prevent the mind from settling.
5. **Doubt (*vicikicchā*):** paralysing uncertainty that makes it difficult to commit to training.

These hindrances appear repeatedly in Buddhist discourses.

And there's something particularly interesting about them: they're not presented merely as sins or moral defects.

They're conditions that make concentration, clarity, and understanding harder to develop.

From our model's perspective, we can regard them as dynamics that favour certain mental configurations.

Desire draws attention towards an object. Aversion keeps it occupied with what we reject. Torpor reduces clarity. Restlessness encourages constant shifts in attention. And doubt may weaken the intentions that sustain practice.

We don't need to feel guilty when they arise. But we don't need to resign ourselves to following them either.

We can learn to recognise them. And perhaps, by understanding their conditions, discover ways of reducing their influence.

## First hindrance: sensual desire

Imagine you're meditating.

You've been observing your breath for three minutes. And suddenly you remember there's chocolate in the kitchen.

Chocolate.

Wonderful chocolate.

Perhaps you begin imagining its flavour. Its texture. The extraordinary pleasure of eating a little piece.

And before you know it, you've mentally organised an expedition to the fridge.

What happened?

Your attention found something more interesting than breathing.

Not terribly mysterious. Our minds are constantly evaluating possibilities, and many pleasant experiences are tremendously attractive.

Sensual desire doesn't simply mean enjoying our senses. The problem arises when attraction occupies our minds in a way that interferes with what we're trying to cultivate.

We can appreciate chocolate, enjoy music, or take pleasure in being with someone we love. But when we're trying to concentrate, the wish for a different experience can become a constant source of distraction.

Through our model, we can investigate the process.

A mental image of something pleasant appears. That image favours desire. Desire directs more attention towards the object. And attention can intensify its appeal.

A small self-reinforcing loop.

What can we do?

We can begin simply by recognising it: "There's desire."

We don't need to become angry or immediately erase the image of chocolate. We can notice that our attention has been drawn away—and return.

But we can also investigate the conditions. Perhaps we're meditating when very hungry. Perhaps we've spent hours consuming intense stimulation. Or perhaps we've developed the habit of seeking entertainment every time boredom appears.

Sometimes changing the context will help. At other times, learning to remain with desire briefly without following it.

And perhaps we'll discover something interesting: a pleasant experience can be appealing without our necessarily having to pursue it.

The chocolate will still be in the kitchen when we've finished meditating.

At least if nobody else finds it first.

## Second hindrance: ill will

Let's move to the next hindrance.

Aversion.

Imagine you're trying to meditate and someone begins making noise. Perhaps a neighbour. Perhaps a car. Or perhaps one of your children has decided that right now is the perfect time to practise producing extraordinarily loud sounds.

Irritation appears.

"Can't we have five minutes of peace in this house?"

Then another thought: "It's always the same."

And perhaps you end up composing an entire speech about humanity's lack of consideration.

Meanwhile, the noise may already have stopped. But your mind is still arguing with it.

Here's a dynamic we know well. An unpleasant experience favours rejection. Thoughts then arise that can feed that rejection. Gradually, attention becomes trapped in what we wanted to avoid.

From our model's perspective, ill will can be understood as a configuration that keeps the struggle against certain contents of experience active.

But what if we recognise the rejection?

We can observe: "There's irritation." "I want the noise to stop." "I'm building a story about how unfair this is."

We don't need to approve of the noise. If possible and appropriate, we can ask for it to be reduced or find somewhere quieter.

But perhaps we don't need to keep an entire inner argument going while deciding what to do.

Here is a direct connection with our first training: love.

In SN 46.51, goodwill is presented as one of the conditions that counteract ill will.

Perhaps cultivating *mettā* can transform our orientation towards people who annoy us. Not because their behaviour has no consequences, but because we may learn to recognise it without necessarily adding hostility.

And when we're particularly angry, perhaps the most sensible thing is to stop meditating for a while and attend to our needs.

Not every difficult experience has to become a heroic opportunity for spiritual growth.

Sometimes closing a window is enough.

## Third hindrance: sloth and torpor

This hindrance has a particularly curious feature.

The more it appears, the less we care that it's appearing.

You begin meditating. The breath feels calm. Thoughts subside. Gradually, a pleasant heaviness appears. Your head starts to droop. Attention becomes less clear.

And when you wake up, you discover you've spent ten minutes experiencing a state of concentration remarkably similar to a nap.

Not necessarily a tragedy. But we shouldn't automatically mistake it for deep meditation either.

Torpor can appear as reduced clarity and mental energy. Sometimes it's accompanied by sleepiness. Other times there's a kind of fog: a peaceful but not particularly lucid experience.

Our model invites us to investigate what favours it.

Perhaps we slept badly. Perhaps we've just eaten too much. Or perhaps our way of practising is reducing activation excessively.

What can we do?

Open our eyes. Sit more upright. Try walking meditation. Choose a time when we're more awake. Or temporarily switch to a more active practice.

But something is fundamental: not every bout of sleepiness is a hindrance we ought to overcome with effort. Sometimes it's a legitimate need for rest.

If we're exhausted, perhaps the sensible thing is to sleep. Not heroically struggle to stay awake while our body asks for something entirely reasonable.

It's also worth remembering that medical conditions, sleep problems, or medication may affect our energy levels. Meditation doesn't replace proper assessment when tiredness is severe or persistent.

Here our model offers a practical lesson: not every difficulty can be resolved by intervening directly in attention. Sometimes we need to change more general conditions.

Like sleeping better.

I know. That spectacularly unexciting solution again.

## Fourth hindrance: restlessness and remorse

This is one of my favourites.

Not because it's pleasant, but because our minds can demonstrate truly extraordinary creativity.

You sit down to meditate and begin remembering everything you still have to do.

A conversation. An email. A decision. Something you said yesterday. Something you should have said five years ago.

And if there's no real problem, perhaps your mind will find one that hasn't happened yet.

Restlessness keeps pushing attention towards new contents. Remorse may keep it trapped in past events.

In our model, we find two interesting dynamics.

The first is the tendency of attention to shift repeatedly when internal stimuli become relevant. The second is the ability of interpretations to keep particular worries active.

Imagine remembering an unpleasant conversation.

An interpretation appears: "I should have answered differently."

It creates discomfort. And that discomfort favours further thoughts about the conversation.

We're feeding a dynamic.

What can we do?

Sometimes simply recognise restlessness and gently return to the breath. Sometimes reduce our demands on ourselves. Perhaps practise walking meditation or spend a few minutes cultivating goodwill.

But some worries are reminding us of things that genuinely need attention.

If we've hurt someone, perhaps we need to repair the harm. If we have an important obligation, perhaps we should write it down and deal with it later.

Meditation shouldn't become a way of avoiding our responsibilities.

We can distinguish between a concern that calls for action and a repetitive pattern that keeps feeding itself without producing solutions.

And when we've done what's reasonably possible, perhaps we can allow ourselves to rest.

We don't have to solve all the universe's problems before observing one breath.

Though I admit that on some days our minds seem convinced otherwise.

## Fifth hindrance: doubt

Now we reach a particularly interesting hindrance: doubt.

Because after so many chapters defending intellectual honesty and the need to question our interpretations, you might be thinking:

"Hang on. Is doubt suddenly a bad thing?"

Good question.

No.

Not all doubt is harmful. In fact, I regard the ability to question our beliefs as a fundamental part of learning.

But there's a difference between open inquiry and doubt that paralyses us.

Imagine deciding to learn meditation. After three days, you begin wondering if you're using the right technique. You read another book, discover another method, and decide to switch.

Two days later, you find a video about a different tradition and begin to suspect you should practise something else altogether.

Eventually, after several weeks of intense research, you've spent twenty hours comparing methods and approximately six minutes meditating.

A considerable intellectual achievement. But perhaps not particularly effective for developing concentration.

There's an important distinction here.

We can investigate a method critically, examine its foundations, consider its risks and benefits, and decide whether it's appropriate.

But we can also become trapped in an endless search for certainty that never arrives.

From our model's perspective, doubt may become a hindrance when our interpretations of uncertainty repeatedly weaken the intentions that make learning possible.

We don't need blind faith in a method. But it may help to choose a reasonable, safe practice, try it for a while, and evaluate what happens—with openness to changing it when there are good reasons.

Wisdom needs the ability to question, but also the willingness to investigate through experience.

We can't obtain the results of an experiment we never carry out.

## What feeds a hindrance

Now I'd like to introduce a teaching I find extraordinarily interesting.

In the *Āhāra Sutta* (SN 46.51), the Buddha describes the conditions that nourish the five hindrances and those that help weaken their development.

The image is magnificent.

Hindrances need nourishment too.

Think about that through our model. They don't arise without cause. They depend on causes and conditions. And certain ways of paying attention can reinforce them.

When we constantly feed hostile thoughts, we may encourage ill will. When we devote our attention to sensory fantasies, desire may intensify. When we maintain agitated mental activity, calm may be harder to develop. And when we obsess over questions that don't lead to practical understanding, we may feed doubt.

But we can develop other conditions too.

Goodwill. Clarity. Stability. Energy. More careful investigation.

This connects directly with our interpretation of ignorance.

We don't need to imagine hindrances as independent forces attacking our minds. We can regard them as dynamics sustained by particular conditions. And perhaps some of those conditions can be changed.

Not always immediately. Not always through willpower. But sometimes through learning and training.

We don't need to fight every hindrance. We can learn to recognise what feeds it.

Perhaps that's one of the most practical ideas in our whole model.

## Different hindrances require different responses

Here's a common temptation.

We find a technique that works, and decide to use it for absolutely everything.

If we're restless, we breathe. If we're sleepy, we breathe. If we're angry, we breathe.

And if the kitchen catches fire, perhaps we also start breathing mindfully while contemplating the impermanence of household appliances.

But perhaps we should call the fire brigade.

Practice requires discernment.

Not every difficulty calls for the same response.

When we're agitated, developing calm may help. When we're sleepy, perhaps we need to increase our energy or rest. When hostility arises, cultivating goodwill can help. If we're caught in paralysing doubt, perhaps we need information, guidance, or a simple practice we can evaluate.

And if a situation requires concrete action, perhaps that action matters more than continuing to meditate.

Through our model, this makes considerable sense. Different problems depend on different conditions. Appropriate interventions may vary, too.

There's no reason to assume one technique will always be the best.

Here wisdom becomes especially practical. It isn't only about understanding how a hindrance operates, but also about recognising which response may be useful in each situation.

And accepting that sometimes we may choose badly. We're learning to choose our tools as well.

## What if we're the hindrance?

There's another problem worth examining: our relationship with the hindrances.

Imagine you're meditating and boredom appears. You notice it.

But then an interpretation arises.

"I shouldn't be bored." "An advanced meditator wouldn't have this problem." "I've been practising too long to still be like this."

Now you're not only bored. You're also rejecting the boredom—and perhaps constructing a story about your own inadequacy.

Our model allows us to regard this as another layer of conditioning.

The original hindrance favours an interpretation. The interpretation generates discomfort. And the discomfort may feed the hindrance even more.

Curiously, a practice intended to reduce suffering can end up creating fresh conditions that sustain it.

That's why our first training matters so much: love.

We can recognise hindrances without treating ourselves as enemies. Accept that we're learning. And continue with patience.

That doesn't mean giving up effort. It means learning to make an effort without turning every difficulty into a condemnation.

Perhaps noticing that we've lost attention is an opportunity to cultivate kindness. Recognising boredom may be a chance to investigate desire. And observing frustration may help us understand how we build expectations about our own practice.

In some circumstances, the hindrance itself can become an object of investigation.

But we should distinguish that from obsessively analysing every difficulty. We don't need to turn every session into a philosophical exam.

Sometimes it's enough to notice what happened—and return.

## The search for extraordinary experiences

I'd like to introduce a hindrance that isn't a separate category among the traditional five, but deserves particular attention.

Obsession with meditative experiences.

When we discover that practice can produce deep concentration, intense well-being, or extraordinary changes in perception, we may begin pursuing those experiences.

We want to repeat that wonderful feeling. Experience *pīti* again. Recover a special tranquillity. Or reach a state we've read about in a book.

There's nothing necessarily wrong with being interested in these possibilities. But perhaps we should ask how that interest is affecting our practice.

Are we learning to understand our experience better, or constantly trying to reproduce a particular experience?

Is curiosity supporting learning, or are we developing a new kind of dissatisfaction when things don't happen as expected?

From our model's perspective, even the search for awakening can become a pattern of attachment.

And here's the extraordinary irony: we may end up suffering because we haven't yet managed to free ourselves from suffering.

Quite ingenious. But not especially helpful.

So it's worth remembering something.

Meditative experiences depend on conditions too. They can arise, change, and disappear.

We don't need to make them a measure of our worth or definitive proof of spiritual progress. We can appreciate them, learn from them, and continue investigating.

## When enthusiasm becomes another hindrance

There's another, less obvious difficulty: excessive energy.

Imagine discovering a practice that fascinates you. You start meditating more, reading more, investigating more. Every day brings new ideas and possibilities.

It can be an extraordinarily stimulating period.

But it's worth noticing what's happening to your rest, sleep, relationships, and responsibilities.

Not every form of intense activation supports clarity.

Sometimes we mistake enthusiasm for stability, or interpret a rapid succession of ideas as proof of profound understanding.

Buddhism teaches the balanced development of different mental qualities. I consider that particularly important.

We don't need to keep increasing the intensity of practice. We also need calm, rest, equanimity, and the ability to recognise when enough is enough.

If you experience a marked reduction in the need for sleep, racing thoughts, unusual euphoria, impulsivity, or extraordinary interpretations that begin to dominate your life, it's sensible to stop intensive practices and consult a mental health professional.

These aren't signs we should automatically interpret as spiritual progress.

And I want to insist on something: the intensity of an experience doesn't prove the depth of our wisdom.

We can learn a great deal from ordinary experiences. Perhaps one of the most important capacities we can cultivate is recognising when we need rest.

## Obstacles outside meditation

So far we've mostly discussed what happens while meditating. But the five hindrances can also help us understand everyday life.

Sensual desire may appear when we constantly check our phones for stimulation. Ill will, when we spend hours mentally replaying an argument. Torpor, when we feel mentally foggy and struggle to pay attention. Restlessness, when we jump from one worry to the next. Doubt, when we can't make a decision because we need impossible guarantees.

That doesn't mean we should label every everyday experience with Buddhist categories or become permanent inspectors of our own minds.

But perhaps the concepts can help us recognise familiar patterns—and investigate what sustains them.

If we find ourselves checking our phones whenever we're bored, perhaps we can explore our relationship with neutral or unstimulating experiences.

If we're trapped in an imaginary argument, we may recognise the thoughts that keep feeding our hostility.

And if we're permanently exhausted, perhaps we need to reconsider our living conditions before increasing our meditation discipline.

The Dharma shouldn't be used to attach new labels to our problems. It can help us understand them—and perhaps respond differently.

## A small experiment: feeding and not feeding

Let's try a simple exercise over the next few days.

You don't need to change your whole practice. Just try to notice when one of the five hindrances appears, whether during meditation or in everyday life.

When you notice it, ask yourself three questions.

**First: what's happening?** Is there desire? Rejection? Sleepiness? Restlessness? Doubt? You don't need to classify the experience perfectly; simply recognise some of its features.

**Second: what seems to be feeding it?** Perhaps you're mentally replaying a story. Perhaps you need rest. Perhaps your attention is caught in an expectation. Or perhaps a particular situation requires action. Don't try to find every cause; one simple observation is enough.

**Third: what might help?** Perhaps returning to the breath, cultivating goodwill, resting, changing posture, seeking information, or taking concrete action.

Then observe what happens.

Does the hindrance weaken? Remain? Change form?

Perhaps you discover that a response that works one day isn't useful the next. That's fine. We're investigating a conditioned system, and its conditions are never exactly the same.

The point is learning to observe without turning every difficulty into a fight.

## The hindrance is not the enemy

We've explored five hindrances that frequently arise during meditation: sensual desire, ill will, sloth and torpor, restlessness and remorse, and doubt.

We've also considered other difficulties, such as obsession with certain experiences and excessive enthusiasm.

Through our model, we can regard all these phenomena as processes that depend on causes and conditions.

We don't need to turn them into personal defects, or assume they'll disappear simply because we understand them intellectually.

We can learn to recognise them, investigate what feeds them, and develop different responses.

Sometimes we'll need more energy. Other times more calm. Sometimes concentration will help. At other times compassion. And there may be moments when the best decision is to stop meditating and attend to a real need.

We don't always need the same tool. Nor do we need to turn practice into a battle.

Perhaps the problem isn't that hindrances arise. Perhaps some additional suffering comes from our relationship with them: demanding that they never appear, interpreting them as failures, and creating a new struggle against our own experience.

We can learn to respond differently—with curiosity, patience, and enough wisdom to recognise when to act and when simply to observe.

A hindrance isn't necessarily something we have to defeat. Sometimes understanding what feeds it and learning to stop adding fuel is enough.

And as we learn to relate to difficulties in this way, perhaps we'll discover something important.

Practice isn't about getting our minds to behave exactly as we want all the time. It's about learning to understand them, care for them, and, when possible, transform the conditions that cause suffering.

In the next chapter, we'll finally leave behind the idea that Dharma has to be practised only while sitting on a cushion.

We'll bring it into everyday life. Into work. Into our relationships. Into conflict. And into those tiny situations that seem designed especially to test every ounce of our wisdom.

Because perhaps that's exactly where we'll discover how much we're learning—and where we'll find even more opportunities to learn.
