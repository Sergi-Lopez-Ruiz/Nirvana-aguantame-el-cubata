# 14. The Noble Eightfold Path

We have a model.

We have explored suffering, karma, ignorance, attachment, emptiness, and nirvana.

We have even questioned some of our favorite ideas about who we are.

Wonderful.

Now what?

Because understanding intellectually how a problem works does not necessarily mean we can solve it.

We can know perfectly well that getting angry rarely improves an argument and still get angry.

We can understand that constantly worrying about the future does not make it more predictable and keep worrying anyway.

And we can read three hundred books about meditation without managing to pay attention to our breathing for thirty seconds.

I speak from experience.

Fortunately, the Buddha did not limit himself to developing explanations of suffering.

He also proposed a path.

The Noble Eightfold Path.

Eight factors that contribute to a more ethical life, a more stable mind, and a deeper understanding of experience.

And here I want to suggest something.

Let's try to understand this path using our model of the mind as a conditioned system.

Not to replace the traditional teachings, but to investigate what we are actually training and why that training can transform how we live.

## Why eight?

The Noble Eightfold Path is the fourth of the Four Noble Truths.

It is the path leading to the cessation of *dukkha*.

Its eight factors are:

1. **Right view** (*sammā-diṭṭhi*): adequately understanding suffering, its causes, its cessation, and the path.
2. **Right intention** (*sammā-saṅkappa*): cultivating intentions of renunciation, non-ill will, and harmlessness.
3. **Right speech** (*sammā-vācā*): avoiding lies, divisive communication, harmful words, and frivolous or useless speech.
4. **Right action** (*sammā-kammanta*): acting ethically, avoiding killing, stealing, and harmful sexual conduct.
5. **Right livelihood** (*sammā-ājīva*): earning a living without harmful or dishonest activities.
6. **Right effort** (*sammā-vāyāma*): preventing and abandoning unwholesome mental states, and developing and maintaining wholesome ones.
7. **Right mindfulness** (*sammā-sati*): cultivating mindful observation of the body, feelings, mind, and phenomena relevant to practice.
8. **Right concentration** (*sammā-samādhi*): developing stability and unification of mind, traditionally associated with the *jhānas*.

The word *sammā* is usually translated as “right,” but we can also understand it as appropriate or fitting.

We are not talking about eight commandments imposed by a divine authority.

We are talking about factors which, according to Buddhist teachings, contribute to liberation from suffering.

Although, let's admit it, eight factors are quite a few.

Especially if we have to remember them while someone is shouting at us for parking where we shouldn't have.

Fortunately, there is another way to organize them.

## Three major trainings

Traditionally, the eight factors of the path are grouped into three broad areas.

**Wisdom (*paññā*):**

- Right view.
- Right intention.

**Ethical conduct (*sīla*):**

- Right speech.
- Right action.
- Right livelihood.

**Concentration or mental discipline (*samādhi*):**

- Right effort.
- Right mindfulness.
- Right concentration.

I find this organization extraordinarily useful.

Because it allows us to understand the path not as a collection of independent rules but as an integrated training.

We learn to understand our experience better.

We learn to act in ways that reduce harm.

And we develop mental abilities that allow us to observe more clearly and respond more steadily.

But I want to suggest an even simpler organization for the practical part of this book.

Three trainings:

Learning to relate with love.

Learning to concentrate.

Learning to investigate our experience.

In the following chapters we will explore them through ethical conduct, *samatha*, and *vipassanā*.

I am not claiming these are exact equivalents of the three traditional categories.

*Samatha* is the cultivation of calm and mental stability; *samādhi* is a broader concept, with specific meanings in certain contexts.

*Vipassanā* is penetrating investigation of experience that contributes to the development of *paññā*, but it is not simply another name for wisdom.

And love, important as it is, does not replace every aspect of *sīla*.

Our proposal is a practical way to organize the training.

A way to make it accessible without needing to immediately learn a huge amount of Buddhist terminology.

## Learning to relate with love

Let's start with ethical conduct.

When we hear the word “ethics,” we may imagine a collection of rules.

Don't do this.

Don't say that.

Behave properly.

And if you make a mistake, feel guilty for a reasonable amount of time.

But I would like to see ethics from a different perspective.

What happens when we learn to relate to ourselves and others through love and compassion?

Imagine someone makes a mistake.

We can respond with contempt.

We can humiliate them.

We can try to demonstrate our superiority.

Or we can recognize that this person is learning, probably does not know all the consequences of their actions, and may need help.

That does not mean we should allow any behavior.

We can set boundaries.

We can express disagreement.

We can protect ourselves and other people.

But we do not need to add hatred to every unpleasant situation.

Through our model, we can investigate how our habitual ways of relating influence subsequent conditions.

If we continuously respond with hostility, we may help create relationships marked by fear, distrust, and conflict.

If we develop habits of listening, honesty, and compassion, we can encourage different dynamics.

We will not always get good results.

Other people's behavior also depends on countless conditions.

But our actions influence the system.

And perhaps even more importantly, they influence us too.

Every time we practice a different response, we develop new possibilities of behavior.

Here we find a deep connection between ethics and wisdom.

The better we understand the conditions influencing people, the easier it may become to stop reducing them to their worst behavior.

And the more we learn to relate with love, the more opportunities we have to listen, understand, and discover things we had not seen before.

Perhaps love and wisdom are not as separate as we tend to imagine.

Personally, I find it increasingly difficult to regard them as two completely independent goals.

## Learning to concentrate

Let's move to the second training.

Concentration.

Imagine you are reading a book.

You begin a paragraph.

Suddenly you remember you need to buy milk.

Then you think about tomorrow's meeting.

Next you recall an unpleasant conversation.

And when you return to the book, you realize you have been staring at the same page for five minutes.

Sound familiar?

Our attention can continually shift from one object to another.

And often it does so without any conscious intention to move it.

*Samatha* practice allows us to train the mind's stability and calm.

We learn to keep attention on an object.

To recognize when we have become distracted.

To return without needing to get angry.

And gradually to develop a more stable relationship with our experience.

When I began meditating, I discovered my mind had an extraordinary capacity for distraction.

In fact, if there were world championships for thinking about irrelevant things while trying to observe your breathing, I suspect I might have qualified.

But over time, something interesting happened.

I began recognizing distractions earlier.

I learned to return.

And gradually developed a mental stability I had never known before.

I also discovered that concentration did not have to be boring or unpleasant.

During my practice, states of well-being and rapture arose, especially related to *pīti*, that profoundly transformed my understanding of meditation.

That taught me something important.

The mind can learn to find calm and satisfaction without constantly depending on external stimulation.

But there is an even more relevant point for our model.

Concentration can help us develop the ability to remain available to what is happening rather than constantly being swept away by every thought or emotion that arises.

That does not mean we should block our emotions.

Nor that we need to become people who never get distracted.

It means we can train abilities that support a more stable relationship with experience.

And that stability can make the next training easier.

## Learning to investigate our experience

Now we come to *vipassanā*.

A Pali word usually translated as insight or clear seeing.

In this book, we will use it to refer to contemplative training that allows us to investigate directly how our experience unfolds.

Here we encounter an interesting difficulty.

We can explain intellectually that our emotions are conditioned.

We can understand that our thoughts arise depending on many factors.

We may even be convinced that our personal identity is a dynamic construction.

But none of that necessarily means we have learned to recognize those processes as they arise.

Imagine someone criticizes you.

Intellectually, you know anger is a conditioned response.

You have read about attachment.

You know the Four Noble Truths.

And perhaps you can even explain the twelve links of dependent origination.

But when you hear the criticism, you get angry.

Very angry.

And for a few moments, all your Buddhist knowledge seems to have gone on vacation.

What happened?

Perhaps we developed an intellectual understanding that has not yet sufficiently transformed certain responses.

*Vipassanā* practice can help us investigate these processes while they happen.

We recognize sensations.

We observe thoughts.

We investigate the arising of desire, rejection, and identification.

And we begin to discover directly some of the relationships we previously knew only through explanations.

From our model's perspective, we can view this practice as a way of developing new conditions that support understanding and transform habits.

Not because observation automatically guarantees change.

But because recognizing a process can change our relationship with it and open up possibilities of response that were not previously available.

And here something seems especially important to me.

Investigation is not about confirming our model. It is about observing what happens, even when it does not match what we expected to find.

If an experience contradicts our ideas, we do not need to ignore it.

We can investigate it.

Perhaps our model is incomplete.

Perhaps our interpretation is wrong.

Or perhaps we do not yet understand well enough what is happening.

That too is part of learning.

## Three trainings that reinforce one another

At this point, it might sound as if we are proposing three different paths.

One for learning to become better people.

Another for developing concentration.

And another for understanding how the mind works.

But our model suggests these capacities can influence one another.

Imagine you have had a conflict with someone.

If you have developed habits of compassion, it may be easier to recognize that person's suffering.

If you have trained mental stability, you may be able to remain attentive without becoming completely trapped in your anger.

And if you have investigated the conditions of your reactions, you may recognize that you are interpreting the situation through a learned habit.

Each capacity can make the others more likely to arise.

Compassion may reduce certain forms of agitation.

Concentration can make clearer observation easier.

Understanding can transform our intentions and our way of acting.

And ethical action can support a mind less disturbed by conflict, remorse, and contradictions.

From systems theory, we could view these relationships as feedback processes that encourage certain wholesome dynamics.

But there is something we need to clarify.

We are not talking about three stages that we must finish one after another.

We do not need perfect ethics before starting to concentrate.

Nor must we master concentration before investigating our experience.

And we do not have to attain definitive understanding before beginning to act with love.

All three trainings can develop together.

Nevertheless, I believe there is a particularly useful educational progression.

Begin by learning to relate to ourselves and others. Then develop mental stability. And, on that foundation, deepen our investigation of experience.

Not because the earlier capacities stop developing.

But because they can provide favorable conditions for those that follow.

From my point of view, it is also a prudent way to begin.

Before seeking extraordinary experiences, we learn to take care of ourselves.

Before intensely investigating mental processes, we develop some stability.

And while deepening our understanding, we keep cultivating love and compassion.

Training continues in all three directions.

## Right effort: learning to cultivate conditions

One factor of the Noble Eightfold Path deserves special attention.

Right effort.

Because it fits our model extraordinarily well.

In the traditional teachings, we find four major tasks associated with this effort:

1. Prevent the arising of unwholesome mental states that have not yet arisen.
2. Abandon unwholesome states that have already arisen.
3. Encourage the arising of wholesome states that have not yet arisen.
4. Maintain and develop wholesome states already present.

Sound familiar?

We are talking about modifying conditions.

If we know certain circumstances favor a harmful reaction, perhaps we can avoid them or prepare better.

If we recognize a reaction is present, we can investigate how to stop feeding it.

If we want to develop a wholesome capacity, we can practice it.

And when that capacity appears, we can encourage the conditions that sustain it.

Imagine you discover that you react with much greater irritability when tired.

You could spend years trying to control your anger through willpower.

Or you could start by sleeping better.

It will not always be enough.

But perhaps you have identified a particularly important condition.

And changing it may have much broader effects than fighting each individual reaction.

Here we find a fundamental difference between effort and violence against ourselves.

Right effort need not become a permanent battle against our mind.

We can develop discipline without hating ourselves.

We can recognize our limitations without resigning ourselves to them.

And we can cultivate wholesome habits without turning every mistake into a personal defeat.

Perhaps one of the most intelligent forms of effort is learning precisely when we need to act and when we need to stop feeding a reaction.

## Right view: a map that also changes

Another particularly interesting factor is right view.

In the Noble Eightfold Path, *sammā-diṭṭhi* occupies a fundamental place.

It relates to understanding the Four Noble Truths and other central teachings of the Dharma.

We might think we must first acquire right view and only then start practicing.

But that would pose a problem.

How can we develop a deep understanding of experience if we have not yet learned to observe it?

And if our understanding changes through practice, what does it mean to have right view from the very beginning?

I think we should distinguish different levels of understanding.

We can begin with a provisional orientation.

For example, considering that our reactions depend on causes and conditions and that some contribute to generating suffering.

This perspective may motivate us to practice.

Then, through practice, we may discover relationships we had not understood before.

And that learning may transform our view.

The new understanding influences how we practice.

And practice continues changing our understanding.

Through our model, we can see this as a self-reinforcing learning process.

We do not need to pretend we know all the answers before beginning.

But neither should we confuse a provisional hypothesis with the fully developed right view of the traditional teachings.

Our model is a tool for investigation.

Not a guarantee of having attained wisdom.

And perhaps an important part of the path is precisely learning to revise our interpretations without having to defend them constantly.

## Mindfulness: remembering that we are practicing

There is another factor that is particularly interesting from our perspective.

*Sati*, commonly translated as mindfulness.

It is often presented as the ability to pay attention to the present moment.

And certainly, that ability is important.

But the term is also connected with remembering or keeping in mind what we need to remember.

In practice, merely observing something for an instant is not enough.

We need to develop the ability to recognize what is happening and remember how we want to relate to it.

Imagine you are trying to learn to respond more calmly when your children get angry.

In the morning, you remember your intention perfectly.

You have reflected on the causes of suffering.

You have meditated.

You have even read an extraordinarily interesting chapter about the Noble Eightfold Path.

Then afternoon arrives.

You are tired.

One of your children starts complaining.

The other decides it is the perfect moment to experiment with the limits of human patience.

And suddenly you have forgotten absolutely everything you wanted to practice.

Welcome to everyday life.

Here we can understand *sati* as a capacity that helps keep our orientation available.

Remembering that we are experiencing a reaction.

Recognizing that we do not have to follow every thought automatically.

And recovering our intention to respond with clarity and compassion.

This is not simply a matter of willpower.

It is a capacity we can train.

And perhaps, over time, remembering will become increasingly natural.

Until certain reactions are recognized before they have had a chance to sweep us away completely.

## The path is conditioned too

There is something I do not want us to forget.

If we provisionally accept our model, the training itself is part of the conditioned system.

Our decision to practice depends on causes and conditions.

Our motivation can change.

Concentration develops through learning processes.

Understanding can change our habits.

And our actions help create new circumstances.

This means we do not need to imagine a completely independent will deciding to transform the mind from outside.

We can use the system's own capacities to modify its tendencies.

We use conditions to transform conditions.

And that seems to me one of the most beautiful possibilities of our perspective.

We do not need to begin as extraordinary people.

We do not need a perfect mind.

Nor definitive understanding.

We can begin with the capacities we have.

Perhaps today we can spend a few minutes paying attention.

Perhaps we can listen to someone more patiently.

Perhaps we can recognize a reaction before turning it into an argument.

And perhaps tomorrow a different opportunity will arise.

Each practice can help develop conditions that make the next practices easier.

We will not always progress in a straight line.

There will be difficult days.

Old habits will reappear.

There will be experiences that contradict our expectations.

But we do not have to turn those difficulties into a sentence against ourselves.

They are also part of what we can investigate.

## What if the path becomes another problem?

There is a particularly curious trap.

We start practicing because we want to suffer less.

But then we build a new image of how we should be.

A person who is always calm.

Always compassionate.

Always mindful.

Able to understand every emotion and respond correctly in every circumstance.

And naturally, we begin to suffer because we are not that person yet.

We have managed to turn the path to liberation into a new source of dissatisfaction.

Congratulations.

Our mind has once again demonstrated its extraordinary creativity.

That is why I want to insist on something.

The Noble Eightfold Path should not become an excuse to hate ourselves for our mistakes.

We can recognize harmful behavior and work to change it.

We can develop discipline.

We can take responsibility.

But we do not need to turn every difficulty into proof that we are not good enough.

From our model's perspective, even the image of a spiritually perfect person can become a conditioned construction that feeds attachment, aversion, and suffering.

Perhaps the path must also include learning to recognize that tendency.

And treating it with a little humor.

Because if, after ten years of meditation, we end up more obsessed with ourselves than when we began, perhaps we should review the instruction manual.

## A small experiment

I would like to suggest a simple exercise to begin exploring the three trainings.

For one day, try to notice three opportunities.

**An opportunity to relate with love.**

It might mean listening to someone without interrupting.

Treating yourself kindly after a mistake.

Helping someone.

Or setting a boundary without needing to humiliate anyone.

Do not look for an extraordinary action.

A small opportunity is enough.

**An opportunity to train concentration.**

Spend a few minutes observing your breathing.

When you notice you have become distracted, recognize what happened and return.

You do not need to fight your thoughts.

Simply practice coming back.

**An opportunity to investigate your experience.**

Choose an everyday reaction.

Perhaps impatience.

Perhaps worry.

Perhaps a little irritation.

Notice which sensations appear, which thoughts accompany the reaction, and which conditions seem to influence it.

You do not need to discover any profound truth.

Simply investigate.

At the end of the day, you can ask yourself:

Has anything changed in the way I relate to what happens?

Perhaps yes.

Perhaps not.

We are not trying to achieve extraordinary results in twenty-four hours.

We are beginning to develop capacities that may accompany us for a long time.

And remember that the three trainings are not independent.

Perhaps concentration allows you to recognize a reaction earlier.

Perhaps that understanding makes a more compassionate response possible.

And perhaps acting compassionately reduces the agitation that previously made concentration difficult.

That is precisely what we want to investigate.

## One path

We have reached the end of our introduction to the Noble Eightfold Path.

Eight factors.

Three major traditional areas.

And our practical proposal to organize training around love, concentration, and investigation of experience.

We are not replacing the Buddha's path with a new system.

We are trying to understand some of his teachings through a perspective that is accessible and useful to us.

From our model's perspective, training can be viewed as a process through which we develop conditions that transform the ways we act, attend, and understand.

Ethical conduct influences our relationships and habits.

Concentration supports a more stable, available mind.

Investigation allows us to recognize processes that previously operated without our being fully aware of them.

And all three capacities can reinforce one another.

We do not need to finish one before starting the next.

But perhaps it is wise to build a foundation of kindness and responsibility, develop mental stability, and gradually move toward deeper investigation.

Not because there is only one valid sequence for everyone.

But because the path must also adapt to the conditions of the person walking it.

And here there is something I consider fundamental.

We are not trying to become perfect people. We are learning to live with greater love and wisdom.

Perhaps along the way we will discover that some things we thought we needed no longer seem so important.

Perhaps we will learn to listen better.

To react with less hostility.

To recognize our emotions without becoming completely trapped in them.

Or perhaps we will discover new questions we cannot yet answer.

All of that can be part of our exploration.

In the coming chapters we will explore each of the three trainings more deeply.

We will begin with love and ethical conduct.

Because before trying to understand the deepest mysteries of consciousness, perhaps it is worth learning to treat each other a little better.

Ourselves.

And others.

The path does not begin when we manage to become different people. It begins with what we do, here and now, with the conditions we have.
