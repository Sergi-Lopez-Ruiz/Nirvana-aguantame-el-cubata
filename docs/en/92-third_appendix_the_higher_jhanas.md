# Appendix — The Higher Jhānas: A Journey to Nowhere

*A hypothesis dreamed up in the shower. Without ever having been there. And without being particularly keen to go.*

I've got good news.

According to the model we've developed in this book, it seems we don't need to reach the higher jhānas to understand the mechanisms that keep suffering going.

Thank goodness for that!

Because, frankly, the prospect sounded like an enormous amount of effort.

Of course, not *needing* to visit them is one thing. Not wondering what the hell happens there is quite another.

So, while taking a shower, I came up with a possible explanation.

**Important disclaimer: I have not personally experienced these states.** What follows is a hypothesis based on our model of the mind as a conditioned system.

I could be completely wrong.

In fact, if someone who has experienced these states can tell me why my interpretation is nonsense, I'd be genuinely grateful.

Here goes.

## 1. Infinite space: where the hell am I?

Imagine we've developed extraordinary concentration and gradually stopped attending to bodily sensations.

The body disappears from our experience.

Not physically, obviously. Relax, we're not talking about magic.

We simply no longer perceive it.

Now what?

My hypothesis is that the mind continues to organise experience around some kind of object.

And when our familiar bodily and spatial reference points disappear, perhaps what remains is precisely a perception of space without boundaries.

**Infinite space.**

Not because we've discovered the true nature of the universe, but because the mind has stopped constructing its usual limits.

First destination reached.

And we haven't even left the house.

## 2. Infinite consciousness: and who's doing the looking?

We go deeper.

Our concentration stabilises. Equanimity grows. And that infinite space, which seemed so extraordinary a moment ago, loses its appeal.

But something remains.

There is awareness of that space.

What if our attention now stops resting on space and turns instead towards the consciousness that knows it?

Perhaps an experience of consciousness without boundaries emerges.

**Infinite consciousness.**

Not necessarily a universal, cosmic or metaphysical consciousness.

Just an experience in which we can no longer find boundaries to what seems to be knowing.

The mind has changed its object.

And we, who came looking for answers, have managed to lose the questions as well.

## 3. Nothingness: well, that was worth it

Onwards.

Now we have infinite consciousness.

Lovely. Vast. Boundless.

But it is still a conditioned experience.

And our system continues to deepen in equanimity.

What happens if we stop attending even to that infinite consciousness?

Perhaps something surprising appears.

**Nothingness.**

Or, more precisely, an experience organised around the absence of what previously occupied our attention.

Not necessarily absolute non-existence.

More like the perception that there is nothing there.

The funny thing is, we're still experiencing something.

Even if that something is precisely *that there's nothing*.

The mind seems to have found a way to turn absence into an object.

Bravo!

We take away all its toys, and it manages to play with the empty box.

## 4. Neither perception nor non-perception: now you see it, now you don't

Here comes the most speculative part.

We've left behind space, infinite consciousness and the perception of nothingness.

But what happens when even that perception of absence loses its stability or relevance?

My intuition is that we might reach a state so subtle that the distinction between perceiving and not perceiving is no longer clear.

Imagine, as a metaphor, a kind of oscillation:

There's something.

There's nothing.

There's something.

There's nothing.

Fainter and fainter.

Until we can no longer say clearly that perception is present, but we can't say it has completely disappeared either.

**Neither perception nor non-perception.**

I don't know whether such an alternation actually takes place. Perhaps perception is simply so faint that our usual categories can no longer capture it.

This is exactly what I'd like to compare with the accounts of people who have experienced this state.

Because perhaps our ability to describe things with concepts is reaching its limit here.

And naturally, we're still trying to describe it.

We're hopeless.

## 5. Nirodha-samāpatti: the show's over

And now for the final stop.

What if even that extraordinarily subtle perception ceased?

Before, metaphorically, we had:

Something. Nothing. Something. Nothing.

What if now it became...?

Nothing.

Nothing.

Nothing.

But here's the catch.

If perception and consciousness really cease, **there is no one left experiencing that nothingness**.

No black screen. No infinite void. No observer enjoying the absence of observations.

The show would simply have stopped.

This would fit the traditional description of *nirodha-samāpatti*, the cessation of perception and feeling.

And here's a fascinating consequence of our model.

A conditioned system could temporarily suspend certain mental processes without ceasing to exist as an organism.

There would be no need to posit a transcendent reality.

Although this hypothesis wouldn't prove that such a reality doesn't exist, either.

The body would still be there.

What would have ceased is conscious experience.

And then something even more interesting happens.

## 6. We're back. What do we find?

Imagine emerging from that state.

Consciousness reappears.

Mental contents begin to become distinguishable.

Gradually, our bodily reference points and ordinary experience return.

The exact sequence would have to be verified. I'm not suggesting that all this can be observed *from within* cessation: if there is no consciousness, there can be no conscious witness to what happens there.

But on returning, perhaps we can recognise something extraordinarily simple.

**Everything we call experience arises dependent on conditions.**

Even consciousness.

Even the subtlest forms of perception.

And perhaps the temporary absence of conscious experience could help us understand just how much our familiar reference points are conditioned constructions.

We wouldn't need to turn the experience into something supernatural.

Nor treat it as definitive proof of our model.

It would simply be another opportunity to investigate.

## 7. And what does any of this have to do with nirvāṇa?

Here's my final thought.

The states we've described seem to represent a progressive reduction in the contents of experience.

But reducing experience is not necessarily the same as eliminating the causes of suffering.

We can stop perceiving the body without eliminating our aversion to pain.

We can experience boundless consciousness without eliminating attachment.

We can experience nothingness without freeing ourselves from ignorance.

Even a temporary cessation of mental activity would not, by itself, demonstrate that the mechanisms producing suffering have disappeared irreversibly.

**The fundamental difference might be between suspending a process and transforming the conditions that produce it.**

And that fits the central proposal of this book.

We're not trying to collect extraordinary experiences.

We're trying to understand what keeps suffering going and how to stop feeding it.

The higher jhānas might offer extraordinarily valuable perspectives.

But perhaps we don't need to turn them into a checklist on the road to liberation.

After all, if the goal is to stop clinging to things, it would be rather ironic to end up clinging to the need to experience every single meditative state.

## 8. An invitation to explorers

I'd like to finish with a request.

If you've experienced any of these jhānas, especially the immaterial attainments or the cessation of perception and feeling, I'd love to hear about it.

Does any of what I've described sound familiar?

Does that possible alternation between perception and its absence actually occur?

How would you describe the transition into the subtler states?

And what can be recognised upon emerging from them?

I'm not looking for anyone to confirm my theory.

I want to understand better.

Because a hypothesis that won't allow itself to be corrected eventually turns into a belief.

And we've already got plenty of those.

There's something especially funny about all this, by the way.

We've built a model to explain how to stop clinging to our mental constructions.

And now we've just built another one.

Luckily, we can let go of this one, too.

**End of the journey.**

And now, if you'll excuse me, I'm getting out of the shower.

The hot water is running out.

---

*Note on sources: the traditional sequence of the four immaterial attainments and cessation appears in the Anupada Sutta (MN 111). The Cūḷasuññata Sutta (MN 121) describes the progressive letting go of objects of attention. The Cūḷavedalla Sutta (MN 44) discusses cessation and the return of mental processes. My proposed explanation of the possible mechanisms is speculative and should not be confused with these teachings.*
