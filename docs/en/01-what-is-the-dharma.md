# 1. What Is the Dharma?

Let's start at the beginning.

What the hell is the Dharma?

Look up the word online and you'll find definitions referring to teachings, universal laws, truths, phenomena, duties and even the order that sustains the universe.

Not bad for a single word.

Dharma is a Sanskrit term, Dhamma in Pali, whose meaning varies according to context.

In Buddhism, it is used mainly to refer to the Buddha's teachings, although it can also refer to the phenomena we experience and the nature of things.

But before we get lost in definitions, I'd like to start with something simpler.

Why would anyone devote their life to understanding how human experience works?

About 2,500 years ago, in northern India, a man we now know as the Buddha asked questions about suffering, its causes and the possibility of freedom from it.

He wasn't the first to ask those questions. He probably wasn't the last to spend a sleepless night thinking about them, either.

But the answer he developed gave rise to an extraordinarily rich tradition that has survived to this day.

And its starting point was profoundly human.

We suffer.

Sometimes because something painful happens to us. Sometimes because we lose what we love.

Because we don't get what we want. Because things change when we'd rather they stayed as they are.

And sometimes we suffer simply because of what our own mind does with what is happening.

Imagine someone says something unpleasant to you.

Perhaps you get angry. Perhaps you feel ashamed. Perhaps you spend the rest of the day replaying the conversation, imagining replies you never gave and anticipating arguments that will probably never happen.

The conversation lasted thirty seconds.

Your mind has been at it for three hours.

And this raises an interesting question:

How much of our suffering comes from what happens, and how much from the way we react to it?

I'm not suggesting that pain is imaginary or that everything depends on our attitude. Terrible circumstances, real losses and forms of suffering exist that won't vanish simply because we think differently.

But it does seem that our reactions can add suffering to pain that is already there.

And if those reactions depend on causes and conditions, perhaps we can understand them.

Perhaps we can even change some of them.

This is one of Buddhism's great practical propositions: suffering has causes, and when certain causes cease to operate, the suffering associated with them can cease too.

The Buddha expressed this proposition through the Four Noble Truths, which we'll examine later.

For now, one idea is enough:

If we better understand the causes of our suffering, perhaps we can learn to suffer less.

But the Dharma doesn't merely offer explanations.

It also proposes a path of practice.

Because understanding intellectually that something harms us doesn't mean we'll stop doing it.

We all know that anger can make us say things we later regret.

And yet we get angry again.

We know that constantly worrying about something we can't change rarely helps much. And yet there we are, still worrying.

It seems that understanding something and learning to live accordingly are two different things.

That's why Buddhist teachings don't stop at knowledge. They also include the cultivation of behaviour, mental capacities and ways of observing experience.

Traditionally, this training is organised around three broad areas: ethical conduct (sīla), mental stability and concentration (samādhi), and wisdom (paññā).

In this book we'll explore these areas through three very simple proposals:

Learning to relate with love. Learning to concentrate. Learning to investigate our experience.

They aren't three stages to be completed one after another. They're capacities that can develop together, reinforcing each other.

And this is where my little reinterpretation begins.

As an engineer, I naturally think in terms of systems.

A system receives influences, responds to them and changes as a consequence of what happens. Its responses depend on its state, its history and the conditions in which it finds itself.

What if we could use this perspective to better understand our minds?

What if many of our problems weren't personal defects, but consequences of conditioned processes we don't yet understand well enough?

Perhaps some of our reactions are habits we've learned over the years. Perhaps others arise when particular circumstances come together. Perhaps even the way we see ourselves affects how we react.

And perhaps we can influence some of those conditions.

This doesn't mean the mind is a simple machine, or that Buddhism can be reduced to systems theory.

It means we're going to use a model to explore some of its teachings.

A model isn't an absolute truth. It's a tool that helps us organise our observations, formulate questions and discover relationships we couldn't see before.

And like any tool, it may be useful for some purposes and insufficient for others.

Over the centuries, different Buddhist traditions have developed different interpretations of the Dharma. Some emphasise meditation, others compassion, philosophical analysis or ritual practices.

We're not going to try to settle all their differences here.

Nor do we need to decide at the outset which metaphysical claims we must accept.

Our starting point will be much more modest.

We have an experience.

Within it arise sensations, thoughts, emotions, desires, memories and intentions.

We can learn to observe some of these processes, investigate how they relate and explore what happens when we change the way we respond.

And we can do so without assuming we already know what we'll find.

Throughout this book we'll use Dharma primarily to mean the body of Buddhist teachings and practices we're trying to understand. Our model will be an interpretation of some of them, not a replacement for the entire tradition.

We don't need to become Buddhists to start investigating our experience.

Nor do we need to believe the Buddha was right about everything.

One question is enough.

What if there were another way to relate to what happens to me?

Let's explore it.
