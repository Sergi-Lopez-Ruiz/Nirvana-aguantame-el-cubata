# 2. Experience: The Only Thing We Can Observe Directly

I'd like to suggest a little experiment.

Look around you.

What do you see?

Perhaps a table, a screen, a window, or a cup of coffee. If you're reading this in the bathroom, I don't need any more details.

Now think about what just happened.

You probably thought you were seeing objects. And of course, that's a perfectly reasonable way to describe it.

But if we get a little more demanding, what you're experiencing is colours, shapes, contrasts, and visual sensations that your brain organises and interprets.

You're not directly experiencing a table. You're having a visual experience that you recognise as a table.

And of course, you can use it to hold a cup, get some work done, or stub your little toe against it. The fact that experience is a representation doesn't mean the outside world is imaginary.

My little toe can testify to that.

## A World We Experience

Our relationship with the world always passes through experience.

We see, hear, smell, taste, feel, and think.

From these experiences, we build an interpretation of what is happening.

For example, imagine you're walking down the street and someone greets you.

You recognise a face, remember who that person is, perhaps feel something pleasant, and decide to greet them back.

All of this can happen in a fraction of a second.

But something else could happen, too.

Perhaps that person reminds you of an unpleasant argument. Their face is the same, but your experience changes completely.

Tension arises in your body. Perhaps discomfort. Maybe you remember something they said three years ago that, for some reason, your mind considers absolutely essential to revisit at that precise moment.

The person hasn't even opened their mouth yet.

And you're already arguing with them.

What's interesting is that a considerable part of what we experience depends not only on what happens outside us, but also on the conditions we carry within.

Our memories, expectations, habits, emotions, and bodily states all take part in constructing our experience.

This doesn't mean we can freely choose what to experience.

It means our experience depends on far more things than we usually acknowledge.

## My First Big Surprise

When I started meditating, I expected to find peace.

I suppose that's what almost everyone expects.

You sit down, close your eyes, breathe calmly, and, with a little luck, begin to resemble one of those smiling people in photographs of spiritual retreats.

My mind had other plans.

During the early stages of my practice, I discovered that inner experience could change in ways I had never imagined.

One of my most surprising discoveries was something known in the Buddhist tradition as *pīti*.

The word is often translated as rapture, joy, or meditative exhilaration. In my case, it took the form of a physical sensation of energy and well-being that could become extraordinarily intense.

I'm not simply talking about thinking of something pleasant.

It was a tangible bodily experience that could arise and spread through the body during meditation.

Over time, I learned to encourage it to arise and, under certain conditions, could access it relatively easily.

I was fascinated.

It turned out my body could produce extraordinary sensations of well-being without anything special needing to happen in the outside world.

I hadn't won the lottery.

Nobody had given me a promotion.

I hadn't even managed to get my children to put away their toys without complaining.

And yet, there was that joy.

This profoundly changed the way I understood emotions.

I began wondering how much of what we call happiness, sadness, fear, or excitement depends on the processes taking place inside us.

I hadn't discovered a magic happiness button. But I had found something worth investigating.

Above all, I had discovered that experience was far more malleable than I had imagined.

## And Who Is Experiencing All This?

This is where things start getting interesting.

We normally think there is an “I” who experiences things.

I see.

I hear.

I think.

I feel.

It seems pretty obvious.

But when we start observing our experience carefully, some awkward questions arise.

Where does a thought come from?

Who decides which emotion appears?

At what point do we choose to feel afraid, get angry, or remember that stupid thing we said at a dinner party fifteen years ago?

If we observe closely, we discover that many experiences arise without our feeling that we chose them.

Thoughts arise.

Emotions appear.

Sensations change.

And the sense of being someone who is living through all this can change, too.

During my meditation practice, I had experiences in which my usual sense of being a separate “I” changed profoundly.

At times, the distinction between the observer and what was being observed seemed to blur.

Some traditions associate such experiences with non-duality.

For quite a long time, I gave them special importance. They seemed like clues that I was discovering something profound about the nature of reality.

Until I began asking myself another question.

What if those experiences were conditioned, too?

What if the disappearance of our usual sense of separation didn't prove that we'd discovered an ultimate reality, but simply showed that our experience could be organised in a different way?

I admit that possibility was a little disappointing.

The magic had been so lovely!

But it was also liberating.

I didn't need to deny those experiences. They had happened, and they had been important to me.

What I needed was to distinguish the experience from my interpretation of it.

Experiencing something extraordinary doesn't necessarily prove that our explanation of what happened is correct.

And that applies to meditation just as much as to any other part of life.

## Experience Is Not an Explanation

Imagine you're dreaming.

In a dream, you can see people, hear conversations, feel fear or joy, and even believe you're flying.

While you're dreaming, the experience can seem completely real.

But when you wake up, you realise that your interpretation of what was happening was mistaken.

That doesn't mean you experienced nothing.

You experienced something.

What changed was your way of understanding it.

Something similar can happen when we meditate, experience very intense emotions, or go through things that don't fit our expectations.

Our brain tries to explain what is happening.

And those explanations may be useful, mistaken, or simply incomplete.

Experience is what we encounter when we observe.

Interpretation is what we think about what we have encountered.

Both are part of our mental life, but it's worth not confusing them.

Even the claim that the outside world exists independently of us is a conclusion that goes beyond what we experience directly.

An extraordinarily useful conclusion, by the way. I recommend continuing to look before crossing the street.

We don't need to solve every philosophical problem about the nature of reality to learn something from our experience.

We can leave those questions open and keep investigating.

## Learning to Observe

Here we encounter an important difficulty.

When we observe our experience, we aren't completely neutral observers.

We have expectations.

We have beliefs.

We have habits of attention.

And sometimes we desperately want to prove we're right.

Me included.

That's why observing isn't simply about paying attention to what happens.

It also means learning to recognise our interpretations, our reactions, and our tendency to turn every discovery into a new certainty.

For example, if you notice an unpleasant sensation in your body, you can observe it.

But perhaps a thought appears, too:

“This is anxiety.”

And then another:

“I shouldn't feel anxious.”

And then:

“After all the meditation I've done, I should have got over this by now.”

In a matter of seconds, we've gone from an unpleasant sensation to a small personal tragedy.

The curious thing is that each of those thoughts is also part of experience.

We can observe them.

And perhaps discover that we don't need to believe them, follow them, or fight them.

This doesn't mean we should ignore our emotions or become passive spectators of our own lives.

It means we can learn to recognise what is happening before reacting automatically.

And that possibility is one of the starting points of meditation practice.

## A Little Experiment

Before we continue, I suggest spending a minute observing your experience.

You don't need to sit in the lotus position.

In fact, if you've never practised that posture, it's probably a splendid way to discover just how conditioned your experience is by knee pain.

Just stop for a moment.

Notice what you're experiencing.

Perhaps you hear a sound.

Perhaps you notice your body against the chair.

Perhaps a thought appears.

Or perhaps you catch yourself wondering how much longer this exercise will last.

Don't try to change anything.

Observe what arises.

And, if you can, distinguish the sensation from what you think about it.

For example:

“There's tension in my chest.”

That's a description of a sensation.

“I'm nervous because everything is going to go wrong tomorrow.”

That's an interpretation that includes memories, expectations, and predictions.

You don't need to reject either one.

Simply recognise the difference.

If you've discovered during this exercise that your mind won't stop thinking, congratulations.

You've just confirmed that you have a mind.

It may not seem like a spectacular discovery, but we had to start somewhere.

## The Starting Point

Throughout this book, we'll use experience as our main field of investigation.

Not because it's infallible.

Not because it allows us to know the whole of reality directly.

And certainly not because our interpretations are always correct.

But because it is what we have immediate access to when we try to observe what's happening.

We'll also draw on shared knowledge, reasoning, traditional teachings, and scientific discoveries to test our ideas.

Individual experience is valuable, but it need not be our only source of knowledge.

In fact, one of the most interesting things we can do is compare what we think we're experiencing with what other people have discovered.

Our aim won't be to find ever more extraordinary experiences.

It will be to learn to understand ordinary and extraordinary experiences alike: pleasant and unpleasant, those we understand and those that leave us completely baffled.

Because perhaps we don't need to change the world to begin changing the way we live in it.

And perhaps the first step is as simple as discovering that what we experience and what we believe about our experience are not quite the same thing.

In the next chapter, we'll explore an idea that will stay with us throughout the book: that our mind, like our experience, depends on causes and conditions.

And that understanding those conditions can open possibilities we hadn't even imagined before.
