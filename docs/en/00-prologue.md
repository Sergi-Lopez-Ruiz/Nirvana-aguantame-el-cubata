# Prologue — Another Book About Awakening?

I am a lucky person. Very lucky.

For a long time I didn't know it. Or perhaps I did, but not in the way I know it now.

I've had many reasons to feel grateful, even though I haven't always known how to recognise them.

I've also been through difficult times. I've suffered, made mistakes and hurt people I love. Some of the things that have taught me the most are precisely the ones I would have preferred never to experience.

I don't have a perfect life. Nor do I have a perfect mind. And fortunately, some time ago I stopped seeing either of those things as a requirement for living well.

I've been lucky enough to meet people who have loved me, stood by me, taught me and, at times, told me things I didn't want to hear. Many of the ideas you'll find here wouldn't exist without them.

And after all that, here I am, writing a book about the Buddha's Dharma.

Who would have thought?

I don't expect to change the world. I don't even expect to change anyone. I want to share a way of understanding human experience that has helped me live better, understand myself and relate to others differently.

Perhaps you'll find it useful. Perhaps you won't.

I don't know.

But that is the intention behind this book.

Before we go on, there's something I'd like to tell you.

You have worth.

Not because you're extraordinary, because you've achieved something important or because you possess some special quality. Nor because you're better than anyone else.

You have worth, just as everyone does.

We're all different. Each of us is the outcome of an extraordinarily complex history of causes, circumstances, experiences and relationships. No two histories are alike.

And perhaps, as we begin to understand how we came to be who we are, it becomes a little easier to treat ourselves with kindness.

I'd like to invite you to love yourself without reservations. Even those parts of yourself you don't much like. Especially those.

You don't need to become someone else to deserve your own love.

That doesn't mean you can't change. In fact, much of this book is about precisely how we change.

But there is an enormous difference between wanting to change because we reject ourselves and wanting to change because we love ourselves.

And now, before you start suspecting you've bought yet another self-help book, I have a few confessions to make.

The first is that I didn't write this book alone.

I've used artificial intelligence. A lot.

It has helped me reflect, organise ideas, find contradictions, explore interpretations and write these pages. We've discussed Buddhism, philosophy, systems theory and some of the hardest questions I've been able to formulate.

Sometimes I've changed my mind during those conversations. Other times I've ended up even more confused than when we started. And I suspect the latter isn't necessarily a bad thing.

I'd like to say I wrote every word myself, but that wouldn't be true. I feel this book is the result of teamwork. A rather peculiar team, considering that one of its members doesn't even need to sleep.

Besides, let's be honest: I'm far too lazy to write so many pages in so little time.

The experiences that inspired this book are mine. So are many of the questions, interpretations and decisions about what is worth telling. Artificial intelligence has helped me develop and express them.

But responsibility for what you're reading, including the mistakes, is mine.

My second confession is that I don't think I've discovered anything new.

About 2,500 years ago, a man we now know as the Buddha devoted his life to understanding human suffering and teaching a path to liberation from it.

Since then, countless people have studied, practised, passed on, debated and reinterpreted his teachings. My understanding owes an immense debt to all of them, even those I disagree with.

I've simply decided to contribute my small part.

I'm a computer engineer, and I have a tendency to try to understand things through models. I like to ask how they work, what elements are involved, how they relate to one another and what happens when we change some of their conditions.

And one day I began looking at the Buddha's teachings in that way.

What if we viewed the mind as a conditioned system? A system that reacts continuously, in which each experience depends on multiple causes and each reaction can help create the conditions for those that follow.

What if our habits, emotions, intentions and even that familiar sense of being an “I” could be understood from this perspective?

And, above all, what could we do with that knowledge?

The more I explored these questions, the more connections I found with Buddhist teachings.

Concepts I had previously struggled to understand began to take on a different meaning.

I'm not claiming that this is the correct interpretation of the Dharma. I'm not even sure it's the best one.

It's a model. A way of looking.

And like every model, it has its limitations.

In fact, while writing this book I've had to reconsider some ideas I'd attached considerable importance to for years. It hasn't always been comfortable. Sometimes it's been fun. Occasionally it's been both at once.

I've learned to be a little suspicious of that wonderful feeling of having finally understood something once and for all.

A few years from now, I may reread these pages and discover that I was wrong about important things.

I hope I'll then have the honesty to acknowledge it. And, if possible, laugh a little.

I don't want you to believe what I'm about to tell you simply because you've read it here. Nor do I want you to exchange one set of beliefs for another, even if the new ones seem much more sophisticated.

I want to invite you to explore.

To observe your experience, ask questions, experiment and discover for yourself what you find.

If something in this book helps you better understand your own experience, wonderful.

If it helps you question something you took for granted, even better.

And if you end up questioning this very book, perhaps we've done a good job.

You don't need to be a Buddhist, meditate for hours or accept any metaphysical explanation to begin. Nor do you have to be suffering, seeking personal transformation or pursuing something called enlightenment.

A little curiosity is enough.

And one last thing: don't mistake the map for the territory. However much we like our model, experience will always be richer, more complex and more surprising than any explanation we try to construct of it.

I don't know what you'll find in this book.

But I'd like you to finish it wanting to look.
