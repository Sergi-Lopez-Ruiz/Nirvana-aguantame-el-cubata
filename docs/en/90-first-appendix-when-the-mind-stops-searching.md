# First Appendix — When the Mind Stops Searching

*On craving, equanimity, and the possibility of learning not to suffer more than necessary*

## The wrong question

Let us imagine the Buddha for a moment, after learning to enter deep states of concentration. The *jhānas* offer calm, pleasure, clarity and, in their most refined forms, equanimity. They are extraordinary experiences. But there is one small drawback: they end.

We do not know whether the Buddha framed his search in precisely these terms, but we can use a question as our starting point: **how can we preserve forever the well-being that arises under particular conditions?**

The question contains a trap. If a state depends on conditions, it will change when those conditions change. We cannot manufacture a permanently unconditioned state out of conditioned parts. However much we improve the engine, it will still need fuel.

Perhaps a more useful question is this: **what if, rather than making well-being permanent, we could stop suffering when that well-being comes to an end?**

This changes the problem completely. It is no longer a matter of preserving an experience, but of understanding and transforming the mechanisms that turn its changing into suffering.

Buddhist traditions speak of *nirvāṇa* and, in some cases, of “the unconditioned.” Our purpose here is not to settle that metaphysical question or claim that this interpretation exhausts the Buddha’s teachings. We will explore a more modest hypothesis: that a thoroughly conditioned system can learn **not to feed certain processes that generate suffering**.

## The cost of resisting experience

Consider two people who receive unfair criticism.

The first is upset. Then a story appears: “They shouldn't treat me like that.” They remember previous criticism, imagine future arguments, try to prove to themselves that they are right, and end up spending the afternoon trapped in the incident.

The second is upset too. They acknowledge the unpleasantness, consider whether a response is needed, and, once the matter has been dealt with, get on with their life.

The difference is not necessarily in the first sensation. It lies in **everything the system constructs afterwards**.

From a functional perspective, the second system seems more efficient: not because it always uses less energy in a physiological sense—we would have to measure that—but because it devotes fewer resources to activity that does not help it respond to the situation. It retains sensitivity, judgment and the capacity to act, while reducing rumination and additional suffering.

The *Sallattha Sutta* (SN 36.6) explains this with the image of two arrows. The initial pain may be unavoidable; we do not always need to shoot ourselves with a second arrow by resisting it.

But what mechanism fires that second arrow?

## The thirst for something less to be missing

In Pali, *taṇhā* means “thirst.” It is usually translated as desire, longing or craving. The image is more precise than many translations: thirst is not merely a preference. **It drives us to seek what we believe will satisfy us**.

We can want a coffee without suffering over it. We can wish to learn, embrace someone or put right an injustice. The problem seems to begin elsewhere: when the present experience feels insufficient. “Not like this. Something is missing. I need something else to be okay.”

And so the search begins: change the moment, obtain something, escape discomfort, hold on to pleasure, find a definitive explanation, finally become someone who no longer has problems.

Sometimes the search works. We get what we wanted and feel relief. But the relief lasts only as long as its conditions do. Later, another sense of insufficiency appears, another promise, another search.

We can imagine the cycle like this:

**Experience → sense of insufficiency → seeking or rejection → temporary relief → renewed seeking.**

This is not a complete canonical definition of *taṇhā*, but a proposed functional interpretation. And it raises a surprising possibility: **perhaps liberation is not about finding what will finally satisfy us, but about no longer needing something to satisfy us all the time**.

Not every preference or intention would disappear. What would disappear—or become profoundly weaker—is the demand that the present must be different before we can be okay.

## Deeper still: vedanā

There is a difficulty with that explanation. In dependent origination, the Buddha places *vedanā* before *taṇhā*. *Vedanā* is the hedonic tone of experience: pleasant, unpleasant or neutral.

Perhaps the sense of insufficiency is not the deepest root. Perhaps we need to look at what happens **between feeling something unpleasant and needing it to disappear**.

Imagine a small frustration arising. We can distinguish three phenomena:

1. The unpleasantness itself: “This is unpleasant.”
2. The reaction: “I want this to end; this shouldn't be happening.”
3. The subsequent elaboration: “Why does this always happen to me?”

They are not necessarily one and the same thing. Unpleasantness can arise without compelling us to reject it, and rejection can arise without our having to obey it.

This leads to a decisive question: if someone feels discomfort without being caught up in it, **has *vedanā* been neutralized, or has the system's relationship with *vedanā* changed?**

These are different hypotheses. The first assumes that the tone of experience becomes less intense. The second allows sensations to remain pleasant or unpleasant without automatically setting off pursuit or escape.

Buddhism is familiar with this distinction. In classical descriptions of the *jhānas*, equanimity can coexist with pleasure; in the fourth *jhāna*, by contrast, a tone that is neither pleasant nor unpleasant is described alongside equanimity. **Equanimity and neutrality are not synonyms**.

## Equanimity is not anesthesia

Enter *upekkhā*: equanimity.

We sometimes imagine an equanimous person as someone who is unaffected by anything. But a mind that fails to register what is happening is not necessarily wise. If we stop feeling discomfort when we put our hand near a flame, we have a rather serious problem.

We can formulate a different hypothesis: **equanimity does not eliminate signals; it reduces the compulsion to react to them**.

Criticism can hurt. Loss can make us sad. Injustice can provoke anger. What changes is our ability to recognize these responses without adding a struggle against their very existence.

“There is anger” need not become “I shouldn't get angry.” And “there is fear” does not oblige us to conclude “I'm a coward.”

Perhaps equanimity and craving form a feedback dynamic:

**Greater equanimity → less reactivity to the tone of experience → less compulsive seeking → greater stability → more favorable conditions for equanimity.**

This is not a proven equation but a model we can compare with experience. It may also work in reverse: tiredness, stress or overload can weaken equanimity and make it easier for old habits to regain their strength.

This helps explain why *samatha*, the training of calm and concentration, can produce deep tranquility that subsequently fades. The conditions sustaining it change. Yet it is possible that *vipassanā*, the investigation of experience, contributes to a more lasting change in our relationship with sensations and reactions.

These are not two separate worlds. Calm can facilitate observation, and understanding can change what disturbs calm.

## When acceptance stops being a task

There is a huge difference between two ways of responding to an unpleasant impulse.

In the first, anger arises and someone tells themselves: “I have to accept this. I mustn't react. I have to be equanimous.” It might work, but it takes effort. Sometimes it even adds another struggle: trying to become the kind of person who never gets angry.

In the second, anger arises and is recognized almost immediately. The reaction is not condemned. It is understood to have arisen from causes and conditions. Its presence is allowed, and its impulse is not necessarily acted upon.

Acceptance seems spontaneous.

This could indicate something important: **what once required deliberate effort is beginning to function as a habit**. Mindfulness (*sati*) detects what is happening; understanding (*paññā*) recognizes its conditioned nature; equanimity (*upekkhā*) allows us not to fight it; and a different response becomes possible.

We do not need to introduce a tiny director hidden inside the head to control all this. Even the decision not to follow an impulse arises from causes and conditions: learning, memory, attention, values and habits participating in the system's operation.

The system remains conditioned. What changes is **its organization**.

One qualification, though: a response that seems spontaneous is not proof of irreversibility. Stability is observed over time and in different circumstances; it cannot be inferred from one particularly peaceful afternoon.

## Can ignorance become wisdom?

We arrive at an even more ambitious hypothesis.

By ignorance, *avijjā*, we mean something deeper than lacking certain facts. In our model, it is a mode of operation in which the system treats its own constructions as unquestionable realities and uses them to fuel further reactions.

An emotion arises and becomes an identity: “That's just how I am.” An expectation arises and becomes an obligation: “This has to happen.” An idea arises and becomes certainty: “Now I've finally understood how everything works.”

But what happens if the system learns to recognize these constructions as they arise?

Ignorance may continue to produce interpretations. Yet those interpretations are no longer automatically granted full authority. They are recognized as conditioned phenomena: sometimes useful, sometimes mistaken, never beyond revision.

**Recognizing ignorance is already a form of wisdom.**

And if that recognition becomes habitual, perhaps wisdom need not intervene like a police officer chasing and punishing thoughts. It can become part of the mind's spontaneous functioning.

We would not need to construct a perfect mind. It would be enough—at least for this hypothesis—if its mistakes stopped so readily dragging it towards suffering and actions that multiply it.

But the idea becomes even more interesting when we apply it to wisdom itself.

## Wisdom can be mistaken too

If every interpretation we make depends on conditions, our model of the Dharma depends on conditions too. The conclusion “everything is conditioned” is, at the very least, a conditioned mental formulation.

So we can ask: **what happens when the system no longer needs even its wisdom to be infallible?**

We can understand a great deal and still be wrong about something. We can experience profound clarity and later discover that we mistook an experience for a metaphysical explanation. We can relinquish a belief without having to replace it immediately with another.

Wisdom, understood functionally, would not mean possessing the final answer, but **being able to revise our answers without experiencing that revision as a personal threat**.

This does not mean every interpretation is equally good. Some predict events better, explain more phenomena, produce fewer contradictions and help us act with greater sensitivity. That is precisely why we should test them.

In Sanskrit, there is a solemn expression for the awakening of a Buddha: *anuttarā-samyak-saṃbodhi*, “complete and unsurpassed awakening.” It is an important formulation within Buddhist traditions, but we must not confuse the name of a goal with an explanation of its mechanisms. Calling something “perfect” does not entitle us to invent a perfect mind.

Even our best ideas must be able to take a seat at the table of inquiry. No crown. No bodyguards. And, if necessary, no dessert.

## So what would liberation be?

We could try out a provisional definition:

**Liberation would be a transformation of the system through which experiences, including unpleasant ones, cease to compulsively activate or feed the processes that produce additional suffering.**

This is not the same as ceasing to feel. It does not guarantee that anger, preference, sadness or mistakes will never arise. Nor does it automatically make anyone incapable of acting badly. An understanding must also be tested against its consequences in life and relationships.

In our interpretation, such a mind could still react, but would recognize its reactions more quickly; it could experience discomfort without needing to turn it into a war; it could make mistakes without needing to defend each one as though its life depended on it.

Would that be the *nirvāṇa* the Buddha spoke of? We do not know. Tradition makes broader and sometimes different claims. Our model is not meant to replace them: it attempts to offer a functional explanation that can be examined without requiring allegiance to a particular metaphysics.

Nor do we know whether all these changes happen at once, gradually, reversibly or with varying degrees of stability. That uncertainty does not invalidate the inquiry. It keeps it honest.

## We shall see

Sometimes the mind becomes calm because everything is going well. Sometimes because we are concentrated. And perhaps, on certain occasions, because **it has stopped desperately searching for something to cling to**.

The difference is not always visible from the outside. Life goes on: tasks, disagreements, tiredness, people to love and problems to solve. Except that, for moments at a time, experience no longer needs to justify itself.

It is tempting to announce that we have arrived. It is also tempting to measure compulsively how far we still have to go. Both can feed precisely the search we were trying to understand.

Perhaps it is enough to observe, to live, and to allow time to test our interpretations.

A conditioned mind can learn to recognize its conditioning. It can learn not to fight everything that arises. And it can even learn not to cling to its explanation of how it managed to do so.

Will it last? Will it run deep? Will it have limits we cannot yet see?

**We shall see.**

And for once, those words need not conceal another search.
