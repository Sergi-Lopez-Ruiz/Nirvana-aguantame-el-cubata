# 16. Concentration: Learning to Be Present (samatha)

Let me suggest something.

For the next thirty seconds, try to pay attention exclusively to your breathing.

Don't think about anything else.

Don't remember what you have to do tomorrow.

Don't wonder whether you're breathing correctly.

And, of course, don't think about a pink elephant.

Done?

How did it go?

If you managed to maintain your attention for thirty seconds, wonderful.

If you didn't, welcome to the club.

And if you're now thinking about a pink elephant, perhaps we should have a conversation about how instructions work.

The curious thing is that we usually assume we're capable of directing our attention.

We decide to think about something.

We decide to concentrate.

We decide to stop thinking about something else.

But when we look at what actually happens, we discover that our minds have an extraordinary capacity to busy themselves with things we hadn't planned.

And that raises an interesting question.

If our attention is conditioned, can we learn to transform the conditions that determine where it goes?

This is where our second training begins.

## What is samatha?

Samatha is a Pali term commonly translated as calm, tranquility, or serenity.

In Buddhism it is associated with developing a stable, tranquil mind, capable of sustaining attention without continually being carried away by distractions.

But I think it would be a mistake to reduce it simply to learning concentration.

Concentration is an important part of the training, but not the only one.

We also cultivate tranquility, clarity, well-being, and equanimity.

In some forms of practice, these qualities can develop into states of meditative absorption known as *jhānas*.

And here I want to make something clear from the outset.

You don't have to reach extraordinary states to benefit from meditation.

You don't have to sit for hours.

You don't have to adopt impossible postures.

And you certainly don't have to turn yourself into a Buddha statue.

Let's start with something much simpler.

Learning to relate to our attention.

## Attention and awareness

There is a distinction that helped me enormously during my early years of practice.

I found it particularly well explained in Culadasa's *The Mind Illuminated*.

The book distinguishes between attention and peripheral awareness.

Imagine you're reading these words.

Your attention is directed mainly toward the text.

But at the same time, perhaps you're aware of the temperature in the room.

You can hear sounds around you.

Perhaps you notice your body touching the chair.

And if someone says your name, you'll probably recognize it even though you're absorbed in reading.

Not all the information in our experience occupies the center of attention.

Some of it remains available in a broader awareness.

In Culadasa's model, meditation training isn't simply about narrowing attention more and more until everything else is excluded.

It also involves developing clear awareness of the context in which attention operates.

I find that particularly important.

Because we can concentrate intensely on something while losing sight of important aspects of what is happening.

Imagine you're arguing with someone.

Your attention becomes completely absorbed in finding arguments that prove you're right.

You're concentrating incredibly hard.

Perhaps harder than in any meditation session you've ever had.

But you've stopped noticing the tension in your body.

You're not really listening to the other person.

And perhaps you've even forgotten why the argument started.

Concentration alone doesn't guarantee wisdom.

We also need to develop the ability to recognize what is happening.

That's why I like to think of samatha as a training that supports a stable and available mind.

Not merely a mind capable of obsessively fixing on one object.

## A mind that learns to return

Let's imagine a very simple meditation session.

You sit comfortably.

Close your eyes, if that feels pleasant.

And decide to observe your breathing.

You notice the air coming in.

You notice the air going out.

For a few seconds everything seems to work perfectly.

Until a thought appears.

“I need to buy milk.”

Then another.

“Have we run out of laundry detergent?”

And then:

“Why am I always the one who has to remember this stuff?”

Before you know it, you've spent three minutes mentally organizing an expedition to the supermarket.

What happened?

Your attention changed objects.

You didn't necessarily decide to do that.

Conditions simply arose that encouraged a distraction.

Perhaps a memory.

An association.

A worry.

Or a thought that seemed more interesting than the sensations of breathing.

From our model's perspective, this isn't some mysterious failure of willpower.

It's a conditioned phenomenon.

And here's one of the most important things I learned from Culadasa.

The moment we discover we've been distracted isn't failure. It's an opportunity to strengthen our ability to notice and recover attention.

Think about it.

For a while, you were distracted.

Then the recognition of that distraction arose.

And now you can return to your meditation object.

That recognition is precisely one of the capacities we're trying to develop.

What happens if we respond by getting angry?

“I've got distracted again.”

“I'm no good at this.”

“It's been ten minutes and I still can't concentrate.”

We've just introduced frustration and rejection into a practice meant to develop tranquility.

A rather questionable strategy.

But what happens if we acknowledge the distraction with a different attitude?

“Ah, I got distracted.”

And return.

Without getting angry.

Perhaps even with a little satisfaction at having recognized it.

We're encouraging different conditions.

And through repetition, that way of responding can become a habit.

## Training is conditioned too

Let's return to our model.

The mind is a system of interdependent processes.

Attention shifts depending on many conditions.

Thoughts, emotions, sensory stimuli, and habits influence what occupies the center of our experience.

So what are we doing when we meditate?

We introduce new conditions.

We choose an object of attention.

We develop the intention to remain with it.

We learn to recognize distractions.

And we practice returning.

Over time, these activities can change our ability to sustain stable attention.

We're not forcing the mind to obey through some independent will.

We're training the system itself to develop new tendencies.

There's something about this I find especially interesting.

At first, maintaining attention may take considerable effort.

But with practice, some abilities can become progressively more automatic.

As happens when we learn to drive.

At first we need to pay conscious attention to an enormous number of details.

The steering wheel.

The pedals.

The mirrors.

The traffic signs.

And that mysterious ability some drivers possess to appear in precisely the place we least expect them.

Over time, many of these tasks become more natural.

Not because we've stopped being conditioned.

But because we've developed conditions that encourage more effective responses.

Something similar may happen in meditation.

Attentional stability, recognizing distractions, and returning can develop until they require less effort.

And that brings us to an interesting paradox.

Part of the training involves learning to make an effort in such a way that, gradually, less effort is needed.

## The five hindrances

Naturally, practice isn't always easy.

Buddhism describes five major hindrances, or *nīvaraṇas*, that can obstruct the development of concentration and clarity.

They are:

**Sensual desire.** The mind seeks pleasant experiences it finds more interesting than the meditation object.

**Ill will or aversion.** Rejection arises toward an experience, person, situation, or even meditation itself.

**Sloth and torpor.** The mind loses energy and clarity.

**Restlessness and remorse.** Agitation, worry, or repetitive thoughts arise.

**Doubt.** We question the practice, our abilities, or the point of what we're doing.

If you've ever meditated, you've probably met several of them.

Perhaps all five have even appeared in a single session.

Especially if you decided to meditate after lunch, you're worried about work, and someone has just told you that meditation is a waste of time.

From our model's perspective, we can view these hindrances as sets of conditions that make particular capacities more difficult.

But the interesting thing isn't to declare them enemies.

It's learning to recognize them.

If sleepiness appears, perhaps we need to rest.

If we're restless, reducing stimulation or developing a kinder attitude may help.

If aversion arises, perhaps we're trying to force our experience to meet conditions that aren't being fulfilled.

And if doubt appears, perhaps we need to understand better what we're practicing and why.

Not all difficulties have the same solution.

And we can't always solve them by trying harder.

Sometimes we need energy.

Sometimes tranquility.

And sometimes the wisest thing is to stop trying to meditate heroically when what our bodies actually need is sleep.

Meditation shouldn't become a contest against our physiological needs.

## When joy appeared

Now I'd like to share a personal experience that profoundly changed my understanding of meditation.

During my early years of practice, especially following Culadasa's teachings, I began discovering that concentration could produce experiences very different from those I expected.

Initially, I imagined that meditation was about finding tranquility.

A silent mind.

A pleasant experience, perhaps a little boring.

But over time something appeared that I hadn't anticipated.

A physical sensation of well-being and energy that could become extraordinarily intense.

In Buddhism, there's a term related to this kind of experience: *pīti*.

It's often translated as rapture, joy, or meditative exhilaration.

In my case, it wasn't simply thinking I was happy.

It was a bodily experience.

A sense of energy, well-being, and joy that could spread through the body.

What surprised me most was that through practice, I began recognizing some of the conditions that helped it arise.

And gradually, I learned to evoke it relatively easily.

I was fascinated.

It turned out that I could experience extraordinary well-being without anything special happening outside.

I didn't need to win the lottery.

I didn't need to buy a new car.

I didn't even need to get my children to tidy their room without complaining.

I could simply sit quietly and experience joy.

And that profoundly changed my relationship with emotions.

Because it became clear that our bodily and mental states play an extraordinarily important role in the experience of well-being.

I hadn't discovered a magical mechanism guaranteeing happiness.

But I had discovered an ability I hadn't known I possessed.

And it taught me something fundamental.

Meditation need not be a bleak path based exclusively on discipline and effort. It can also be a deeply joyful path.

## Pīti: the body is involved too

There's something I want to emphasize about pīti.

It reminds us that meditation isn't an activity performed by a mind separate from the body.

Human experience includes bodily sensations, emotions, thoughts, perceptions, and physiological processes that are deeply interconnected.

When we develop certain forms of concentration, we may experience changes in breathing, body perception, and feeling tone.

Some people experience pleasant sensations.

Others experience warmth, vibrations, lightness, or a sense of energy.

But not everyone experiences pīti in the same way.

And we shouldn't take my experience as a universal description of what must happen.

The important point is recognizing that concentration can transform much more than our ability to pay attention.

It can influence how we relate to our bodies and emotions.

This becomes particularly interesting when we think of the mind as a conditioned system.

Certain mental conditions can influence bodily experiences.

And certain bodily conditions can influence mental states.

We're not talking about two completely separate worlds.

We're investigating a profoundly interdependent system.

But we must avoid another mistake.

We don't need to chase pīti.

If it appears, we can learn from the experience.

If it doesn't, practice can still be extraordinarily useful.

And if we become obsessed with obtaining it, perhaps we'll end up developing exactly the attachment we were trying to understand.

Which would be rather ironic.

## The jhānas: deepening concentration

In some Buddhist traditions, samatha training can lead to deep states of concentration known as *jhānas*.

Four jhānas are commonly described, associated with a progression of mental qualities.

In the first, factors such as pīti, pleasure or well-being, and deeply gathered attention arise.

In the second, deeper unification develops, accompanied by joy and well-being.

In the third, pīti becomes less prominent, while equanimity and a more serene well-being stand out.

In the fourth, traditional descriptions emphasize the purity of mindfulness and equanimity, beyond ordinary feeling tones of pleasure and pain.

There are also four formless meditative attainments, which some modern classifications group with the preceding four as the “eight jhānas,” although the traditional texts distinguish the categories.

We won't turn this chapter into an advanced manual of these states.

Many books and teachers have devoted enormous effort to explaining their different interpretations and methods.

Besides, traditions don't always agree on the depth of absorption or precise characteristics required to say someone has reached a particular jhāna.

I want to emphasize something else.

These states show just how much experience can be transformed by training attention and certain mental qualities.

But we shouldn't automatically confuse them with liberation.

We can develop extraordinary concentration and still have habits of attachment, aversion, or identification.

The jhānas can provide favorable conditions for investigating experience.

But deep concentration alone doesn't guarantee liberating understanding.

And here is a question I consider particularly important.

Why do we want to develop concentration?

## Being available

For a long time, I thought a highly trained mind would be one capable of controlling its experiences.

Eliminating distractions.

Avoiding unpleasant emotions.

Maintaining pleasant states for as long as possible.

But over the years I've come to value a different capacity.

Being available to what happens.

Imagine you're talking with someone.

They say something unexpected.

Perhaps an emotion appears.

Perhaps a thought.

Perhaps a bodily reaction.

If your attention becomes completely trapped in that response, you may stop listening.

But if you've developed some stability, perhaps you can recognize what has appeared without entirely losing touch with the situation.

It doesn't mean the reaction disappears.

It means the reaction doesn't necessarily have to occupy all the available space in your experience.

From our model's perspective, this may represent a change in the functional organization of attention and awareness.

A trained mind may have a greater capacity to recognize processes without becoming completely absorbed in them.

Of course, this doesn't mean meditators are immune to stress, tiredness, or intense emotions.

I've experienced for myself how old habits can return in difficult circumstances.

But the ability to recognize them and respond differently can develop.

And perhaps that's one of the most valuable capacities of this training.

Not controlling everything that arises.

But learning to remain sufficiently present to relate to it better.

## Equanimity isn't indifference

Here we find another fundamental quality: *upekkhā*, equanimity.

We already discussed it when exploring love.

But it also plays a central role in meditative development.

Equanimity enables us to experience pleasant and unpleasant phenomena without necessarily being carried away by the desire to preserve some and eliminate others.

It doesn't mean we don't care.

Nor that we should become cold or emotionally distant.

We can love someone deeply and maintain equanimity.

We can experience sadness and retain some stability.

We can recognize a problem and act to solve it without needing to fuel an endless internal struggle against its existence.

From our model's perspective, we could understand equanimity as a capacity that changes the relationship between experience and response.

An unpleasant sensation needn't automatically set off a chain of rejection.

A pleasant experience needn't immediately turn into attachment.

And a thought doesn't have to occupy all our attention just because it appeared.

This doesn't necessarily mean reactions vanish.

But perhaps we can learn not to follow them automatically.

And over time, some responses may lose strength.

Equanimity isn't about disconnecting from the world.

It can help us remain available to it.

## A simple practice

Let's begin with a five-minute exercise.

No previous experience needed.

Find a relatively quiet place.

Sit in a comfortable, stable position.

There's no need for a special posture.

If it feels pleasant, close your eyes. If not, leave them open with a relaxed gaze.

For a few moments, recognize how you feel.

Perhaps you're tired.

Perhaps you're thinking.

Perhaps you're calm.

You don't need to change anything.

Now gently direct your attention toward your breathing.

You can observe the sensations of air entering and leaving through the nose.

Or the movement of your abdomen.

Choose somewhere comfortable for you.

Don't try to control your breathing.

Just observe.

At some point, a distraction will probably arise.

A memory.

A thought.

A bodily sensation.

Perhaps the desire to finish the exercise.

When you notice it, recognize what happened.

And return to the breath.

Without getting angry.

Without reproaching yourself.

If possible, appreciate having noticed.

You've just recognized a distraction.

That's precisely part of what we're training.

Continue like this for a few minutes.

And when you finish, take a moment to notice how you feel.

You don't need to feel different.

You don't need to have reached a special state.

You've simply spent a few minutes training attention.

And that's enough to begin.

## The mistake of trying not to think

There is a widespread idea about meditation.

That it consists of emptying the mind.

Thinking of nothing.

Eliminating every distraction.

And remaining in a kind of perfect mental silence.

That isn't an adequate description of everything samatha involves.

Thoughts can arise during practice.

Especially at the beginning.

And we don't need to make their appearance a problem.

What we're learning is to recognize where attention goes and develop a more stable relationship with it.

Sometimes there will be many thoughts.

Sometimes few.

At times tranquility will appear.

At others, restlessness.

Conditions change.

Our practice will have different kinds of days too.

The important thing isn't to make every session wonderful.

It's to gradually develop certain capacities.

And to do so kindly enough that we can continue practicing.

Because if we end up angry every time we sit down because we haven't managed to stop thinking, perhaps we're training a very different skill from the one we intended.

## Five minutes every day

There is something I consider especially important for beginners.

Regularity.

You don't need to start by meditating an hour a day.

Perhaps five minutes are enough to establish a habit.

Even two minutes can be a good start.

The important thing is finding a sustainable practice.

Because attentional training doesn't depend only on what happens during a session.

It also depends on developing the conditions that encourage us to practice again.

We can choose a time of day.

Prepare a comfortable spot.

Reduce distractions.

And establish a simple intention.

Sit.

Observe.

Return when distracted.

Not especially spectacular.

But perhaps that's precisely its virtue.

From our model's perspective, we're developing habits.

And habits are built through repetition and the conditions that support their continuation.

We don't have to begin with an extraordinary transformation.

We can start with five minutes.

And discover what happens.

If practice causes intense discomfort, disorientation, or worrying changes in sleep or mood, it's wise to reduce it or stop and seek professional advice or guidance from a qualified instructor. Intensive practices are not suitable for everyone or in every circumstance.

Meditation should help us live better.

Not become another obligation that pushes us to ignore our needs.

## When practice leaves the meditation cushion

Now imagine you've been practicing for some time.

You've developed a greater capacity to recognize when your attention wanders.

Perhaps returning to the breath has become easier.

And some sessions contain moments of tranquility.

Wonderful.

But what happens when you get up?

Do all those abilities vanish immediately?

Not necessarily.

We can begin using them in everyday situations.

While listening to someone.

At work.

While walking.

When we experience a powerful emotion.

Or when we need to make a decision.

Perhaps we'll discover that we can pay attention to a conversation without continually preparing our response.

Perhaps we'll recognize that we're worried before spending twenty minutes imagining terrible scenarios.

Or perhaps we can remain with an unpleasant sensation for a few moments without reacting immediately.

These small changes can be extraordinarily valuable.

Not because they make us perfect people.

But because they expand our possible responses.

And this connects directly with our first training.

Love.

Greater mental stability can help us listen better.

Recognize our emotions.

And respond more thoughtfully toward ourselves and others.

We won't always manage it.

But perhaps we can do it increasingly often.

## Concentration isn't the destination

I want to end with a warning.

Concentration can become fascinating.

We can develop states of tranquility, well-being, and stability that feel extraordinarily pleasant.

And it's natural to want to experience them again.

But we shouldn't forget what we're practicing for.

If we use concentration exclusively to escape our difficulties, we may end up depending on it like any other pleasant experience.

And if we start measuring our worth by the depth of our meditative states, we'll have created a new identity that needs defending.

Our minds are perfectly capable of turning meditation into another object of attachment.

That's why I want to remind you of something.

Concentration isn't necessarily the destination. It's also a capacity that can help us travel the path.

It allows us to develop stability.

Recognize our reactions more clearly.

And create favorable conditions for investigating experience with greater clarity.

But it doesn't replace ethics.

Or guarantee wisdom.

And it doesn't turn our extraordinary experiences into metaphysical truths.

We can enjoy the fruits of concentration without needing to make them our identity.

And we can keep investigating.

## Learning to be

We've explored samatha as a training in calm, stability, and concentration.

We've seen that attention also depends on causes and conditions.

And that through practice, we can develop capacities that change how we relate to it.

We don't have to eliminate every thought.

We don't have to control every emotion.

Or produce extraordinary experiences.

We can start with something much simpler.

Sit.

Observe.

Recognize when we've been distracted.

And return.

Again and again.

With patience.

With curiosity.

And, if possible, with a little smile.

Because perhaps learning concentration isn't only about keeping attention fixed on an object.

Perhaps it's also about learning to be present for our own lives.

Without continually chasing another experience.

Without being caught in every thought.

And without forgetting that the world keeps happening while we try to understand it.

It's not about acquiring a mind that never gets distracted. It's about developing a mind that can return.

And over time, perhaps we'll discover that this ability to return is one of the simplest and most valuable forms of freedom we can cultivate.

In the next chapter, we'll explore the third training: vipassanā, the investigation of experience.

Because a more stable mind can help us observe more clearly.

And perhaps when we learn to look carefully, we'll discover that some things we took for granted were never quite as we imagined.
