# 13. Nirvana: What Does It Mean to Extinguish the Fire?

We've arrived.

After twelve chapters discussing suffering, ignorance, conditioning, karma, emptiness, and a self that seems increasingly unsure of who it is, we are finally going to talk about nirvana.

The great goal of Buddhism.

Ultimate liberation.

The end of suffering.

Sounds wonderful.

Almost too wonderful.

And naturally, the first question we might ask is:

Where do I sign up?

Unfortunately, I don't know of any registration form.

And if someone promises you nirvana in three weekends, with a special discount if you book before Sunday, perhaps you should carefully investigate the conditions that produced that offer.

But what exactly is nirvana?

And above all, what can we understand about it through our model?

## Extinguishing the fire

The word *nirvana* comes from Sanskrit. In Pali, we find *nibbāna*.

Traditionally, it is associated with the image of a fire going out—its extinguishing or cooling.

An image I find especially interesting.

Imagine a campfire.

As long as it has fuel, the fire keeps burning.

We can watch its flames, feel its heat, and hear the wood crackle.

But what happens when it stops receiving fuel?

Eventually the fire goes out.

We do not have to chase down every flame to destroy it.

We do not have to convince it that burning is a bad idea.

Quite simply, when the conditions that sustain combustion disappear, the fire stops burning.

In Buddhist teachings, nirvana is closely associated with the extinction of the three unwholesome roots we have studied: greed, aversion, and ignorance.

In the Nibbānapañhā Sutta (SN 38.1), Sāriputta answers a question about nirvana by identifying it with the ending of those three roots.

This is not an explanation of attaining permanent happiness by accumulating pleasant experiences.

It is an explanation centered on the cessation of what feeds suffering.

And here we find an extraordinary connection with our model.

What if liberation could be understood, at least functionally, as the cessation of the dynamics that generate and perpetuate suffering?

Let's explore that possibility.

## A wheel that stops being fed

In previous chapters we have viewed the mind as a conditioned system.

Our experiences arise depending on causes and conditions.

Our reactions help change subsequent conditions.

And certain dynamics can reinforce themselves.

Imagine someone criticizes you.

An unpleasant feeling arises.

Then comes anger.

Anger encourages defensive thoughts.

Those thoughts feed the anger even more.

And perhaps you end up responding aggressively, creating new conditions for the argument to continue.

We already know this dynamic.

But now imagine something different.

Someone criticizes you.

An unpleasant feeling arises.

You recognize it.

Perhaps a defensive impulse appears too.

But you do not need to follow it.

You do not need to construct a story about how unfair the world is.

You do not need to prove you are right.

You can listen.

Perhaps you discover the criticism is useful.

Perhaps you find it unfair and decide to set a boundary.

Or perhaps you simply get on with your day.

The important point is that certain dynamics that used to feed suffering have stopped doing so.

Is that nirvana?

Not necessarily.

Responding equanimously to criticism does not prove we have attained ultimate liberation.

But it can help us understand an important principle.

When a reaction stops feeding the conditions sustaining a particular form of suffering, that suffering may decrease or cease.

Our model allows us to investigate this mechanism.

And perhaps it can also help us understand why the Buddhist path is so concerned with transforming our deepest tendencies.

## Liberation from what?

When we hear the word “liberation,” we can imagine many things.

Freedom from our obligations.

Freedom from people who annoy us.

Freedom from our problems.

Or, if we are feeling particularly ambitious, freedom from having to work on Mondays.

But Buddhist liberation points to something different.

It is not simply about arranging our lives to avoid every unpleasant situation.

Nor about developing absolute control over our emotions.

It is associated with the extinction of the fundamental conditions that sustain the suffering of *saṃsāra*.

And here there is something I consider particularly important.

Perhaps the question is not only whether we are free.

Perhaps we should ask:

What do we want to be free from?

From feeling sadness?

From experiencing fear?

From things changing?

From the possibility that people we love may suffer?

It does not seem possible to construct a human life in which none of these possibilities exists.

But perhaps we can free ourselves from some of the dynamics through which our mind turns those experiences into new sources of suffering.

From needing everything to be the way we want.

From rigid identification with our interpretations.

From the compulsion to defend our self-image constantly.

From certain habits that lead us to react again and again in ways that hurt us.

This does not exhaust the traditional definition of nirvana.

But it may offer an entry point for understanding its practical dimension.

## Do we need a perfect mind?

For a long time, when I thought of enlightenment, I imagined an extraordinary mind.

A mind that never became angry.

That never had unpleasant thoughts.

That remained constantly at peace.

A kind of spiritual supercomputer, perfectly optimized and free of programming bugs.

And naturally, I wondered how long I would need to meditate to achieve something like that.

But over time I began to question that image.

Is it really necessary for every unpleasant experience to disappear in order for the suffering associated with attachment and aversion to stop arising?

Buddhist texts offer an interesting distinction.

In Itivuttaka 44, two forms of nirvana are described: with residue and without residue.

The first corresponds to a liberated person who continues living, perceiving, and experiencing pleasure and pain.

The second is associated with the definitive cessation of conditioned existence after that person's death, according to the traditional account.

This distinction deserves attention.

Even someone considered fully liberated still has a body.

Their senses continue working.

They can experience pleasant and unpleasant sensations.

Liberation does not necessarily mean that every painful experience disappears.

What has ceased, according to traditional teaching, is the greed, aversion, and ignorance that sustained suffering.

From our model's perspective, this invites us to distinguish between an experience arising and the dynamics that develop in response to it.

We can feel pain.

But must we necessarily reject it?

We can experience loss.

But must we endlessly fight the fact that it happened?

We can recognize an unpleasant emotion.

But must we identify with it?

We do not know how far these capacities can develop in each person.

And it would be a mistake to use a theoretical model to guarantee extraordinary outcomes.

But distinguishing experience from reaction gives us a clear direction for investigation.

Perhaps liberation is not about manufacturing perfect experiences.

Perhaps it is about profoundly transforming our relationship with them.

## The inertia of conditioning

There is something I find particularly interesting when we observe our habits.

We may understand deeply that a reaction harms us and still find that it appears.

Imagine that for decades you have developed a tendency to react defensively in certain situations.

You have learned to recognize it.

You understand many of its causes.

You no longer need to justify it.

And yet, under certain circumstances, the response may still arise.

Perhaps you are tired.

Perhaps you are under extraordinary pressure.

Or perhaps someone succeeds in activating a deeply rooted habit.

We can use the image of inertia to understand this.

A wheel that has been pushed for a long time may continue spinning even when we stop pushing it.

But here we need to distinguish two phenomena.

One is the persistence of certain learned patterns.

The other is the creation of new conditions that reinforce those patterns.

From the perspective of our model, we can investigate the possibility that an old response continues to arise without our necessarily having to feed it in the same way.

Nevertheless, we must be especially careful when connecting this idea with nirvana.

Buddhist tradition does not define an *arahant* simply as someone who still has greed, hatred, and ignorance but has learned not to reinforce them.

It states that these roots have been extinguished.

Thus, our analogy of inertia may be useful for understanding stages of training, but it does not prove that ultimate liberation consists of leaving harmful tendencies intact while no longer feeding them.

That is an important difference.

And a question worth continuing to investigate.

## And what happens to the self?

Perhaps you are wondering what role the self plays in all this.

If we have learned to see it as a conditioned construction, does reaching nirvana mean we must eliminate it?

Not necessarily in the sense we usually imagine.

In everyday life we need to remember our names, recognize family members, fulfill responsibilities, and make use of our personal history.

Liberation does not seem to require becoming someone who cannot remember who left the keys on the table.

Although I admit that on some days I already manage that without achieving nirvana.

The teaching of not-self particularly questions identifying with phenomena as though they constituted a permanent, independent self under our control.

Through our model, we can investigate the difference between using a personal identity and clinging to it.

We can have a story without needing to defend it constantly.

We can recognize our qualities without constructing a sense of essential superiority.

We can acknowledge our mistakes without making them a verdict on who we are.

And we can use the word “I” without needing to imagine a controller independent of all the processes that make up our experience.

During my practice, one change I have valued most has been learning to relate to my own self more kindly.

I did not need to destroy it.

I could understand some of the conditions that had constructed it.

Recognize its function.

And stop treating every one of its worries as an order I had to obey.

I am not claiming that this experience amounts to nirvana.

But I do think it has helped me better understand what liberation from certain forms of identification might mean.

## Nirvana is not just another extraordinary experience

There is a particularly common temptation on contemplative paths.

Confusing extraordinary experiences with liberation.

During meditation, we can experience deep states of concentration.

Rapture.

Tranquility.

Equanimity.

Altered perceptions of time.

Changes in our usual sense of identity.

And experiences that are difficult to describe in everyday language.

Some can be extraordinarily valuable.

I myself have experienced meditative rapture, particularly related to *pīti*, which profoundly transformed how I understood the relationship between body and mind.

But there is a question I consider fundamental.

To what extent does an extraordinary experience transform the conditions that generate our everyday suffering?

We can experience wonderful peace during meditation.

And then get angry because someone left dirty dishes in the kitchen.

The peace was real as an experience.

But perhaps it had not yet transformed all our habits.

That does not make meditation useless.

Quite the opposite.

States of concentration can support important capacities for practice.

But we should not confuse access to a pleasant experience with the definitive extinction of the causes of suffering.

Nor should we assume that the more extraordinary an experience is, the greater the wisdom we have necessarily developed.

Perhaps genuine transformation shows itself in far more ordinary situations.

In our capacity to listen.

In how we respond when someone contradicts us.

In how we treat our children when we are tired.

In our willingness to acknowledge a mistake.

And in how we relate to other people's suffering.

These are not definitive proofs of liberation.

But they are aspects of our lives where we can investigate the fruits of practice.

## Is nirvana conditioned?

Now we arrive at a difficult question.

If everything we experience depends on causes and conditions, what about nirvana?

In Buddhist teachings, nirvana is described as *asaṅkhata*, the unconditioned or unconstructed.

This seems to present a problem for our model.

If we interpret the mind as a conditioned system, how can we explain something unconditioned?

There are several philosophical and doctrinal possibilities, and we are not going to resolve them definitively here.

But I do want to establish a distinction.

The path leading to liberation includes conditioned practices, actions, and intentions.

We learn.

We meditate.

We develop habits.

We cultivate understanding.

And these activities depend on causes and conditions.

Nevertheless, the tradition does not therefore identify nirvana with a mental state produced by those same conditions.

We can use our model to investigate how certain suffering-generating dynamics cease.

But that does not prove we have fully explained what unconditioned nirvana means in Buddhist teachings.

Perhaps our functional interpretation is enough to explore some practical aspects of the path.

Perhaps there are dimensions of the teaching that our model cannot adequately represent.

And perhaps we will have to revise some of our hypotheses.

That does not strike me as a problem.

That is precisely why we devoted a chapter to the limits of our model.

It does not have to become a universal explanation to be useful to us.

## Is nirvana the end of the path?

Imagine someone manages to free themselves deeply from the dynamics that previously caused suffering.

Does that mean there is nothing left for them to do?

That they should sit beneath a tree for the rest of their days?

That they can no longer enjoy a conversation, learn something new, or help others?

That does not seem necessary.

According to tradition, the Buddha himself continued teaching for decades after his awakening.

And in Mahāyāna we find an especially beautiful expression of the relationship between liberation and compassion: the ideal of the bodhisattva, who cultivates wisdom and compassion for the benefit of beings.

From our model's perspective, we can explore a possibility.

Perhaps freedom from certain forms of attachment and aversion allows us to be more available to the world.

Without needing constantly to defend our self-image.

Without turning every difficulty into a personal threat.

Without getting caught in every story our mind is capable of constructing.

And perhaps that freedom can support the ability to act with love.

Not because a liberated person must necessarily behave in one particular way.

But because certain dynamics that previously constrained their responses no longer exert the same influence.

Here we find a connection with something we have repeated throughout this book.

Wisdom does not have to take us away from the world.

It can help us relate to it better.

## Don't turn nirvana into an obsession

There is a rather amusing paradox.

Imagine you decide to attain nirvana.

You want it intensely.

You start constantly comparing your experiences with descriptions you have read.

You ask yourself whether you have progressed enough.

You become frustrated when old habits appear.

And every time you experience an unpleasant emotion, you think:

“This shouldn't still be happening to me.”

Congratulations.

You have managed to turn liberation from attachment into a new object of attachment.

Our mind has a truly extraordinary talent for such things.

But that does not mean we should abandon every aspiration to free ourselves from suffering.

Buddhism distinguishes between craving that feeds attachment and wholesome aspirations that support practice.

We can want to learn.

We can want to reduce suffering.

We can develop a sincere intention to live with greater wisdom and compassion.

Without needing to turn every experience into an examination of how awakened we are.

Personally, I believe this distinction is fundamental.

I do not want to use the Dharma to manufacture a new perfect image of myself.

Nor to construct a spiritual identity I must defend constantly.

I would rather use it as a tool to understand my experience better and learn to relate to it.

And if, in the process, I discover that one of my interpretations was mistaken, wonderful.

We will have found something new to investigate.

## A small experiment

Let's use the image of fire.

Think of an everyday situation that often generates distress.

Perhaps a recurring argument.

A worry.

An expectation you find hard to let go of.

Or a reaction that arises when someone behaves differently from how you would like.

Do not choose the most painful situation of your life.

Let's start with something simple.

Now try to observe what keeps that dynamic alive.

Which thoughts appear repeatedly?

Which emotions feed those thoughts?

Which interpretations reinforce your emotions?

What do you do when they appear?

Do your responses help sustain the problem?

You do not need to find a perfect explanation.

Simply try to identify some of the conditions.

Then ask yourself:

What would happen if I stopped feeding one of them?

Perhaps you could stop mentally reconstructing an argument.

Perhaps you could let go of an unrealistic expectation.

Perhaps you could recognize an emotion without turning it into a story about yourself.

Or perhaps you will discover that you need to change something in your surroundings, ask for help, or set a boundary.

We are not seeking resignation.

We are investigating which conditions sustain suffering and which can favor its cessation.

And if the reaction appears again tomorrow, that does not mean you have failed.

Perhaps you have discovered that there are other conditions you do not yet understand.

Or that a habit requires more time and training to change.

You do not have to put out every fire in the universe this afternoon.

You've got enough on your plate learning to notice where you're adding fuel.

## What does it mean to extinguish the fire?

We have explored nirvana from different perspectives.

Traditionally, it is associated with the extinction of greed, aversion, and ignorance, and with liberation from suffering and the cycle of rebirth.

It is also described as unconditioned, a dimension of the teaching our model does not claim to explain completely.

From our functional perspective, we have explored how certain mental dynamics can generate and perpetuate suffering and how transforming their conditions may support its cessation.

We have not proven that our explanation is equivalent to traditional nirvana.

Nor have we resolved every question about the nature of consciousness, free will, or existence after death.

But perhaps we have found a useful way to approach the teaching.

We do not need to imagine nirvana as an extraordinary experience to chase desperately.

We can begin by investigating the conditions that sustain our everyday suffering.

Recognize our reactions.

Understand our habits.

Develop mindfulness, concentration, compassion, and wisdom.

And learn not to feed some of the dynamics that once seemed inevitable.

Perhaps, over time, this practice will profoundly transform how we live.

Perhaps we will discover possibilities we cannot yet imagine.

And perhaps some of the questions we started with will no longer seem quite so important.

I do not know how far this path may take us.

But there is something about the image of fire that I find especially beautiful.

We do not need to hate a fire to understand how it goes out.

And perhaps we do not need to hate our mind to learn to free ourselves from the conditions that make us suffer.
