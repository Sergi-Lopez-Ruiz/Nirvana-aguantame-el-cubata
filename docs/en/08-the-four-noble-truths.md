# 8. The Four Noble Truths

Imagine you go to the doctor because your back hurts.

After examining you, the doctor says:

“I have four pieces of news.”

“Four?”

“Yes. First, you've got a problem. Second, it has causes. Third, we can do something about it. And fourth, I'm going to tell you how.”

Not a bad start.

Now imagine the doctor spends the next twenty years doing nothing but repeating those four statements, without explaining what to do.

You'd probably end up looking for another doctor.

Fortunately, the Buddha was rather more practical.

The Four Noble Truths form one of the fundamental structures of his teachings. Traditionally, they are presented as part of his first discourse, known as the *Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta*, the discourse on setting the wheel of the Dharma in motion.

The four truths are:

1. There is *dukkha*: suffering, dissatisfaction and unsatisfactoriness.
2. *Dukkha* has an origin.
3. There is a cessation of *dukkha*.
4. There is a path leading to that cessation.

At first glance, they seem fairly simple.

And in a way, they are.

The really interesting part appears when we stop seeing them as four philosophical claims and start using them to investigate our experience.

## First Noble Truth: there is dukkha

In the previous chapter, we explored the meaning of *dukkha*.

We saw that it isn't limited to obvious pain, but also includes dissatisfaction associated with change and the inability of conditioned phenomena to offer definitive satisfaction.

The first truth invites us to recognise this feature of our experience.

But recognition doesn't mean resignation.

Nor does it mean adopting a pessimistic view of life.

It means honestly observing what's happening.

Imagine you're going through a difficult situation.

Perhaps you've got a problem at work, an argument in the family or a worry you can't get out of your head.

You can try to ignore your discomfort.

You can distract yourself.

You can persuade yourself that you shouldn't feel this way.

Or you can begin by recognising that you're suffering.

It sounds simple.

But sometimes recognising our suffering is surprisingly difficult.

Perhaps because we think we should be stronger.

Perhaps because we're ashamed of it.

Or perhaps because we've lived with certain forms of discomfort for so long that we've stopped noticing them.

From our model's perspective, this first truth invites us to observe the state of the system without immediately judging it.

What's happening?

What sensations are arising?

What emotions are we experiencing?

What thoughts accompany those emotions?

We don't have to solve the problem yet.

First, we try to understand it.

And here we find an important detail in the original teachings: the task associated with the First Noble Truth isn't to eliminate *dukkha* immediately, but to understand it fully.

That might not seem a huge difference, but it can radically change how we practise.

Because if we try to eliminate something before understanding it, we may end up fighting processes we don't even know.

## Second Noble Truth: the origin of dukkha

The Second Noble Truth states that *dukkha* has an origin.

In traditional teachings, this origin is identified particularly with *taṇhā*, a Pali word often translated as thirst, craving or longing.

It isn't just any kind of desire.

It's a way of wanting characterised by the need to obtain, preserve or eliminate certain experiences.

Traditionally, three forms are distinguished:

- Craving for pleasant sensory experiences.
- Craving to become, or to continue existing, in a particular way.
- Craving for non-existence or for the elimination of what we reject.

Let's look at an example.

Imagine you've had a bad day.

You get home, sit on the sofa and decide to eat a piece of chocolate.

It's delicious.

So far, no problem.

But then perhaps you want to keep experiencing that pleasure.

You take another piece.

Then another.

And when the chocolate runs out, you discover that you're rather angry with the universe.

Not because the chocolate was bad.

But because your relationship with that pleasant experience has changed.

You've moved from enjoying something to needing it to continue.

The difference may seem small, but it's fundamental.

From our model's perspective, we can view this process as a sequence of conditioned responses.

A pleasant sensation arises.

That sensation encourages a desire.

The desire may turn into craving.

And craving may generate attachment, frustration and new responses that fuel discomfort.

Not every pleasant experience necessarily triggers this sequence.

Nor does every desire generate suffering.

But we can investigate what happens when our mind turns a preference into a demand.

And of course, the process works in the opposite direction too.

An unpleasant experience arises.

We want it to disappear.

The more we fight it, the greater our frustration may become.

And that frustration becomes a new unpleasant experience we also want to eliminate.

A dynamic that can feed back into itself.

Here we find an important connection with our model.

Suffering doesn't necessarily arise from a single isolated cause. It can arise and persist through networks of conditioned processes that reinforce one another.

The Second Noble Truth identifies craving as a central condition in the origin of *dukkha*.

Our model tries to explore how it develops, what feeds it, and how it participates in these dynamics.

Later, we'll study dependent origination, a teaching that considerably broadens this perspective and relates suffering to processes such as ignorance, feeling, craving and clinging.

For now, the important thing is to understand that we're not simply talking about no longer wanting things.

We're investigating a particular way of relating to them.

## Third Noble Truth: the cessation of dukkha

This is probably the most hopeful of the four truths.

Suffering can cease.

But we need to be careful about what we mean by cessation.

It doesn't necessarily mean we can eliminate every painful experience.

It doesn't mean our bodies will stop ageing, we'll never fall ill again or the people we love will always remain by our side.

The Third Noble Truth refers to the cessation of the craving that gives rise to *dukkha*.

In the Buddhist tradition, this cessation is intimately connected with *nibbāna*, or nirvana.

We'll have time to explore that concept.

For now, let's look at it from a much more everyday perspective.

Recall a time when you were very angry.

Perhaps for a while you fed that anger with thoughts, memories and interpretations.

But it's also possible that, at some point, something changed.

Maybe you understood better what had happened.

Perhaps you realised you'd misinterpreted another person's intentions.

Or you simply stopped feeding the thoughts that kept the anger alive.

And then the anger began to subside.

What's interesting isn't only that the emotion disappeared.

It's that the conditions sustaining it stopped operating in the same way.

From our model's perspective, we can interpret these processes as everyday examples of conditioned cessation.

When particular conditions are no longer present, some processes stop occurring or being maintained.

But let's be precise.

An emotion disappearing temporarily isn't equivalent to the definitive liberation described by Buddhism.

Interrupting one episode of anger is one thing.

Profoundly transforming the tendencies that repeatedly cause certain experiences to trigger the same suffering is quite another.

And the traditional ideal of completely eradicating the craving that fuels *dukkha* is more ambitious still.

Our model can help us explore these different levels without confusing them.

Here a question arises that I find particularly interesting.

What happens if we stop trying to control every experience and begin transforming the conditions that lead us to cling to them?

Perhaps we don't need a perfect mind.

Perhaps we need to learn to relate differently to whatever arises.

And perhaps, with sufficient training, some of the dynamics that once fed our suffering will stop doing so.

The Third Noble Truth asserts that genuine liberation from that suffering exists.

Our task is to investigate what we can understand about it, without turning our first observations into proof that we've attained its definitive form.

## Fourth Noble Truth: the path

Now we arrive at the practical part.

If suffering has causes, and certain causes can cease, the next question seems obvious.

What can we do?

The Buddha proposed the Noble Eightfold Path.

Traditionally, it consists of eight factors:

1. Right view.
2. Right intention.
3. Right speech.
4. Right action.
5. Right livelihood.
6. Right effort.
7. Right mindfulness.
8. Right concentration.

The word “right” can feel a little uncomfortable.

It seems to suggest a list of instructions we must obey, with someone grading us if we get things wrong.

Fortunately, I don't know of any celestial examination board marking our meditation exams.

In this context, we can understand “right” as what is aligned with the path leading to the cessation of suffering.

And there's something especially interesting here.

These eight factors aren't eight steps we must complete one after another.

They're dimensions of a training that mutually support one another.

Traditionally, they're often grouped into three broad areas:

Ethical conduct (*sīla*), concerning how we act and relate to others.

Concentration or mental stability (*samādhi*), concerning training attention and certain mental qualities.

Wisdom (*paññā*), concerning deep understanding of experience and the processes that generate suffering.

In this book, we'll use a practical reinterpretation of these three areas.

Learning to relate with love.

Learning to concentrate through *samatha*.

Learning to investigate our experience through *vipassanā*.

It's worth clarifying that *samatha* and *vipassanā* are practices or qualities that contribute to developing the path; they aren't simply alternative names for *samādhi* and *paññā*.

Nor am I claiming that our formulation of love replaces the entire richness of Buddhist ethics.

It's a simple way of organising our training.

A proposal we'll explore later.

For now, it's enough to understand something important.

The path isn't only about learning new things. It's also about developing new ways of acting, paying attention and understanding.

From our model's perspective, we can regard it as a process of transforming the conditioned system.

We develop habits that encourage beneficial behaviour.

We train abilities that allow us to observe more clearly.

We investigate the conditions that generate suffering.

And that learning can change our future responses.

Practice introduces new conditions.

And those conditions can transform the way we experience life.

## Four truths, four tasks

There's an aspect of the Four Noble Truths that I find particularly valuable.

The Buddha didn't present them merely as statements we're supposed to accept.

He also described a task associated with each.

The first truth, *dukkha*, is to be understood.

The second, its origin, is to be abandoned.

The third, its cessation, is to be realised.

The fourth, the path, is to be developed.

This completely transforms how we interpret the teachings.

It isn't simply about believing suffering exists.

It's about understanding it.

Knowing that craving contributes to suffering isn't enough.

We need to learn to abandon the dynamics that feed it.

It's not enough to imagine that liberation is possible.

The tradition invites us to realise it through experience and practice.

And admiring the Noble Eightfold Path isn't enough either.

We have to cultivate it.

From our perspective, we can understand these four tasks as a process of investigation and transformation.

We observe a phenomenon.

Investigate its conditions.

Explore its cessation.

And develop the capacities that support that transformation.

I like this way of viewing the Four Noble Truths because it prevents us from turning the Dharma into a collection of pretty ideas.

We're not trying to win a philosophical argument.

We're trying to learn something that can change how we live.

## And where does ignorance come in?

If you've been paying attention, you may have noticed something curious.

We've explained that craving is a fundamental condition of suffering.

But why does craving arise?

Why does our mind develop tendencies that lead it to cling to certain experiences and reject others?

And why do we keep feeding those dynamics even after finding that they hurt us?

This brings us to another fundamental concept in Buddhism: *avijjā*, ignorance.

It doesn't simply mean not knowing certain things.

A person can have three doctorates and still react in ways that generate an enormous amount of suffering.

In fact, they may be able to devise extraordinarily sophisticated explanations to justify every reaction.

Buddhist ignorance is particularly associated with a mistaken or insufficient understanding of fundamental aspects of experience, including the Four Noble Truths.

From our model's perspective, we can explore a functional interpretation.

Perhaps certain habits of perception, interpretation and reaction help maintain the very dynamics that generate suffering.

Perhaps ignorance isn't merely an absence of information, but a configuration of conditioned processes that perpetuates particular ways of experiencing and responding.

And perhaps wisdom can be understood, in part, as developing capacities and tendencies that transform those dynamics.

This is a hypothesis of our model, not a complete traditional definition of *avijjā*.

But I think it's worth investigating.

Because it leads us to an interesting possibility.

Perhaps understanding the Four Noble Truths intellectually is only the beginning.

Real change might occur when that understanding begins to transform our habitual responses.

When we stop reacting in particular ways, not because we're constantly forcing ourselves to, but because we've learned something profoundly different.

## A small experiment

Let's use the Four Noble Truths to investigate an everyday experience.

The next time something bothers you, try to observe what happens.

Don't start with the most difficult experience of your life.

A small frustration will do.

For example, imagine someone is late for an appointment.

First, acknowledge the discomfort.

Perhaps you feel impatience, anger or worry.

You don't need to eliminate anything.

Just recognise that you're experiencing something unpleasant.

Then investigate its conditions.

Perhaps you expected the other person to arrive on time.

Perhaps you interpret their lateness as disrespect.

Perhaps you've been tired all day and this wait is the last thing you needed.

Notice whether a wish arises for the situation to be different.

And whether that wish turns into a demand that feeds your discomfort.

Now explore what happens when you recognise that, for the moment, the person hasn't arrived.

You don't have to convince yourself that lateness is wonderful.

Nor do you have to give up saying that punctuality matters to you.

Simply investigate whether you can stop feeding some of the reactions that are intensifying your suffering.

Finally, consider what you can do.

Perhaps you could call and ask what happened.

Perhaps you need to set boundaries if it keeps happening.

Or perhaps you can use the wait to rest for a few minutes.

There is no universal answer.

What matters is observing whether your way of responding helps reduce suffering or feeds it.

And if you can't manage it, that's fine too.

You've just found something new to investigate.

## An extraordinarily practical proposal

The Four Noble Truths may seem like a very simple teaching.

There is suffering.

It has causes.

It can cease.

There is a path.

But their depth becomes apparent when we begin relating them to our experience.

We don't need to accept every philosophical interpretation of Buddhism to start investigating.

We can observe how our suffering arises.

We can explore the conditions that sustain it.

We can discover what happens when particular reactions are no longer maintained.

And we can develop abilities that encourage different ways of responding.

The Buddhist tradition proposes a horizon of liberation far deeper than merely reducing a few everyday annoyances.

We shouldn't lose sight of that.

But neither do we have to begin by trying to understand nirvana completely.

We can start with something much closer.

An emotion.

A reaction.

A habit.

A small opportunity to act differently.

And perhaps, little by little, we'll discover that the Four Noble Truths aren't only an explanation of suffering.

They're also an invitation to understand how we help sustain it and how we can learn to stop doing so.

Understand. Abandon. Realise. Develop.

Four tasks.

A whole lifetime to explore them.

And absolutely no need to pass an exam at the end.
