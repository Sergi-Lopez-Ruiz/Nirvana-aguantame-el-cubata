# 18. Three Trainings, One Path

Imagine you've spent several months meditating.

You've developed fairly decent concentration.

You can stay with your breathing, notice your thoughts, and experience moments of profound calm.

You've also learned a lot about the Dharma.

You understand impermanence, conditioning, and dependent origination.

You can even explain why the self need not be an independent controller of our mental processes.

Wonderful.

Now imagine coming home after an exhausting day.

Your children are arguing.

There are toys everywhere.

Someone has spilled a glass of water.

And when you try to restore a little order, one of them answers back in a way you don't like.

Suddenly, a reaction appears.

Anger.

Tension.

An urge to raise your voice.

And all that fascinating knowledge about the conditioned mind seems to have mysteriously vanished.

What happened?

Perhaps nothing particularly surprising.

You've learned a great deal, but certain conditions can still encourage responses you'd rather avoid.

Meditation doesn't automatically turn us into patient people.

Knowledge of the Dharma doesn't guarantee compassionate actions.

And loving our children deeply doesn't mean we always know the best way to relate to them.

But we can learn.

And this is where our three trainings begin to take on deeper meaning.

## Three ways to transform the same system

In the previous chapters, we introduced three broad capacities.

**Love:** learning to relate to ourselves and others in ways that support well-being and reduce harm.

**Concentration:** developing stability, clarity, and mental availability through training attention.

**Investigation:** directly understanding the processes that arise in our experience and the conditions that help sustain them.

We could treat them as three different disciplines.

But our model lets us see them as three ways of influencing the same conditioned system.

Love affects our intentions, relationships, and behavioral habits.

Concentration changes our ability to sustain attention and recognize what is happening.

Investigation transforms how we understand experience and can change the interpretations that fuel particular reactions.

And the interesting thing is that none of these abilities works in complete isolation.

By developing one, we may create conditions that help the others grow.

And that opens extraordinary possibilities.

## One argument, three trainings

Let's return to our family argument.

You're tired.

Your child is angry.

And you begin to feel your patience running out.

Let's see what the three trainings might contribute.

First, love.

You remember that your child isn't an enemy you need to defeat.

He's a child.

He's learning.

He has feelings, needs, and difficulties he may not yet know how to express.

And you are responsible for caring for him, protecting him, and teaching him.

That doesn't mean you must accept any behavior.

You can set boundaries.

You can correct him.

But you don't have to humiliate him or use your authority to release your frustration.

That orientation can influence your intentions.

Next, concentration.

Perhaps you manage to notice tension in your body.

You realize you're beginning to raise your voice.

You notice the urge to respond immediately.

But your attention isn't completely captured by that impulse.

You remain sufficiently available to recognize what is happening.

And perhaps that allows you to pause for a few seconds.

Finally, investigation.

You recognize that you're tired.

You notice that you've interpreted your child's reply as disrespect.

Perhaps you discover that this interpretation is feeding your anger.

And you understand that the situation doesn't necessarily require the reaction that was arising.

Now you can respond differently.

Perhaps you need to speak firmly.

Perhaps you need to separate the children.

Perhaps it's better to wait a moment before continuing the conversation.

Or perhaps you need to acknowledge that you're too upset and ask another adult to step in, if possible.

What matters is that all three capacities can contribute to a different response.

Love provides direction.

Concentration encourages stability.

Investigation helps us understand the reaction.

Together, they can transform what happens next.

There is no guarantee that this will always work.

But we can learn to develop conditions that make a caring response more likely.

And when we don't manage it, that too is an opportunity to practice.

Recognize the mistake.

Repair the harm.

And take concrete measures to reduce the chance of repeating it.

Because understanding our conditioning doesn't remove our responsibility.

## Not every difficulty has the same solution

Here is something I consider especially important.

When we find a reaction that makes us suffer, we usually try to correct it directly.

If we get distracted, we try to concentrate harder.

If we get angry, we try to calm down.

If we're afraid, we try to convince ourselves that we have nothing to fear.

But what if the problem isn't only in the reaction we're trying to change?

Imagine you've been sleeping badly for several days.

You're irritable.

You find it hard to concentrate.

And every minor setback seems to turn into a catastrophe.

You could spend hours analyzing each episode of irritation.

You could try to develop extraordinary patience through sheer willpower.

Or you could start by getting some sleep.

Not an especially spectacular philosophical solution.

And we probably wouldn't sell many spirituality books with a title like *Enlightenment Through Eight Hours of Sleep*.

But it might be an extraordinarily sensible intervention.

From the perspective of our model, we can investigate which conditions encourage particular reactions and which ones we can change.

That means we don't always need to intervene directly in what seems to be the problem.

Sometimes changing a more general condition can change several responses at once.

Sleeping better may improve patience and concentration.

Reducing unnecessary conflicts can encourage mental calm.

Developing compassion toward ourselves can reduce certain forms of judgment that make practice difficult.

And understanding a reaction better can mean we need less effort to recognize it and respond differently.

We don't always have to fight every symptom. Sometimes we can change the conditions that help produce it.

That doesn't mean every problem has a simple solution.

Some difficulties require professional help, significant changes in circumstances, or interventions beyond the reach of meditation.

But our perspective may help us ask more useful questions.

Instead of asking only how to eliminate a reaction, we can investigate what sustains it.

## The three trainings support one another

Now imagine another situation.

You've had an argument with a colleague.

You feel they treated you unfairly.

You're angry.

And you spend the rest of the day replaying the conversation.

How could our three trainings help?

Perhaps love allows you to recognize that your colleague has worries and limitations too.

That perspective might reduce some hostility.

With less hostility, your mind might be less agitated.

And that can make concentration easier.

More stable attention may help you notice the thoughts feeding your anger.

Then, through investigation, you may discover that much of the discomfort comes from an interpretation you had never questioned.

That understanding may change your response to your colleague.

And perhaps make a more constructive conversation possible.

Notice what happened.

Love encouraged stability.

Stability helped investigation.

And investigation contributed to a more compassionate response.

But we could go in the opposite direction.

Perhaps investigation helps you recognize your thoughts first.

That reduces the intensity of the reaction.

Then you can concentrate better.

And that stability supports a more thoughtful response.

Or perhaps concentration practice allows you to recognize anger before acting.

And that creates an opportunity to respond with love.

We don't need to impose one order.

The relationships between these capacities can be circular.

Systems theory gives us a way to see them as feedback processes.

And here lies one of our model's great possibilities.

We can cultivate conditions that encourage other healthy conditions, creating processes that contribute to their own development.

Just as some harmful habits can reinforce themselves, we can also develop dynamics that support well-being, clarity, and compassion.

## Ethical conduct transforms the mind too

Sometimes, when we talk about meditation, we focus so much on formal practice that we forget the importance of our everyday behavior.

But imagine spending the whole day lying, manipulating, and treating people badly.

Then you come home and decide to meditate to find inner peace.

Perhaps you'll manage it for a few minutes.

But I suspect you've made your training needlessly complicated.

Our actions have consequences.

They influence our relationships.

Our worries.

Our memories.

And the conditions we'll meet later.

Acting ethically can reduce certain sources of conflict and remorse.

It can also encourage habits of generosity, honesty, and trust.

And those conditions may support a calmer mind.

Traditional teachings describe precisely this relationship between ethical conduct, tranquility, concentration, and understanding.

That doesn't mean an ethical person never experiences agitation.

Or that correct behavior eliminates all our problems.

But we can investigate how our actions help shape our later experience.

And perhaps we'll find that some of the best conditions for meditation are created long before we sit on a cushion.

## Concentration can transform our relationships too

The relationship works the other way as well.

A more stable mind may help us listen better.

Imagine someone telling you about a problem.

As they speak, thoughts appear.

You remember something you wanted to say.

You begin preparing your answer.

Perhaps your own problems distract you.

And although you look as though you're listening, you've missed much of what the other person was trying to communicate.

Now imagine you've developed a greater ability to notice distractions and return to what you're attending to.

Perhaps you can listen more fully.

Recognize the tone of the conversation better.

Notice your reactions without having to follow them immediately.

And remain available to understand what the other person is trying to express.

This, too, is a way of caring.

We don't have to experience some extraordinary emotion to demonstrate affection.

Sometimes paying sincere attention to someone is one of the simplest and deepest expressions of love.

And here we find a beautiful connection.

Training attention can also become a way of learning to love.

## Investigation can set love free

There is another relationship I find particularly important.

Understanding can change the way we see people.

Imagine somebody behaving in a way that deeply upsets you.

Perhaps your first interpretation is that they're selfish.

Or rude.

Or that they simply enjoy making your life difficult.

But if you investigate the conditions that may be influencing them, perhaps other possibilities emerge.

They may be frightened.

They may not understand the consequences of their behavior.

They may have learned to defend themselves in a way that is now harmful.

Or perhaps they're going through difficulties you know nothing about.

You don't have to invent a specific explanation.

And you don't have to justify their behavior.

But recognizing that many conditions may be involved can weaken our tendency to reduce a person to a label.

And that may make room for compassion.

Here lies an interesting possibility.

Wisdom need not be a cold, purely intellectual activity.

When it transforms our interpretations, it can also change our emotional responses and relationships.

And perhaps in some circumstances love will arise more naturally.

Not because we force ourselves to feel it.

But because we've stopped feeding some of the interpretations that prevented it.

## Where should we start?

If the three trainings support each other, you may be wondering where to begin.

My suggestion is simple.

I'd start with love.

Not because we must become perfectly compassionate before learning to meditate.

But because a caring attitude toward ourselves and others can provide an especially valuable foundation.

Then I'd gradually introduce concentration.

Learning to notice distractions.

Developing stability.

Finding a little calm.

And, on that foundation, I'd deepen the investigation of experience.

But I want to stress that this is a teaching proposal.

Not a universal law.

Some people begin with meditation and discover later that they urgently need to work on their relationships.

Others develop an important understanding of their habits before ever practicing formal concentration.

And many people cultivate wisdom and compassion through life experiences that have nothing to do with sitting down to meditate.

The path needn't be identical for everyone.

From the perspective of our model, that makes sense.

No two mental systems are exactly alike.

Our histories differ.

Our habits too.

And the conditions that favor learning can vary enormously.

What matters isn't following a rigid sequence.

It's developing capacities that support one another.

And doing so in a way we can integrate into our lives.

## We don't have to turn our lives into a retreat

There is something I want to make clear.

I don't expect you to quit your job.

Or retreat to a mountain.

Or spend eight hours a day meditating.

If you wish to dedicate your life to contemplative practice, there are traditions and communities that can accompany you.

But this book is also for people with families, jobs, responsibilities, and limited time.

People who, for example, manage to find ten minutes to meditate before their children discover where the biscuits are hidden.

I think we can learn an enormous amount through small, sustained practices.

Five minutes of concentration.

A conversation in which we try to listen better.

A reaction we recognize before responding.

A mistake we learn from without punishing ourselves.

We don't have to spend our lives constantly monitoring our spiritual development.

In fact, turning every moment of existence into a progress assessment may be another way of feeding suffering.

We can practice.

Learn.

And carry on living.

Because our goal isn't to become experts in observing ourselves.

It's to develop abilities that help us relate better to the world.

## When training becomes another demand

There is a trap I know quite well.

We begin practicing because we want to live better.

But then we construct a new image of who we ought to be.

Someone who is always calm.

Always compassionate.

Always attentive.

Able to recognize every emotion before reacting.

And, if possible, completely liberated before the summer holidays.

Then we begin comparing our experience with that image.

And every difficulty becomes proof that we're still not good enough.

We've managed to turn the path to liberation into a new source of dissatisfaction.

Our minds are extraordinarily efficient at producing this result.

That's why I think we should remember something important.

We're not training to become perfect people.

We're learning to develop capacities.

And learning involves mistakes.

We can make mistakes.

We can have difficult days.

We can discover that particular reactions still arise after years of practice.

That doesn't mean we must resign ourselves.

But neither must we turn these difficulties into a sentence against ourselves.

We can recognize them.

Investigate their conditions.

And keep learning.

We don't have to hate what we want to transform.

In fact, perhaps learning to treat ourselves kindly is one of the conditions that will help us change the most.

## A seven-day experiment

Let's end with a practical suggestion.

For one week, spend a few minutes each day exploring the three trainings.

You don't have to do it perfectly.

Nor complete a checklist.

Simply try to create small opportunities to cultivate each capacity.

**Love.** Each day, choose one action that could contribute to your own well-being or someone else's.

It could be as simple as listening attentively, expressing gratitude, helping someone, or resting when you need to.

It needn't be a great sacrifice.

In fact, I'd rather it wasn't.

Remember the oxygen mask.

**Concentration.** Spend five minutes observing your breathing.

Notice distractions.

And return calmly.

You don't need to reach a special state.

Just practice.

**Investigation.** During the day, try to notice a reaction that interests you.

Perhaps a little irritation.

A desire.

A worry.

Notice what sensations arise and which interpretations might be involved.

Don't try to analyze everything.

One observation is enough.

At the end of the week you can ask yourself:

Have I discovered anything about my habits?

Has it become easier to recognize a particular reaction?

Have I found an opportunity to respond more kindly?

Is there some condition I could change to make practice easier?

Don't look for an extraordinary transformation.

Perhaps the most important change is simply that you've started noticing things that used to pass unnoticed.

And that alone can open new possibilities.

If any practice produces intense or persistent discomfort, reduce its intensity or stop and seek appropriate guidance. We don't all need the same training, and forcing practice is no demonstration of wisdom.

## One path

We've reached the end of our presentation of the three trainings.

Love.

Concentration.

Investigation.

Three simple words describing extraordinarily complex capacities.

From our model's perspective, we can see them as complementary ways of transforming the conditions that influence our experience.

Love directs our actions toward care and reducing harm.

Concentration encourages mental stability and availability.

Investigation helps us understand the processes that generate our reactions and develop different ways of relating to them.

And all three capacities can reinforce one another.

We don't need perfection in any of them.

Nor should we turn them into three separate compartments of life.

We can practice concentration while learning to treat ourselves kindly.

We can investigate our reactions while trying to help someone.

And we can develop understanding through our everyday relationships.

Perhaps, over time, some of these capacities will become increasingly natural.

Perhaps we'll need less effort to recognize certain reactions.

Perhaps we'll begin responding more clearly in situations that used to sweep us away.

Or perhaps we'll discover difficulties we didn't even know how to recognize.

It doesn't matter.

We're still learning.

Because the aim of our training isn't to build a perfect version of ourselves.

It's to learn to live with more wisdom and love.

And here I'd like to share one last thought.

Sometimes it seems we need to transform our minds completely before we can do anything good for the world.

As if we first had to solve all our problems, understand all our conditioning, and attain an extraordinary inner peace.

But perhaps there's another way.

We can begin caring while we learn.

We can learn while we act.

And we can transform our conditions through the very actions we take to improve the world.

We don't have to wait until we're completely ready to begin living with love and wisdom. We can learn to do so while we live.

In the next part of the book, we'll explore how to put all this into practice.

We'll begin with something very simple.

Sitting down.

Breathing.

And discovering that our minds have far more to do than we'd imagined.
