# 10. Karma, Habits, and Intentions

“That’s karma.”

We usually hear this when someone who has behaved badly suffers some misfortune.

A driver overtakes you dangerously and, a few miles later, you find them by the roadside with a flat tire.

“Karma!”

Let’s admit it. There is a certain satisfaction in imagining that the universe has a department dedicated to dispensing justice.

An especially efficient department, too, when it comes to people we dislike.

Unfortunately, Buddhist karma is considerably more complex than that.

And, in my opinion, much more interesting.

## What is karma?

The word *karma* comes from Sanskrit; in Pali, it is *kamma*. Its basic meaning is “action.”

But in Buddhism it has a more specific meaning.

In the Nibbedhika Sutta (AN 6.63), the Buddha identifies karma with intention, *cetanā*, and explains that when we form an intention, we act through body, speech, and mind.

This is an important distinction.

We are not simply talking about any event that produces another event.

If the wind blows and a branch falls onto your car, that does not necessarily mean the universe is punishing you for cheating on your diet.

Karma concerns intentional actions and their consequences.

In Buddhist traditions, those consequences may manifest at different times, even in later lives according to traditional interpretations of rebirth.

Our model does not seek to prove or disprove these claims.

We will initially focus on a dimension we can investigate in our experience: how our intentions and actions influence our behavior, our relationships, and the conditions that encourage future responses.

And this raises an interesting question.

Where do our intentions come from?

## Do we choose our intentions?

Imagine you are sitting comfortably on the sofa.

Suddenly a thought appears:

“I feel like eating some chocolate.”

Perhaps you get up immediately.

Perhaps you remember that you are trying to eat less sugar.

Perhaps you decide to wait.

Or perhaps you begin an extraordinarily sophisticated internal negotiation to justify why a small piece of chocolate is, in fact, a perfectly reasonable nutritional decision.

Whatever the outcome, we can investigate what happened.

Why did the desire appear?

Perhaps you were hungry.

Perhaps you saw an advertisement.

Perhaps you were bored.

Perhaps you have associated chocolate with relaxation for years.

Why did you decide to eat it—or not eat it?

Perhaps your habits, preferences, goals, memories, or emotional state played a part.

And now comes the uncomfortable question.

Is there any reason to believe that the intention to eat chocolate arose completely independently of all those conditions?

From the perspective of our model, we do not need to assume that.

We can regard intentions as processes that also arise depending on causes and conditions.

This does not mean decisions do not exist.

It means deciding is precisely one of the processes we are trying to understand.

Deliberation, reflection, and planning are all part of our mental activity.

And they, too, may be conditioned.

Our proposal is that intentions can be understood as conditioned responses which, in turn, condition our future actions and experiences.

We are not claiming to have definitively solved the philosophical problem of free will.

We are using a perspective that allows us to investigate the emergence of our intentions without having to imagine a controller independent of the rest of the mind.

## An intention is not an order

There is another point I consider important.

Just because an intention appears does not necessarily mean we have to act on it.

Imagine someone makes an unpleasant remark to you.

Anger appears.

Then the intention to respond aggressively arises.

But perhaps other tendencies also emerge.

You remember that the person is going through a difficult time.

You recognize that you are tired.

Or you simply have a habit of giving yourself a few seconds before responding.

The aggressive intention may still be there.

But it is not the only influence on the system.

And eventually a different response may arise.

Perhaps you decide to speak calmly.

Perhaps you set a boundary.

Or perhaps you would rather continue the conversation another time.

We do not need to imagine that one part of our mind, entirely free from conditioning, has heroically defeated another.

We can investigate how different conditions contributed to a different response.

Here we find an interesting connection with Culadasa’s mind-system model.

In *The Mind Illuminated*, intentions can be understood within distributed mental activity, in which different processes contribute to behavior and the training of attention.

Our model extends that perspective to investigate ethical consequences and everyday habits as well.

But we should remember that we are proposing a functional interpretation, not claiming that Culadasa developed exactly the same conclusions.

## Karma as inertia

Let’s introduce an analogy.

Imagine a spinning wheel.

Each time you push it in the same direction, you help maintain or increase its movement.

If you stop pushing, it may continue spinning for a while.

It has inertia.

But depending on the conditions, its movement may gradually slow down.

Something similar can happen with our habits.

Imagine that for years you have responded to criticism with anger.

Each episode may reinforce associations, expectations, and habitual ways of responding.

Over time, the reaction may become easier to trigger.

It might even appear before you have had time to understand what is happening.

But one day you begin to develop different habits.

You learn to recognize the earliest signs of anger.

You practice different responses.

You make an effort to understand others better.

And gradually, these new conditions can transform your behavior.

Yet that does not necessarily mean all your old tendencies disappear immediately.

They may return when you are tired.

Or when you are going through an especially difficult situation.

Or when someone, with admirable precision, finds that emotional button you have spent forty years trying to deactivate.

From the perspective of our model, we can use inertia as an analogy for understanding the persistence of certain conditioned patterns.

Past actions can help shape our present tendencies, even after we have begun developing different ways of responding.

I want to make it clear that this analogy is not a complete definition of Buddhist karma.

The tradition includes ethical dimensions and consequences that cannot be reduced to psychological habits.

But the image helps us explore a practical question:

What are we feeding through our actions?

## Positive karma, negative karma, and results

In everyday language we often speak of good and bad karma.

In Buddhism, we find distinctions between skillful or wholesome actions (*kusala*) and unskillful or harmful actions (*akusala*).

These categories are not simply about receiving a reward or a punishment.

They relate to the mental roots of actions and to their capacity to support or hinder liberation from suffering.

Traditionally, greed, hatred, and ignorance are regarded as unwholesome roots, while their absence or opposing qualities support wholesome actions.

From the perspective of our model, we can investigate the consequences of our intentions and behavior.

But we need to be careful here.

We cannot judge an intention solely by the immediate result we observe.

Imagine trying to help someone and, through an error of judgment, making their life more difficult.

Your intention may have been compassionate, even though your action had negative consequences.

Or imagine acting out of a desire to humiliate someone, only for the unexpected result to be that they learn something valuable.

That does not automatically make your intention wholesome.

The intention, our understanding of the situation, the action, and its consequences are related but distinct aspects.

And all deserve attention.

For our practical training, I would suggest examining three questions:

What intention is driving the action?

What consequences can we reasonably foresee?

What actually happens afterward?

This kind of investigation allows us to learn without turning every outcome into a final verdict on whether we are good or bad people.

Because an action’s consequences depend on many conditions.

We do not control all of them.

But we can learn to respond better to those we know about.

## Responsibility without an absolute controller

We have reached one of the most difficult questions in our model.

If our intentions are conditioned, are we responsible for our actions?

I think we need to distinguish two ways of understanding responsibility.

The first imagines a person as an agent completely independent of circumstances, able to choose responses from a place beyond all influence.

That interpretation is difficult to reconcile with our model.

The second sees responsibility as part of our capacity to learn, respond to consequences, repair harm, and change our future behavior.

That interpretation does not require an absolute controller.

Imagine you make a mistake and hurt someone.

Perhaps your reaction was automatic.

Perhaps you were tired.

Perhaps habits learned over many years contributed to what happened.

Understanding those conditions may be important.

But it does not erase the harm.

Nor does it remove the need to act.

You can acknowledge what you did.

Apologize.

Listen to the person who was hurt.

Repair whatever you can.

And work to reduce the likelihood of repeating it.

From the perspective of our model, those actions also depend on conditions.

But that does not make them any less important.

Indeed, they may be precisely the conditions that allow future behavior to change.

Understanding the causes of an action does not mean justifying it. It means having more information with which to respond appropriately to its consequences.

This distinction is especially important when our actions affect other people.

Compassion must include both the person who made the mistake and the person who suffered its consequences.

And understanding a reaction should never become an excuse to keep causing harm.

## You don't need to punish yourself to learn

There is a habit I know rather well.

Making a mistake.

Realizing it.

Feeling bad.

And then spending a considerable amount of energy mentally punishing myself for making it.

As if the additional suffering were some kind of compulsory tax on learning.

But what are we actually achieving?

Perhaps a degree of remorse helps us recognize the harm and develop a sincere intention to put things right.

That can be healthy.

Yet we can also become trapped in a cycle of guilt, rejection, and identification.

“I’m a bad person.”

“I always do the same thing.”

“I’ll never manage to change.”

These thoughts can create even more distress.

And that distress does not always encourage wiser behavior.

Sometimes it can even make it harder.

From the perspective of our model, we can ask something different:

What conditions contributed to my acting this way?

What consequences did it have?

What can I do now to repair the harm?

What conditions do I need to change to reduce the likelihood of repeating it?

This is not about automatically absolving ourselves.

It is about learning.

And learning may require far more honesty than self-punishment.

Because punishing ourselves allows us to remain focused on how terrible we feel.

Whereas learning forces us to look at what actually happened and what we need to change.

## Wisdom as a transformation of our intentions

Let’s return to one of the central ideas of this book.

If intentions arise depending on causes and conditions, we can also investigate how those conditions are transformed.

Imagine that for years you have responded to certain problems by trying to control absolutely everything.

You anticipate difficulties.

Construct scenarios.

Plan responses.

And end up exhausted trying to prevent events that might never happen.

But over time you learn to recognize this tendency.

You understand that many circumstances are beyond your reach.

You develop a greater tolerance for uncertainty.

And begin cultivating different responses.

Perhaps, when facing a complicated situation, the need to control everything no longer arises so strongly.

Perhaps a simpler intention emerges.

To listen.

To understand.

To act where it is useful.

To accept what you cannot change.

Change does not consist solely in forcing yourself to act differently.

It may mean that, gradually, different intentions begin to arise under similar circumstances.

And this strikes me as one of the deepest possibilities of practice.

We do not need to imagine an eternal battle between a conscious will and rebellious impulses.

We can explore how training changes the tendencies of the system.

Ethics helps develop ways of acting that reduce harm.

Concentration supports the stability and clarity needed to recognize what is happening.

Investigating our experience can help us understand and transform the conditions that feed certain reactions.

And these capacities reinforce one another.

Perhaps wisdom is not just knowing the best response.

Perhaps it also involves having developed the conditions that allow that response to arise more naturally.

## Can karma cease?

Here we encounter an especially interesting question.

In Buddhist teachings, the path to liberation is not simply about accumulating an infinite amount of good karma.

There is also talk of the cessation of karma and of the conditions that sustain the cycle of suffering.

From the perspective of our model, we can explore a functional interpretation.

Imagine that for years you have fed a dynamic of attachment, rejection, or identification.

Each reaction has contributed, in some way, to maintaining the conditions that encourage its repetition.

But through practice you develop a different understanding.

Certain experiences continue to arise.

Perhaps some old habits still manifest.

Yet you do not necessarily respond by feeding the same dynamics anymore.

Using our analogy, we might say that the wheel still has some inertia, but it is no longer receiving some of the pushes that used to keep it spinning.

This does not prove that we have attained Buddhist liberation.

Nor does it mean that a liberated person is incapable of performing any intentional action.

Buddhist traditions contain much more precise—and differing—explanations of the relationship between a liberated person’s actions, karma, and its fruits.

But our analogy allows us to investigate a useful distinction.

One thing is for consequences of earlier conditions to arise.

Quite another is to continue creating the conditions that sustain the same suffering.

And perhaps part of the path consists in learning to distinguish the two.

## A small experiment

Over the next few days, choose a habitual reaction you would like to understand better.

It does not have to be anything particularly serious.

Perhaps you interrupt people while they are speaking.

Perhaps you check your phone constantly.

Perhaps you respond impatiently when you are tired.

The next time that reaction arises, try observing three moments.

**Before acting:** What intention is emerging?

What sensations, emotions, or thoughts seem connected to it?

Can you recognize what you are trying to achieve or avoid?

**While acting:** Does what you are doing correspond to what you genuinely want to encourage?

Are you causing harm you could avoid?

Is there another way you might respond?

**After acting:** What consequences have you noticed?

What have you learned?

What could you change next time?

Do not turn this exercise into a moral examination.

You do not need to classify every thought as good or bad.

Nor do you need to constantly control every intention.

Simply try to develop curiosity about the processes that contribute to your responses.

If you discover that you have repeated an old habit, you do not need to punish yourself.

You can recognize it.

Learn something.

And keep practicing.

## What we do changes us

We reach the end of this chapter with an important idea.

In Buddhism, karma is deeply connected with intention and the consequences of our actions.

From the perspective of our model, we have proposed viewing intentions as conditioned processes that participate in creating new conditions.

This allows us to investigate how habits develop, how certain dynamics persist, and how training can transform them.

We do not need to assume we are agents completely independent of our circumstances.

But neither do we need to abandon responsibility.

Our actions matter.

What we do to other people matters.

And what we cultivate through our responses can profoundly influence how we live in the future.

Perhaps we are not free to choose every thought, emotion, or intention that arises.

But we can learn to develop conditions that favor wiser, more compassionate responses.

And, over time, perhaps some reactions we once had to restrain will simply begin to lose their strength.

Not because we have defeated a part of ourselves.

But because we have learned to live differently.

Karma need not be a sentence. It can be an invitation to understand that what we do also contributes to shaping what we become.

And if we are going to keep shaping ourselves through our actions, perhaps it is worth doing so with a little more love and wisdom.

Even if, now and then, we still eat the chocolate.
