# 7. Dukkha: The Problem We're Trying to Understand

I've got good news and bad news.

The bad news is that you're going to suffer.

The good news is that you probably knew that already.

And the truly interesting news is that perhaps you don't have to suffer as much as you think.

One of Buddhism's best-known teachings is that existence is marked by something called *dukkha*.

Traditionally, this Pali word is translated as “suffering”. But while useful, that translation can be inadequate.

*Dukkha* can also refer to dissatisfaction, unease and that feeling that things never quite turn out the way we'd like.

Sometimes it's terrible pain.

At other times it's something far subtler.

For example, imagine you've been looking forward to your holiday for months.

At last it arrives.

You're somewhere wonderful, the weather is lovely, there's a cold drink in your hand and you don't have to answer a single email.

Everything is perfect.

Until a thought appears.

“What a shame I have to go back to work in a week.”

Congratulations.

You've just introduced suffering into your holiday without absolutely anything changing.

It's a rather impressive skill.

And we all have it.

## Does that mean everything is suffering?

This is one of the interpretations of Buddhism that causes the most confusion.

We sometimes hear that the Buddha taught that life is suffering.

But that statement can convey a rather pessimistic picture.

As though Buddhism consisted of discovering that everything is awful and then spending forty years meditating in an attempt to accept it.

That doesn't strike me as a particularly attractive description.

And it doesn't do justice to the richness of his teachings either.

The Buddha acknowledged the existence of pleasure, joy, well-being and deeply satisfying mental states.

In fact, many Buddhist practices deliberately cultivate qualities such as tranquillity, rapture, gratitude, compassion and equanimity.

So what's the problem?

Our pleasant experiences aren't permanent, and we can't guarantee they'll satisfy all our desires.

A wonderful moment can end.

A relationship can change.

Our bodies grow old.

The people we love can suffer.

And even if we got everything we wanted, we'd probably end up wanting something else.

That doesn't mean we have to stop enjoying life.

It means that perhaps we're asking our experiences to provide something they can't always offer.

Permanent, guaranteed satisfaction, independent of circumstances.

And when that expectation isn't fulfilled, *dukkha* arises.

## Three ways of understanding dukkha

The Buddhist tradition offers a very useful classification of three forms of *dukkha*.

The first is *dukkha-dukkhatā*, the suffering of suffering.

It's the most obvious form: physical pain, illness, sadness, fear, loss or despair.

We don't need great philosophical knowledge to recognise it.

If you stub your little toe against the corner of a table, you're probably not going to wonder whether it's a manifestation of the unsatisfactory nature of conditioned phenomena.

You're more likely to express a rather strong opinion about the table.

The second form is *vipariṇāma-dukkhatā*, suffering associated with change.

Pleasant experiences change and end.

We can thoroughly enjoy something and suffer when it disappears.

But notice an important detail.

The problem doesn't necessarily lie in the experience ending.

It may also lie in our resistance to its ending.

A sunset lasts only a few minutes.

We can enjoy it fully or complain that it doesn't last forever.

The sunset is the same.

Our relationship with it can be very different.

The third form is *saṅkhāra-dukkhatā*, the unsatisfactoriness of the conditioned.

This one is harder to understand.

It refers to a deep characteristic of conditioned phenomena: they depend on causes and conditions, they change, and they cannot provide us with absolute security.

In our model, we might interpret it as a limitation of any system that tries to find definitive stability in experiences dependent on changing circumstances.

That doesn't mean every conditioned phenomenon must feel unpleasant.

It means we can't rely on it as an inexhaustible, unchanging source of satisfaction.

And perhaps here we find one of the keys to the problem.

## Pain, and what we do with it

Imagine someone criticises you.

You feel a pang of discomfort.

Perhaps tension appears in your chest. Perhaps shame. Perhaps anger.

So far, we have an unpleasant experience.

But then something else begins.

“Who do they think they are, speaking to me like that?”

“They always treat me this way.”

“I should have said that to them.”

“Next time they'll see.”

And for the next few hours, you keep reliving the situation.

The criticism lasted twenty seconds.

Your mind has spent the whole afternoon planning revenge that you'll probably never carry out.

Here we can distinguish two processes.

On the one hand, the unpleasant experience that arose under particular circumstances.

On the other, the subsequent reactions that can maintain, amplify or transform that discomfort.

The Buddhist tradition uses a beautiful image to explain this difference: the two arrows.

The first arrow represents the pain we experience.

The second represents the additional suffering we can generate through our reactions.

And here's an important clarification.

We can't always avoid the first arrow. But we can learn to notice when we're shooting ourselves with the second.

That doesn't mean the extra suffering is always voluntary.

Many of our reactions are automatic, deeply learned and difficult to change.

Nor does it mean that people who suffer are to blame for their suffering.

It means that, in certain circumstances, we can learn to change how we respond.

And that possibility is worth exploring.

## Dukkha as a conditioned process

Let's return to our model.

We've proposed looking at the mind as a system of interdependent processes.

Experiences arise depending on particular conditions.

Our responses affect subsequent conditions.

And some dynamics can reinforce themselves through repetition.

What happens if we apply this perspective to *dukkha*?

Imagine we experience something pleasant.

A feeling of well-being arises.

Then perhaps we want that experience to continue.

And we may start worrying about losing it.

The pleasant experience is still there, but now we've added a new condition: fear that it will end.

Or imagine an unpleasant experience.

Discomfort arises.

Then comes resistance.

“I don't want to feel this.”

That resistance can generate tension, frustration or further unpleasant emotions.

And those emotions can feed the resistance even more.

The system has begun reinforcing its own discomfort.

From this perspective, we can interpret an important part of *dukkha* as the result of conditioned dynamics that generate or perpetuate dissatisfaction.

I'm not claiming that all suffering can be explained this way.

Physical pain, illness, traumatic situations and social circumstances can require much broader explanations and interventions.

But our model can help us understand something important: certain forms of suffering can be maintained by the very mental processes trying to eliminate them.

We get angry because we're angry.

We worry because we're worried.

We blame ourselves for feeling emotions we consider inappropriate.

And each of these reactions can become a new condition for the next one.

A wheel that keeps turning.

## When we stop fighting

There's something I've learned over years of meditation that has profoundly changed my relationship with emotions.

An unpleasant experience doesn't always have to disappear in order to stop being a problem.

Sometimes it's enough to stop fighting it.

For a long time, I thought meditation was largely about learning to create pleasant mental states and avoid unpleasant ones.

And of course, learning to cultivate tranquillity, concentration and well-being is a valuable part of training.

But over time I began to discover something different.

An unpleasant emotion could arise, and I still didn't have to make it disappear immediately.

I could feel sadness without becoming a sad person.

I could experience fear without having to build a terrible story around it.

And I could acknowledge anger without letting it determine all my responses.

I don't always manage it.

The mind has an extraordinary ability to bring back old habits precisely when you start thinking you've overcome them.

Especially when I'm tired.

But the possibility of relating differently to whatever arises has been one of my practice's most valuable discoveries.

And here's an interesting paradox.

Sometimes the harder we try to eliminate an unpleasant experience, the more importance we give it.

And the more importance we give it, the harder it becomes to let it pass.

Perhaps the solution isn't always to make what we dislike disappear.

Perhaps, sometimes, it's to learn to stop feeding it.

## Must we eliminate all our desires?

By now, it might seem that Buddhism proposes giving up every desire.

Want nothing.

Expect nothing.

Don't enjoy anything too much.

An extraordinarily peaceful existence.

And possibly a rather boring one.

But here we need to distinguish different kinds of wanting.

Wanting our children to be well isn't the same as feeling unable to bear anything unpleasant happening to them.

Enjoying a meal isn't the same as needing to eat constantly to escape our discomfort.

Wanting to improve as people isn't the same as hating ourselves for not yet being who we imagine we should be.

Buddhist teachings distinguish different forms of desire, such as *taṇhā*, the thirst or craving that feeds suffering, and *chanda*, which depending on context can mean an intention or desire to act, even in a wholesome way.

Not all desires work in the same way.

And within our model, we can try to understand their consequences and the dynamics they help sustain.

Perhaps the point isn't to stop wanting.

Perhaps it's to learn to want without making what we want an indispensable condition for being at peace.

Even the desire to be free from suffering can form part of a healthy path.

Although if we become obsessed with not suffering, we'll probably end up suffering because we're still suffering.

Our minds really are creative.

## Suffering is not a failure

There's something I particularly want to make clear.

Suffering doesn't mean you've failed.

It doesn't mean you're weak.

Nor does it mean you're meditating badly or haven't understood the Dharma well enough.

We're living organisms, with vulnerable bodies, complex histories and relationships that matter to us.

Experiencing pain, fear, sadness or frustration is part of our human condition.

And we don't need to add another layer of judgement to these experiences.

“I shouldn't be feeling this.”

“After everything I've learned, I should have got past this.”

“There's something wrong with me.”

Perhaps we can begin with something much kinder.

Acknowledge what's happening.

Understand that it has causes.

And explore whether there's a way of responding that doesn't add even more suffering.

For me, this perspective is deeply connected with compassion.

When we understand that people don't freely choose every experience that arises in their minds, perhaps it becomes easier to treat them with kindness.

Including ourselves.

That doesn't remove our responsibility for our actions.

But it can radically change how we try to learn from our mistakes.

## A small experiment

Next time you experience a minor everyday annoyance, try observing what happens.

You don't need to begin with a particularly painful experience.

It could be a short wait, an irritating noise or an unexpected change of plans.

Imagine you're waiting in a queue.

The queue moves slowly.

You notice some impatience.

Nothing extraordinary so far.

Now notice whether thoughts like these appear:

“This is unbearable.”

“This always happens to me.”

“I'm wasting my time.”

“The person in front could hurry up a bit.”

You don't need to stop them appearing.

Nor do you have to convince yourself that waiting is wonderful.

Just observe what happens when those thoughts arise and how they affect your experience.

Does the tension increase?

Does irritation appear?

Do you start imagining even more unpleasant situations?

Then try something different.

Recognise that you're waiting.

That you'd rather not be.

And that, for the moment, this is what's happening.

Perhaps absolutely nothing will change.

Or perhaps you'll discover that part of your discomfort depended on the battle you were fighting against a situation you couldn't change at that moment.

This isn't about learning to endure every circumstance passively. If you can solve a problem, solving it may be the most sensible response.

It's about investigating what we're adding to our experience.

## What are we trying to understand?

Let's return to our original question.

What is *dukkha*?

In the Buddhist tradition it's a broad concept encompassing obvious suffering, dissatisfaction associated with change, and the inability of conditioned phenomena to provide definitive satisfaction.

Within our model, we'll be especially interested in investigating how particular conditioned reactions can generate, intensify or perpetuate that discomfort.

We're not going to try to build a mind incapable of experiencing pain.

Nor will we pursue an emotional perfection in which sadness, fear or frustration never appear.

We'll explore how our relationship with these experiences works.

Which conditions influence our responses.

Which habits help feed suffering.

And which abilities we can develop to respond with greater wisdom, equanimity and love.

Because perhaps the most important question isn't:

“How can I make sure nothing unpleasant ever happens to me?”

But:

“How can I learn not to hurt myself more than necessary when life doesn't go the way I'd like?”

And that's a question worth exploring.

In the next chapter, we'll encounter the Four Noble Truths, the framework the Buddha used to present suffering, its causes, its cessation and the path leading to that cessation.

What's interesting is that, viewed through our model, they can be understood not only as philosophical statements, but also as a practical proposal for investigating and transforming our experience.
