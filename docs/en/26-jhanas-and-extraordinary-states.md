# 26. Jhānas and Extraordinary States

I have something to confess.

When I started meditating, I expected to find tranquility.

Perhaps learn to concentrate.

Perhaps reduce stress.

And, if everything went particularly well, persuade my mind to stop arranging imaginary meetings while I was trying to sleep.

But I discovered something I hadn't expected.

Meditation could produce extraordinarily intense experiences of well-being.

And I'm not talking about feeling a little bit relaxed.

I'm talking about a deep, physical joy that could spread through my body and completely transform my experience for those moments.

Naturally, my first reaction was to ask:

“How on earth did nobody tell me about this before?”

And my second was to try to understand it.

That curiosity led me to investigate one of the most fascinating practices in Buddhism.

The jhānas.

But before continuing, I want to clarify something.

We're not turning this chapter into a guide to attaining extraordinary experiences.

I don't want to promise you indescribable states of happiness.

Or present eight levels you need to conquer to win some kind of world meditation championship.

We're going to do something I find much more interesting.

Investigate what these states might teach us about how our experience works.

And, especially, what place they might have on a path oriented toward developing wisdom and reducing suffering.

## What Are the Jhānas?

Jhāna is a Pali word associated with deep states of meditative absorption or collectedness. In Sanskrit, we find dhyāna.

The Buddhist discourses describe four jhānas characterized by different qualities of concentration, well-being, and equanimity.

They also describe four formless attainments, or arūpa samāpatti, associated with contemplative experiences beyond ordinary perceptions of form.

Modern explanations frequently group all these states together as the “eight jhānas.”

But it's worth remembering that early texts distinguish the four jhānas and the four formless attainments conceptually.

Why do they matter?

Because they show that our experience can be profoundly transformed by training certain mental abilities.

We can develop extraordinarily stable attention.

Experience well-being without immediately depending on external stimuli.

And cultivate states of equanimity we don't ordinarily encounter in everyday life.

But here comes a fundamental warning.

An extraordinary experience doesn't, by itself, prove we've developed extraordinary understanding.

We can learn a great deal from the jhānas.

But we shouldn't automatically confuse them with awakening.

## The Book That Helped Me Understand Them

I want to pause at a book that has been especially important in my exploration.

*Right Concentration: A Practical Guide to the Jhanas*, by Leigh Brasington.

Brasington is a meditation teacher who studied the jhānas with Ayya Khema and presents a practical approach based on his interpretation of the Pali discourses.

What interests me most about his proposal is that he treats the jhānas as experiences one can learn to cultivate.

Not as supernatural rewards.

Or as experiences necessarily reserved for extraordinary beings.

But as contemplative possibilities that may arise when we develop certain conditions.

His method places special importance on access concentration and pleasant sensations.

Once a certain degree of attentional stability has been achieved, he suggests using a pleasant sensation as the object of concentration.

By maintaining attention on it, well-being can intensify and encourage the appearance of pīti.

I found this approach particularly interesting.

Because it fitted something I'd begun to discover through my own experience.

Well-being could depend on conditions that were possible to recognize and, to some extent, cultivate.

But Brasington also insists on something fundamental.

The jhānas are not awakening.

Part of their value lies in preparing a more stable, clear, and available mind for contemplative investigation.

And here we find a direct connection to our three trainings.

Concentration can encourage certain experiences.

But we can then use that stability to investigate.

Understand.

And perhaps transform our responses.

We don't need to pursue pleasure indefinitely.

We can learn from it.

## Have I Experienced the First Four Jhānas?

Here I want to share my experience with as much caution as possible.

I believe I've experienced relatively light forms of the first four jhānas.

I don't say this as certification of a meditative attainment.

Nor do I claim that my experience corresponds exactly to every traditional description.

In fact, one difficulty in talking about the jhānas is that different teachers and schools use quite different criteria to identify them.

Some interpretations require very deep levels of absorption.

Others, such as Brasington's approach, work with more accessible experiences of concentration, often called “light jhānas.”

So claiming that someone has experienced a jhāna depends partly on the criteria being used.

My personal impression is that I've recognized experiences that fit reasonably well with the qualities described for the first four states.

Rapture.

Well-being.

Greater stability.

And a progressively greater role for equanimity.

But I think we need to distinguish three things.

What I've experienced.

The traditional classification that might correspond to it.

And the explanation I propose for its mechanisms.

The first belongs to my experience.

The second requires comparing descriptions with criteria that may vary.

And the third is a hypothesis.

We don't have to confuse them for the experience to have value.

In fact, I think distinguishing them is an important part of what we're learning.

## The Jhānas and Our Model

Let's return to our perspective.

The mind can be viewed as a system of conditioned processes.

Our attention depends on many factors.

Our emotions too.

And our habits influence how our experience is organized.

From this perspective, the jhānas suggest a fascinating possibility.

Perhaps, when we cultivate certain conditions, the system can develop forms of organization very different from its usual ones.

More stable attention.

Less distracting activity.

Changes in bodily experience.

And new relationships between concentration, pleasure, and equanimity.

We could provisionally describe them as meditative configurations arising when certain conditions come together.

But we must be careful.

We're not claiming to have identified exactly the neural mechanisms generating each jhāna.

Nor that every meditator experiences the same states in identical ways.

Our model attempts to offer a functional interpretation.

A perspective for investigation.

Not a definitive scientific explanation.

And perhaps that's precisely why the jhānas are so interesting.

They allow us to explore how far what we consider our normal experience may be just one of many possible configurations of a conditioned mind.

## First Jhāna: Discovering Rapture

Let's begin with the first.

Traditional descriptions of the first jhāna portray a mind temporarily withdrawn from sensual desires and unwholesome states.

Qualities such as pīti, rapture or exhilaration, and sukha, pleasure or well-being, arise.

They also mention vitakka and vicāra, terms interpreted differently across traditions, associated in this context with directing and sustaining mental activity on the object.

When I began experiencing pīti, something particularly surprised me.

The well-being was physical.

It wasn't simply thinking that I was happy.

It was an intense sensation of joy and energy that could spread through the body.

And gradually I began to recognize some of the conditions favoring its appearance.

That transformed my understanding of emotions.

Because it was clear that well-being could develop through internal processes, without anything special happening in the outside world.

I hadn't discovered a magical mechanism.

Or a permanent source of happiness.

But I had discovered an ability I hadn't known before.

And perhaps one of the first lessons we can draw from these experiences is precisely that.

Our well-being doesn't depend exclusively on what we achieve in the external world.

It also depends on conditions we may learn to cultivate.

Of course, not everyone will experience pīti with the same intensity.

And we shouldn't confuse just any pleasant sensation with attaining the first jhāna.

But even without classifying our experiences precisely, we can learn something valuable.

Concentration and well-being can reinforce each other.

And here I want to pause at a hypothesis I find particularly interesting.

## Pīti, the Default Mode Network, and a Possible Well-Being Feedback Loop

I'm about to enter territory where we need to be especially careful.

I want to suggest a personal interpretation of pīti.

Not a demonstrated scientific explanation.

A hypothesis.

Neuroscience studies a group of brain regions known as the default mode network.

This network participates in processes such as self-referential thinking, autobiographical recollection, and certain forms of spontaneous mental activity.

It isn't simply the “ego network.”

Nor is it a harmful structure we should switch off.

It serves important functions in our mental lives.

But some studies have found changes in its activity and connectivity during certain forms of meditation.

And here a possibility interests me.

Could the reduction or reorganization of certain forms of self-referential processing contribute to the appearance of meditative well-being?

Imagine that during concentration practice, some of the mental activity involving our worries, personal stories, and constant evaluation of events diminishes.

Perhaps that allows certain pleasant sensations to become more prominent.

Now imagine a second mechanism.

A small pleasant sensation appears.

Our attention turns toward it.

By attending to its pleasant aspects, perhaps its subjective intensity increases.

That increase makes us more inclined to continue attending to it.

And this attention may intensify the well-being again.

We've constructed a possible positive feedback loop.

Well-being → attention to well-being → greater well-being → greater ease in sustaining attention.

From a functional perspective, I find this hypothesis fascinating.

Because it could help explain why certain pleasant sensations become extraordinarily intense during practice.

And it fits part of Brasington's practical description.

However, here we need to distinguish explanatory levels carefully.

The possibility that attending to a pleasant sensation intensifies it doesn't demonstrate which brain circuits are responsible.

And the finding that some meditation practices modify the default mode network doesn't demonstrate that this modification produces pīti.

Nor do we know whether it would be correct to speak of simple relaxation or deactivation of that network.

Current research points to more complex changes in the organization and connectivity of different brain networks.

So my proposal is provisional.

Perhaps pīti involves an interaction between reduced interference from certain mental processes, sustained attention, and feedback mechanisms supporting well-being.

We don't know exactly how these elements relate.

But I think they're worth investigating.

Because if this explanation proves useful, it might help connect contemplative descriptions with functional and scientific models of experience.

Without needing to turn meditative rapture into a supernatural mystery.

And without prematurely reducing it to a single neural explanation.

## Second Jhāna: When Effort Steps Back

The second jhāna is traditionally described through a greater unification of mind.

The activity of directing and discursively evaluating the object settles down.

And the rapture and well-being associated with concentration remain.

Brasington highlights a particularly interesting transition.

In his explanation, the bodily intensity of pīti may recede while emotional well-being becomes more prominent.

Attention stabilizes around that experience of satisfaction.

We can interpret this transition through an idea we've already explored.

When we learn an ability, it may initially require deliberate effort.

But with training, some operations can become more natural.

Imagine learning to ride a bicycle.

At first you have to pay conscious attention to every detail.

Later, balance begins to maintain itself more spontaneously.

We shouldn't take this analogy literally.

The transition between jhānas is much more specific than learning a motor skill.

But perhaps it can help us picture a difference.

Sometimes maintaining attention requires continual intervention.

At other times, stability seems to sustain itself with much less deliberate effort.

And that can profoundly transform the experience.

From our model's perspective, we can investigate how certain forms of concentration come to depend less on repeatedly renewed conscious intention.

Perhaps training itself encourages stability sustained by the conditions that have been developed.

Here we find an interesting lesson.

Practice isn't always about trying harder. Sometimes it's about learning to stop interfering with an ability we've already developed.

But that doesn't mean we should pursue a complete absence of effort.

Or that every experience of ease is equivalent to the second jhāna.

We're using an analogy to understand a contemplative transformation described by tradition.

And like all our analogies, it has limits.

## Third Jhāna: When Joy Grows Quiet

We reach a transition I find particularly beautiful.

In traditional descriptions of the third jhāna, pīti recedes.

The intensity of rapture fades.

And greater equanimity, clarity, and a more serene form of well-being stand out.

Brasington uses a distinction here that I find especially helpful.

Well-being may shift from intense joy to quieter satisfaction.

A form of contentment.

Perhaps without needing anything special to happen.

This may seem strange.

Why would we want to leave behind an extraordinarily pleasant experience?

If we've managed to develop a state of intense rapture, shouldn't we try to preserve it?

Our usual intuition might say yes.

But perhaps we're discovering something important about our relationship with pleasure.

Not every pleasant experience needs to be intense.

And greater intensity doesn't always produce greater tranquility.

Imagine celebrating something extraordinary.

You're euphoric.

There's excitement.

Energy.

A very intense sense of joy.

Now imagine a quiet afternoon with someone you love.

Nothing special happening.

Nothing to celebrate.

Simply enjoying being together.

Both experiences can be pleasant.

But their qualities are different.

The second may include a serenity we don't necessarily find in the first.

I'm not claiming these everyday examples are equivalent to the jhānas.

But they may help us understand why a decrease in the intensity of rapture needn't mean a loss of well-being.

From our model's perspective, we can explore a possibility.

Perhaps certain forms of pleasant activation give way to other, calmer and more stable configurations.

And perhaps this transformation supports the development of equanimity.

Well-being doesn't have to keep growing in intensity to deepen in quality.

That seems particularly relevant in a culture that often equates happiness with accumulating increasingly stimulating experiences.

Perhaps we can also learn to appreciate things that don't need to impress anyone.

## Fourth Jhāna: Equanimity

In the fourth jhāna, traditional descriptions emphasize equanimity and the purity of mindfulness.

The earlier tones of pleasure and pain have been left behind, in the specific sense of this absorption.

And we find especially deep meditative stability.

Here an interesting question arises.

If intense pleasure no longer predominates, what's so special about this state?

Perhaps we should reconsider our idea of well-being.

For much of our lives, we seek pleasant experiences.

Try to preserve them.

And try to avoid unpleasant ones.

But the fourth jhāna invites us to consider a different possibility.

An experience in which equanimity occupies a central place.

Not because everything has become indifferent.

But because stability and balance no longer depend on maintaining a particular intensity of pleasure.

From our model's perspective, this may help us investigate how the relationships between sensation and response change.

We needn't conclude that the fourth jhāna represents definitive liberation from attachment.

Indeed, Buddhist teachings clearly distinguish attaining deep absorption from extinguishing the roots of suffering.

But we can recognize that these states demonstrate extraordinary possibilities for mental stability.

And perhaps that stability can provide favorable conditions for investigating experience more clearly.

There's something I find especially important.

We shouldn't confuse meditative equanimity with permanent indifference to the world.

Someone can develop very deep states of concentration and still need to learn how to relate to others.

Love remains fundamental.

And wisdom too.

## A Progression from Pleasure to Equanimity?

At this point, I want to offer a personal interpretation.

When I look at the four jhānas through our model, I see a possible functional progression.

First, concentration is associated with intense experiences of rapture and well-being.

Then, stability increases and deliberate activity becomes less prominent.

Later, intense rapture gives way to more serene forms of satisfaction.

And finally, particularly deep equanimity appears.

We could imagine it as a training process in which the mind learns to find stability while depending less and less on intensely pleasant experiences.

But we must be careful.

We don't know whether this description adequately represents every process involved.

Nor should we assume that the jhānas always develop linearly and identically in everyone.

Different traditions use different criteria and methods.

And a meditator may experience variations, setbacks, or difficulties.

Our model is one possible interpretation.

Not a demonstration.

Even so, I find something very valuable in this perspective.

The jhānas might teach us that concentration doesn't merely allow us to sustain attention.

It can profoundly transform our relationship with pleasure, tranquility, and equanimity.

And perhaps these transformations help develop useful conditions for contemplative investigation.

## “All Right. Keep Going”

There's something I want to say about how some teachers respond to extraordinary meditative experiences.

In certain traditions, when a student describes a particularly intense experience, the response can be surprisingly simple.

“All right. Keep going.”

Or:

“Don't cling to it. Continue practicing.”

I understand the intention behind this teaching.

Pleasant experiences can become objects of attachment.

We can also become excessively fascinated with meditative states.

And sometimes continually attempting to reproduce an experience can interfere with further investigation.

If the main goal of training is to develop equanimity, it makes sense to learn not to turn every extraordinary phenomenon into something we need to preserve.

But I want to express a different perspective here.

Not clinging to an experience doesn't mean we have to give up understanding it.

When I discovered pīti, I could have simply acknowledged that it was pleasant and moved on.

But that experience opened an extraordinarily valuable investigation.

It helped me question some of my ideas about emotions.

About the relationship between body and mind.

And about the conditions supporting well-being.

Why should we ignore those possibilities for learning?

Of course, we shouldn't get stuck indefinitely on every experience either.

Or turn practice into an obsessive search for explanations.

But I think there's a fundamental difference between chasing an experience and investigating it.

We can observe how it arises.

Recognize its conditions.

Examine our interpretations.

And ask what we've learned.

Then we can continue.

In fact, that seems to be one of the great possibilities of combining samatha and vipassanā.

Concentration allows us to explore experiences.

Investigation helps us understand them.

And equanimity can keep us from becoming trapped in attempts to preserve them.

We don't need to choose between understanding and letting go.

Perhaps we can cultivate both.

## Experience Needs a Framework, but the Framework Must Learn Too

There's something else I find particularly important.

When we undergo an extraordinary experience, we try to make sense of it.

We look for words.

Concepts.

Explanations.

And we normally use the mental frameworks we already know.

If we've studied certain Buddhist teachings, perhaps we interpret the experience through those concepts.

If our background is scientific, perhaps we look for physiological or neurological explanations.

And if our beliefs are religious, perhaps we find meanings connected with them.

This isn't necessarily a problem.

Models help us understand.

But we must remember that our interpretation is conditioned too.

The same experience can receive different explanations.

And they needn't all be equally correct.

That's why I want to suggest an investigative attitude.

When an extraordinary experience appears, we can ask:

What did I actually experience?

What conditions seem to have favored its appearance?

What interpretation am I constructing?

What other explanations might be possible?

Has anything changed in the way I live?

And perhaps one particularly important question:

Am I using my model to understand the experience, or am I forcing the experience to confirm my model?

Because we don't want to replace the unquestionable authority of a tradition with the unquestionable authority of our own interpretation.

Our model is conditioned too.

It can be mistaken too.

And it must change when we find reasons to revise it.

But that's precisely why I consider extraordinary experiences potentially valuable.

Not as automatic proof of some definitive truth.

But as opportunities to investigate aspects of our experience that normally lie beyond our reach.

Ignoring them completely might mean losing some of their potential.

Clinging to them may prevent us from moving forward.

Perhaps the most useful answer lies elsewhere.

Experience, investigate, learn, and let go.

## The Four Formless Attainments

So far, we've discussed the four jhānas.

But Buddhist traditions also describe four formless meditative attainments.

They're particularly deep contemplative experiences organized around objects or dimensions of perception very different from the usual ones.

They're known as:

1. The sphere of infinite space.

2. The sphere of infinite consciousness.

3. The sphere of nothingness.

4. The sphere of neither perception nor non-perception.

Their names are fascinating.

And I admit they can also be dangerously suggestive.

Because it's easy to read “infinite consciousness” and imagine we've found proof of a universal mind.

Or read “nothingness” and conclude we've discovered the absolute absence of reality.

But we must remember something fundamental.

The name of a contemplative experience doesn't demonstrate a metaphysical claim about the universe.

Let's explore them carefully.

## Fifth: The Sphere of Infinite Space

The first formless attainment is called ākāsānañcāyatana, the sphere of infinite space.

Traditional descriptions associate it with transcending certain perceptions of form and attending to an experience of boundless space.

Imagine for a moment that our usual perception of bodily and spatial boundaries changes profoundly.

Perhaps some of the references we normally use to organize space disappear.

And an experience of extraordinary spaciousness appears.

I'm not claiming this description reproduces the traditional state exactly.

It merely offers an intuitive orientation.

From our model's perspective, we can investigate what would happen if certain perceptual conditions changed profoundly.

Our ordinary experience of space depends on many processes.

Bodily perception.

Sensory signals.

Attention.

And our interpretations.

If some of those processes change, perhaps our experience of spatial extension changes too.

But we must avoid one conclusion here.

Experiencing boundless space doesn't necessarily mean we've proved that physical space is infinite.

Experience and an objective description of the universe are different questions.

And perhaps that distinction is especially important when investigating extraordinary contemplative states.

## Sixth: The Sphere of Infinite Consciousness

The second formless attainment is called viññāṇañcāyatana, the sphere of infinite consciousness.

That name can be even more suggestive.

Infinite consciousness.

Does it mean universal consciousness exists?

That all minds are part of a single mind?

That we've discovered a fundamental reality behind every experience?

Not necessarily.

The teachings describe a meditative attainment associated with a particular experience of boundless consciousness.

That doesn't, by itself, prove the existence of a universal mental substance.

From our model's perspective, we might investigate how certain configurations of attention and perception change our usual sense of the boundaries of consciousness.

But we shouldn't regard the metaphysical nature of what we experience as settled.

Here I want to share something personal.

Along my path, I've had experiences in which the usual sense of separation between observer and observed changed profoundly.

For some time, I interpreted some of them through ideas associated with nonduality.

But later I began questioning how necessary those interpretations really were.

The experience could be real as an experience.

Without my initial explanation needing to be definitive.

And I believe the same caution is appropriate when investigating the formless attainments.

We don't need to dismiss them.

Nor turn them into proof of our beliefs.

We can learn from them.

While continuing to ask what we're experiencing and what we're interpreting.

## Seventh: The Sphere of Nothingness

The third formless attainment is called ākiñcaññāyatana, the sphere of nothingness.

Here we need to be particularly careful with language.

The word “nothingness” may lead us to imagine an absolute absence of existence.

But the traditional description shouldn't automatically be confused with a philosophical claim that the universe doesn't exist.

We're talking about a specific contemplative state.

A configuration of experience described through the perception that “there is nothing.”

From our model's perspective, we could investigate how certain perceptual contents stop organizing our experience in the usual way.

Perhaps some of the references we normally regard as fundamental lose prominence.

But we don't need to conclude we've discovered the true nature of reality.

An experience of absence doesn't necessarily prove the objective absence of everything we usually perceive.

Here we find another reason to keep two questions separate.

What have we experienced?

And:

What can we reasonably conclude from that experience?

The first belongs to contemplative observation.

The second requires interpretation, comparison, and analysis.

Confusing them can lead us to conclusions that feel extraordinarily convincing.

But aren't necessarily correct.

## Eighth: Neither Perception nor Non-Perception

The fourth formless attainment is called nevasaññānāsaññāyatana.

The sphere of neither perception nor non-perception.

I admit the name isn't particularly straightforward.

It isn't exactly something you can explain at a family dinner without someone eventually asking whether you'd like another glass of wine.

Traditional accounts describe an extremely subtle contemplative attainment.

Perceptual activity becomes so refined that it isn't adequately described either as ordinary perception or as a complete absence of perception.

Here our model encounters obvious limits.

We can talk about different degrees of perceptual activity.

Changes in attention.

And experiences of extraordinarily subtle consciousness.

But it would be intellectually careless to claim we know exactly what happens in this state merely from a conceptual explanation.

Nor should we imagine we can understand it completely through an analogy.

Perhaps certain contemplative experiences require specific training to be recognized adequately.

But that doesn't mean we have to turn them into supernatural mysteries.

We can acknowledge that traditional descriptions of very deep states exist.

And admit that our understanding of them is limited.

I don't see that as an intellectual defeat.

Quite the opposite.

Perhaps it's a fairly sensible form of honesty.

## And What About the Cessation of Perception and Feeling?

There's another meditative attainment that deserves special attention.

Nirodha-samāpatti, the cessation of perception and feeling, also called saññāvedayitanirodha.

Traditional explanations relate it to the temporary cessation of perception and feeling and an extraordinarily deep suspension of mental activity.

The Theravāda tradition, especially its commentaries, reserves this attainment for practitioners with advanced liberating attainments and mastery of the formless absorptions.

It isn't simply a ninth state that anyone can attain after completing the previous eight.

Nor should we confuse it with deep sleep, loss of consciousness, or a subjective sense of emptiness.

But I want to pause here.

Because from our model's perspective, this attainment raises some of the most fascinating questions in the entire book.

And some of the hardest.

**What if even consciousness were conditioned?**

Let's imagine the traditional descriptions are correct in one very specific sense.

During nirodha-samāpatti, conscious experience ceases completely.

There are no thoughts.

No sensations.

No perception of time.

Not even an experience of darkness or emptiness.

Simply, during that interval, there's no phenomenal consciousness.

What could we learn from that?

My first speculation is that consciousness itself would be a conditioned phenomenon.

Not a permanent, unchanging foundation on which our experiences appear.

But something whose manifestation depends on certain conditions.

If those conditions cease, consciousness could cease.

And when the appropriate conditions come together again, conscious experience might reappear.

That would fit our model extraordinarily well.

We wouldn't need to imagine an independent consciousness that remains observing everything that happens.

We could view it as a function or process of the conditioned system itself.

But we need to be careful.

A person's experience of a discontinuity in consciousness doesn't by itself prove that every possible form of consciousness is conditioned.

Nor does it definitively resolve philosophical debates about its nature.

Nor does it demonstrate that states described by traditions correspond exactly to a complete interruption of all activity related to consciousness.

My proposal is a hypothesis.

A possibility that seems coherent with our model.

And one perhaps worth investigating.

**Can we experience the absence of experience?**

Now we reach my second speculation.

And I think it's even more interesting.

If there's no consciousness during that state, then we cannot consciously experience what happens during it.

Think about it.

To experience something, there must be an experience.

But if we're talking precisely about the complete cessation of experience, what could be observed?

There would be nobody watching an infinite darkness.

Because seeing darkness would already be an experience.

Nor would there be a sensation of emptiness.

Because feeling emptiness also involves experiencing something.

Nor would there be a perception of the absence of time.

Because recognizing the passage or absence of time requires conscious processes.

So if we provisionally accept that definition of complete cessation, a logical consequence seems to follow.

We can't directly know the interval as conscious content.

What we can recognize is that our experience shows a discontinuity.

And perhaps reconstruct what happened afterward.

Here I find an interesting connection with some explanations by Culadasa in *The Mind Illuminated*.

Discussing cessation events, Culadasa distinguishes between what can later be remembered as an interruption of conscious contents and what he describes, in certain circumstances, as objectless consciousness.

His explanation is connected to his model of the mind-system and consciousness.

It isn't exactly the same as my interpretation.

Indeed, there's an important question here: are we talking about a complete absence of consciousness, or an absence of conscious objects?

Because they're not necessarily equivalent.

My hypothesis concerns specifically the first case.

Complete cessation.

If there's no consciousness whatsoever, there can be no conscious experience of the interval.

And therefore, what we know about it would have to depend on what happens before and after.

**Deconstruction and reconstruction.** Here we come to what I consider the most revealing possibility.

Perhaps the most interesting thing about complete cessation isn't the state itself.

Because if it really lacks consciousness, there would be no experience to investigate while it occurs.

Perhaps the most interesting thing lies precisely at its boundaries.

The deconstruction of experience before cessation, and its subsequent reconstruction.

Imagine we could observe how certain functions of our experience begin to disappear.

Perhaps perception changes.

Then certain forms of identification.

Our usual representations of time.

The organization of conscious contents.

And eventually a point is reached at which experience ceases.

I'm not claiming that's the exact traditional sequence.

It's an image intended to illustrate what we might investigate.

Now imagine the reverse process.

Experience reappears.

Conscious contents arise.

Certain forms of perception return.

Interpretations appear.

Perhaps the familiar sense of being someone who experiences begins to organize itself again.

If we could recognize part of these processes, perhaps we'd discover something extraordinarily valuable.

Not necessarily about what exists during cessation.

But about how our ordinary experience is constructed.

And here our model would find fascinating territory for investigation.

Because we've spent much of this book looking at the mind as a conditioned system.

We've discussed the self as a construction.

Intentions as conditioned processes.

Attention, emotions, and interpretations.

But we normally observe all these phenomena while the system is operating.

What might we learn if we could recognize how some of them stop operating and reappear?

Perhaps a great deal.

Or perhaps we'd discover that our capacity for reconstruction is more limited than we imagined.

We should consider that possibility too.

Because a later interpretation isn't necessarily a faithful memory of every process that occurred.

We could mistake a break in memory for a cessation of consciousness.

Or attribute properties to an event that we couldn't directly observe.

So even this investigation would have important limitations.

But I find the question extraordinarily valuable.

If consciousness disappears and reappears depending on conditions, what can we learn by observing how our experience disorganizes and reorganizes itself?

**Something I haven't experienced.** Here I want to be especially clear.

As far as I can determine, I haven't consciously attained nirodha-samāpatti.

I don't know these states from personal experience.

I've read descriptions.

Studied interpretations.

And developed some hypotheses.

But I can't claim to have personally verified what happens.

I'd like to investigate it someday.

Not to win another meditative trophy.

Or to prove I've reached an extraordinary level.

But because I have a sincere curiosity about these processes.

Especially what we might learn from the disappearance and reappearance of conscious experience.

Yet I also recognize my current limits.

And I don't want to turn that interest into a need that dominates my practice.

There are reasons for caution with advanced meditative states, especially those requiring intensive training.

And perhaps there are many other ways to investigate how experience is constructed without attempting to attain complete cessation.

Curiosity doesn't oblige us to chase immediately everything we don't yet know.

We can formulate a question.

Acknowledge that we don't have an answer.

And keep learning.

**One last paradox.** There's something I find especially amusing.

We've spent thousands of years searching for extraordinary spiritual experiences.

And now we're considering the possibility that one of the most revealing might be precisely an experience that, strictly speaking, we couldn't experience while it happens.

Rather peculiar.

But perhaps there's an important lesson in that.

Not all knowledge comes from directly observing an object.

We can also learn by studying relationships.

Changes.

Conditions.

Absences.

And consequences.

Perhaps complete cessation could be revealing not because it contains a mysterious experience, but because its conditions of entry and exit might allow us to investigate the construction of our ordinary experience.

We don't know whether this interpretation is correct.

But it seems to me a hypothesis worth keeping open.

And for now, that's enough.

## The Jhānas Aren't a Competition

I want to return to something especially important.

When we learn these states exist, it's easy to imagine them as a ladder.

First jhāna.

Second.

Third.

And so on until the eighth.

Or, if we're particularly ambitious, all the way to cessation.

But perhaps we should be careful with that approach to practice.

Because we can turn the jhānas into a new measure of our worth.

Compare our experiences with others'.

Try to classify every session.

And grow frustrated when we can't reproduce a state.

From our model's perspective, that could become a new dynamic of attachment.

A pleasant experience generates desire.

Desire generates expectations.

Expectations encourage comparisons.

And comparisons may produce dissatisfaction.

We've managed to turn a practice intended to develop concentration and clarity into a new source of suffering.

Quite impressive.

That's why I want to remind you of something.

We don't need to attain all the jhānas to practice the Dharma.

Nor should we assume that someone who accesses deeper states of concentration necessarily possesses more wisdom or compassion.

The relationship between concentration and understanding matters.

But it isn't automatic.

And perhaps the more useful question isn't:

“Which jhāna have I attained?”

But:

“What am I learning, and how is it transforming the way I live?”

## Samatha and Vipassanā: Two Abilities That Support Each Other

Let's return to our three trainings.

The jhānas are particularly associated with the development of samatha and samādhi.

But what happens with vipassanā?

Buddhist tradition relates states of concentration to the development of liberating understanding, although there are different explanations of how the practices combine.

For example, we can use a stable mind to investigate more clearly the sensations, thoughts, and processes that arise.

We can also observe the impermanence of meditative experiences.

Recognize that even the deepest states of well-being depend on conditions.

And discover that trying to preserve them can become a form of attachment.

But here we must avoid a mistake.

An experience of absorption doesn't automatically guarantee understanding.

We can develop an extraordinary ability to concentrate without transforming all our habits.

And we can intellectually understand impermanence without recognizing it deeply in our experience.

That's why I consider it so important to maintain all three trainings.

Love.

Concentration.

Investigation.

Each contributes in a different way.

And perhaps some of the most valuable transformations arise when they support each other.

Concentration can facilitate investigation.

Investigation can transform our relationship with pleasure.

And love can help us use our abilities without turning them into tools for feeling superior to others.

Because perhaps a mind capable of extraordinary concentration, yet unable to treat other people kindly, still has a great deal to learn.

## Caution Is Wisdom Too

Before finishing, I want to add a warning.

Intensive meditation practices can produce very powerful psychological and physiological experiences.

They're not always pleasant.

And not everyone responds in the same way.

Under certain circumstances, sleep disturbances, anxiety, disorientation, unusual perceptual experiences, or excessive emotional activation may arise.

That's why I don't recommend pursuing deep states of absorption through intensive practice without adequate preparation.

Especially for people with a history of psychological destabilization, professional guidance is important, as is avoiding practices that significantly disrupt sleep or everyday balance.

We shouldn't automatically interpret every extraordinary experience as a sign of spiritual progress.

And we certainly shouldn't increase meditation time simply because an experience seems fascinating.

Intensity doesn't guarantee wisdom.

And our aim isn't to discover how far we can push the mind before something goes wrong.

It's to learn to relate better to our experience.

Perhaps some people will find deep value in advanced investigation of the jhānas.

Others may prefer a simple practice of concentration and goodwill.

Both paths can be valuable in their respective contexts.

We don't need to explore every contemplative possibility to benefit from the Dharma.

Nor should we turn our personal preferences into universal requirements.

## A Small Investigation: Pleasure and Stability

I'm not going to suggest an exercise to attain the first jhāna.

Let alone the eighth.

That would be an unrealistic recommendation for an introductory book.

But we can investigate something much simpler.

During a short concentration practice, notice whether any pleasant moments arise.

Perhaps a sense of tranquility.

A little satisfaction at recognizing a distraction.

Or a bodily feeling of well-being.

You don't need to produce anything.

Or chase it.

Simply observe.

What happens when something pleasant appears?

Can you recognize it without immediately trying to intensify it?

Does the desire for it to continue appear?

Does your attention begin to get caught up in preserving it?

And when it disappears, what happens?

Does frustration arise?

Or can you continue the practice peacefully?

This small exercise doesn't attempt to reproduce the jhānas.

It simply allows us to investigate something important.

Our relationship with pleasure.

And perhaps we'll discover that enjoying and clinging aren't exactly the same thing.

We don't need to reject what's pleasant.

We can learn to appreciate it without making demands of it.

And perhaps that ability will be useful far beyond meditation.

## What I Really Learned from Rapture

I want to return to my experience with pīti.

Because something about it marked me profoundly.

Before discovering those sensations, my understanding of happiness was much more closely tied to what happened in my life.

Achieving things.

Solving problems.

Reaching goals.

And enjoying pleasant experiences.

Of course, all those circumstances can influence well-being.

But discovering that I could experience such intense joy through concentration changed my perspective.

I began to recognize that our emotional state also depends deeply on internal processes we may learn to cultivate.

That didn't make me a permanently happy person.

Nor solve all my problems.

Or prove I'd attained any extraordinary spiritual realization.

But it changed some of my questions.

Instead of asking only what I needed to achieve to feel good, I began becoming interested in the conditions involved in my own experience of well-being.

And that opened an investigation that's still ongoing.

Perhaps that's the most important lesson I want to share.

Not that you need to experience pīti.

Or attain particular states of absorption.

But that human experience has possibilities we may not yet know.

And we can explore them.

With curiosity.

With caution.

And without turning every discovery into a definitive truth.

## Beyond the Extraordinary

We've explored the four jhānas and the four formless attainments.

We've talked about rapture, well-being, and equanimity.

And also about the possibility that, in certain contemplative attainments, conscious experience itself might cease.

We've recognized some of our model's limitations.

We don't know how to explain all these states completely.

Nor can we conclude that traditional descriptions correspond to perfectly identified neurophysiological configurations.

But we can recognize something important.

Experience can be profoundly transformed through training.

And those changes can help us investigate our relationship with attention, pleasure, perception, and consciousness.

However, I want to finish by recalling something.

Perhaps the most extraordinary experiences aren't necessarily those that transform our lives the most.

We can attain a wonderful state of concentration.

And continue responding with hostility to criticism.

We can experience a profound sense of infinite space.

And still struggle to listen to our partner.

Or discover extraordinary tranquility during meditation.

And completely lose patience when our children don't put their toys away.

Contemplative states can be valuable.

But they don't replace everyday learning.

They don't replace responsibility.

Or love.

Perhaps the most important question isn't how far we can go during meditation.

But what happens when we return.

When we open our eyes.

Get up.

And relate to the world again.

Yet I don't want to reduce these experiences to mere tools for achieving a calmer mind either.

I believe they can teach us something.

About our emotions.

About attention.

About pleasure.

And about the extraordinary flexibility of our experience.

Perhaps even about the conditions allowing consciousness itself to arise.

Although that last point remains an open question for now.

We can learn not to chase experiences.

Without having to ignore them.

We can recognize that they depend on conditions.

Without denying their value.

And we can use them to revise our models, rather than as evidence that we already know all the answers.

An extraordinary experience doesn't need to become an absolute truth to teach us something extraordinarily valuable.

Perhaps that's a good way to view the jhānas.

Not as trophies.

Or proofs of spiritual superiority.

But as fascinating possibilities of a conditioned mind we're still learning to understand.

In the next chapter we'll face an even harder question.

What does it mean to awaken?

And perhaps we'll discover that, after exploring all these extraordinary states, the answer has less to do with attaining a special experience than with transforming our relationship with what we're already living through.

Although, of course, we'll have to examine that interpretation carefully too.

Because by this point in the book, you should have learned something about me.

Every time I think I've found a definitive answer, I get an extraordinary urge to investigate it all over again.
