# 15. Love: Learning to Relate to the World (sīla)

I have a theory.

I believe the world would be a considerably better place if we learned to love a little more.

And I don't mean only loving our partners, our children, our friends, or that dog who looks at us as though we were the most extraordinary person in the universe.

I mean something broader.

Learning to relate to ourselves, to others, and to whatever happens with affection, understanding, and a wish not to cause unnecessary suffering.

Sounds fairly simple.

Until somebody cuts in front of you in the supermarket queue.

Then, suddenly, all our universal love is subjected to an extraordinarily demanding test.

But perhaps that's exactly what makes it interesting.

Because loving when everything goes our way can be fairly easy.

The question is what happens when things get complicated.

And, above all, whether we can learn to respond differently.

## What does love have to do with the Dharma?

In the previous chapter we presented three broad trainings that we'll use throughout the book.

Learning to relate with love.

Learning to concentrate.

And learning to investigate our experience.

The first is inspired by *sīla*, ethical conduct.

In Buddhism, sīla involves training our actions, speech, and ways of relating to others. It includes commitments such as avoiding harm, acting honestly, and developing behaviors that support our own well-being and that of others.

Traditionally, it is expressed through precepts and practical recommendations.

But I'd like to offer another way to approach this training.

What would happen if, rather than starting by asking which rules we ought to obey, we began by asking how we might learn to relate with love?

I'm not claiming that love and sīla are exactly the same thing.

Ethical conduct requires discernment, responsibility, and criteria that cannot be reduced to feeling affection.

But I think love can give us a very valuable orientation.

Because when we genuinely love somebody, we normally don't wish to hurt them.

Their well-being matters to us.

We try to understand them.

And when we make a mistake, we want to put it right.

Of course, loving someone doesn't guarantee we'll behave correctly.

We all know situations in which people who love each other end up hurting each other.

But that doesn't invalidate love.

It reminds us that we also need to learn to express it wisely.

## Love is an art

Years ago I came across an idea I found extraordinarily interesting.

In *The Art of Loving*, published in 1956, Erich Fromm proposes looking at love as an art that can be learned and cultivated.

Not something we simply have or don't have.

Nor a mysterious emotion that appears when we meet the right person.

But a capacity requiring knowledge, discipline, patience, attention, and practice.

Think about that for a moment.

Nobody expects to learn to play the piano simply because they love music.

Nor do we expect to become excellent cooks because we have a deep love of Spanish omelette.

Although, being Spanish, I believe that last one really ought to count as a professional qualification.

We know that developing a skill takes training.

And yet, when we talk about love, we often seem to expect everything to happen spontaneously.

As though love were an absolute quality.

Something that appears, endures, and, if it's real, needs no learning.

Perhaps part of this way of understanding love is connected to certain interpretations of our cultural and religious heritage, including the Christian tradition.

Though it would be unfair to reduce the entire richness of Christian thought to a single view of love.

What I want to emphasize is a distinction I consider fundamental.

What if loving weren't merely something we feel, but also something we can learn to do increasingly well?

This is one of the ideas from Fromm that interests me most.

Because it profoundly changes our relationship with love.

If it's a skill, we can develop it.

We can make mistakes.

We can recognize our limitations.

We can learn from other people.

And we can practice.

This perspective fits especially well with our model of the conditioned mind.

The way we love also depends on causes and conditions.

Our experiences.

The habits we've developed.

How we interpret other people's behavior.

Our ability to recognize emotions.

And the responses we've learned to offer.

If these conditions can change, perhaps our capacity to love can change too.

We don't need to wait until we're extraordinary people.

We can start practicing with what we have.

And here I'd like to share a personal conviction.

I believe learning to love more, and better, could be one of the most valuable purposes of our existence.

Perhaps even our destiny as human beings on this Earth.

I know that sounds rather emotional.

And perhaps we ought to put some violin music on in the background.

But I mean it.

If, by the end of our lives, we've learned to understand others better, cause less harm, and love more deeply, I don't think we'll have wasted our time.

## What do we mean by love?

The word *love* can mean many things.

We can speak of romantic love, family affection, friendship, or the fondness we feel for other people.

But Buddhism offers concepts that can broaden our understanding.

One is *mettā*, usually translated as loving-kindness or goodwill.

Mettā doesn't demand that we feel an intense emotion toward everyone.

It's about sincerely wishing beings well.

Another important concept is *karuṇā*, compassion, which leads us to recognize suffering and wish for its relief.

We also find *muditā*, appreciative or sympathetic joy at others' well-being, and *upekkhā*, equanimity.

These four qualities are known as the *brahmavihāras*, or sublime abodes.

And together, I think, they describe something beautiful.

We can wish people well.

We can recognize their suffering.

We can rejoice in their happiness.

And we can learn to relate to them without being completely carried away by our preferences and aversions.

We don't have to feel the same about every person.

Or love everyone in the same way.

But perhaps we can learn to recognize people's worth even when we dislike their behavior.

And that can profoundly transform our relationships.

## Who do you love most in the whole world?

I have two sons.

And there's a question I've taught them to answer since they were little.

“Who do you love more than anyone else in the whole world?”

The answer I've taught them is very simple:

“Myself.”

Yes.

Themselves.

It might sound selfish.

Especially when you say it to someone expecting to hear “Mum,” “Dad,” or perhaps “my little brother.”

But to me, it's an enormously important lesson.

I want my children to learn to love themselves.

Unconditionally.

Without continually having to prove they deserve that love.

Without waiting to become better students, better athletes, or better people.

I want them to know they have worth simply because of who they are.

And I want them to learn something even more important.

Loving ourselves is also a decision. A decision we can make and cultivate.

I don't mean we can simply decide to feel wonderful every morning.

Or that looking into the mirror and repeating how extraordinary we are will do the trick.

I mean deciding to treat ourselves with kindness.

Deciding that our well-being matters too.

Learning to protect, respect, and look after ourselves.

Even when we don't like ourselves very much.

Especially then.

Because loving yourself doesn't mean being permanently satisfied with yourself.

It means recognizing your worth without turning it into a reward you must earn.

And that's one of the things I most want to pass on to my sons.

Not that they be perfect.

Or stand out above everybody else.

But that they learn to live without becoming their own enemies.

## The strange prestige of suffering

I was born and raised in Spain.

And over time, I've begun questioning something about the culture in which I grew up.

I have the impression that in certain circles there's a particular admiration for people who constantly sacrifice themselves for others.

The person who never complains.

Who is always available.

Who puts other people's needs before their own.

Who suffers in silence.

And who seems to become a better person the more they sacrifice.

Of course, not everybody in Spain thinks this way. And this tendency isn't unique to my country.

But it's something I've recognized in my own cultural experience.

Sometimes suffering seems to confer a kind of moral prestige.

As though taking care of ourselves were selfish.

As though enjoying life were suspicious.

As though loving others deeply necessarily required loving ourselves less.

I no longer share that view.

I don't believe a person is better because they suffer more.

Nor do I believe systematically sacrificing our well-being is some superior demonstration of love.

Naturally, there are situations in which caring for someone requires enormous effort.

Sometimes we must give up important things for the sake of those we love.

And those sacrifices can be extraordinary expressions of generosity.

But there is a fundamental difference between making sacrifices when circumstances require them and turning sacrifice into our identity.

Between helping somebody because we love them and feeling that we only have worth when we neglect our own needs.

And here is an image I find particularly useful.

## The oxygen mask

If you've ever flown, you've probably heard the safety instructions.

In the event of a loss of cabin pressure, oxygen masks will drop down.

And if you're traveling with children, you must put your own mask on first, before helping them with theirs.

The first time we hear this instruction, it can seem strange.

Shouldn't we protect our children first?

Shouldn't a good mother or father always put their children's needs first?

Well, no.

Because if you lose consciousness through lack of oxygen, you'll hardly be able to help anyone.

Least of all your children.

The instruction isn't an invitation to selfishness.

It's an intelligent way to care for others.

Taking care of yourself can be a necessary condition for taking care of others.

And I think this image applies to many aspects of life.

If we're permanently exhausted, patience may become harder.

If we continually ignore our needs, we may end up developing resentment.

If we treat ourselves with contempt, we may find it harder to offer genuine understanding to others.

That doesn't always happen, but it's worth investigating.

From the perspective of our model, looking after our own conditions can profoundly affect how we relate to other people.

Sleeping.

Resting.

Recognizing our emotions.

Asking for help.

Setting boundaries.

And learning to treat ourselves kindly.

These aren't necessarily selfish activities.

They can be part of our capacity to love.

That's why I want my sons to answer “myself” when I ask whom they love more than anyone else.

Not because they should consider themselves more important than everybody else.

But because I want them to understand that they, too, deserve their own love.

That their worth doesn't depend on how much they sacrifice for others.

And that caring for yourself isn't the opposite of caring for others.

Perhaps it's one of its conditions.

## Everybody has a story

Imagine meeting somebody who behaves unpleasantly.

They interrupt constantly.

They speak aggressively.

They don't listen.

And they seem convinced that the universe revolves around their needs.

You probably wouldn't find them particularly likable.

But now imagine learning something about their history.

Perhaps they grew up in a difficult environment.

Perhaps they've spent years feeling rejected.

Perhaps they've learned to defend themselves constantly because, for a long time, no one defended them.

We don't know whether any of those explanations are true.

And we shouldn't invent stories to justify harmful behavior.

But we can recognize something important.

We don't know all the conditions that have contributed to somebody behaving the way they do.

Now let me offer a little thought experiment.

Think again about that unpleasant person. (Replace “person” with “four-year-old child” and notice how your interpretation changes.)

Curious, isn't it?

Perhaps a different attitude suddenly appears.

Instead of seeing a badly behaved person, we see a child still learning to regulate emotions.

Rather than interpreting every behavior as a conscious decision to harm others, we start considering which needs, difficulties, or experiences may be influencing them.

The behavior hasn't changed.

Our interpretation has.

And that interpretation can profoundly alter our response.

Why is it easier for us to understand that a child is conditioned by circumstances?

Do we somehow stop being conditioned when we turn eighteen?

Of course, abilities and responsibilities change with age.

An adult may possess resources a child hasn't yet developed.

But that doesn't mean adult behavior no longer depends on causes and conditions.

Perhaps we can try to understand adults with a little of the patience we show children.

Without infantilizing them.

Without removing responsibility.

And without having to justify harmful conduct.

Simply recognizing that behind every behavior lies a story.

## Love doesn't mean allowing everything

Here we need to make a fundamental clarification.

Loving does not mean accepting any behavior.

It doesn't mean allowing someone to hurt us.

Or giving up on protecting those who depend on us.

Nor does it mean avoiding every conflict.

Imagine someone mistreats your child.

You can understand that this person probably has their own difficulties.

You can recognize that their behavior depends on circumstances you don't know.

You may even feel compassion for them.

But that doesn't mean you must allow them to keep hurting your child.

You can intervene.

Set boundaries.

Ask for help.

And act as firmly as necessary to protect your child.

The difference is that you don't need to turn the other person into an enemy you must hate.

You can reject their behavior without reducing their entire identity to it.

Compassion doesn't remove boundaries. It may help us establish them more clearly.

And there's something else.

We deserve that protection too.

Loving ourselves means recognizing our needs and our worth.

Sometimes acting with love means saying no.

Leaving a harmful relationship.

Or stopping our attempts to solve problems beyond our reach.

Love doesn't have to be soft.

It can be extraordinarily firm.

But it doesn't need to feed on hatred.

## Learning to love ourselves

Many people can show enormous patience toward others while treating themselves with terrible harshness.

They make a mistake and begin punishing themselves.

They fail to meet an expectation and feel inadequate.

They experience an unpleasant emotion and conclude they should be different.

For a long time, I knew this way of treating myself very well.

I had an image of who I should be.

An ideal version who always knew what to do, never made mistakes, and could solve any problem.

An extraordinary person.

Pity he didn't exist.

And the harder I tried to become that person, the more reasons I found to be dissatisfied with myself.

Over time I began understanding something else.

I didn't have to hate the parts of myself I wanted to change.

I could recognize that many of my habits had arisen in response to particular circumstances.

Some had been helpful.

Others had become sources of suffering.

But I didn't need to declare war on them.

I could try to understand them.

And, sometimes, even feel grateful for what they'd been trying to do for me.

That doesn't mean all my behavior was appropriate.

Or that I could stop taking responsibility.

It means I could learn to correct myself without despising myself.

And perhaps that's one of the most important forms of love we can develop.

We don't need to be perfect to deserve our own affection.

We don't even need to wait until we've changed.

We can begin now.

With our difficulties.

Our contradictions.

And everything we're still learning.

## Responsibility is love too

There's an idea I consider fundamental.

Understanding our conditioning doesn't free us from responsibility for our actions.

Imagine that during an argument, you respond aggressively to someone you love.

Perhaps you were tired.

Perhaps you were under pressure.

Perhaps habits learned over many years contributed to your response.

Understanding those conditions may help you avoid repeating it.

But it doesn't undo the harm you've caused.

The other person may be hurt.

They may need protection, repair, or time.

And your understanding of the causes doesn't replace any of those needs.

So what can we do?

Recognize what happened.

Listen.

Apologize when appropriate.

Repair whatever we can.

And work to change the conditions that encouraged our behavior.

That, too, is love.

Perhaps even one of its most important expressions.

Because loving somebody isn't simply feeling something pleasant toward them.

It also means their well-being genuinely matters in our actions.

And that takes learning.

Sometimes a lot of it.

## Love as a training

Let's return to Erich Fromm's idea.

If love is an art, we can practice it.

We don't have to wait to feel some extraordinary emotion.

We can develop capacities that allow us to love better.

Buddhist traditions have particular practices to cultivate *mettā*, loving-kindness.

One consists of directing wishes for well-being toward ourselves and other people.

We can begin with someone we love.

Remember that this person wants to be happy and free from suffering.

And sincerely cultivate the wish that they find well-being.

Then we can gradually extend that attitude to people with whom we aren't as close.

Even, when we're ready, to people we find difficult.

We don't need to force ourselves to feel a particular emotion.

Nor pretend to feel affection.

We're training a disposition.

A way of orienting the mind toward well-being.

And, as with other capacities, practice can encourage its development.

But that's not the only way to train love.

We can also practice through everyday actions.

Listening.

Helping.

Acknowledging our mistakes.

Expressing gratitude.

Or taking a few seconds to understand someone before judging them.

We don't need to wait for extraordinary love before beginning to behave lovingly.

Sometimes actions can help develop the very dispositions that make them increasingly natural.

And here our model of the conditioned mind becomes especially useful.

We can develop habits of listening.

We can train our ability to recognize other people's suffering.

We can learn to interpret particular behaviors differently.

And we can cultivate intentions that encourage more compassionate responses.

Perhaps loving better doesn't mean discovering an emotion that was always hidden somewhere.

Perhaps, at least in part, it means transforming the conditions that allow us to relate differently.

## The joy of others

There's one Buddhist quality I'd particularly like to highlight.

*Muditā*, appreciative or sympathetic joy.

It means learning to rejoice in other people's happiness and well-being.

Sounds simple.

Until someone gets exactly what we wanted.

A colleague gets promoted.

A friend achieves something we've spent years trying to accomplish.

Or we discover that somebody else seems to have a far more interesting life than ours.

And suddenly our joy for others develops a few technical difficulties.

Comparison appears.

Perhaps envy.

Perhaps a little feeling of injustice.

Through our model, we can investigate what's happening.

Someone else's success doesn't necessarily harm us.

But it may activate a story about who we are and what we should have achieved.

And that interpretation can generate suffering.

Muditā invites us to develop a different relationship.

Other people's happiness doesn't have to threaten ours.

We can learn to celebrate it.

Even when our own lives aren't going exactly as we'd wish.

And perhaps we'll discover something wonderful.

The more people whose well-being makes us happy, the more opportunities we have to experience joy.

Not a bad deal.

## Equanimity: loving without possessing

Another fundamental quality is *upekkhā*, equanimity.

It is sometimes confused with indifference.

But they aren't the same.

Indifference can mean that something or someone no longer matters to us.

Equanimity allows us to recognize what is happening without being completely swept away by our reactions.

And this can be especially important when we love someone.

Imagine your child is going through a difficult time.

Naturally, you want to help.

Perhaps you feel worried, sad, or afraid.

But what happens if those emotions come to dominate your response completely?

Perhaps you try to control every aspect of your child's life.

Perhaps you can't bear to see them frustrated.

Or perhaps you end up making decisions that relieve your own distress without necessarily helping them learn.

Love may need equanimity.

Because loving someone also means recognizing that we cannot control all their experiences.

We can accompany them.

Protect them.

Teach them.

Set boundaries.

And help when needed.

But we cannot live somebody else's life for them.

Not even our children's.

From our model's perspective, equanimity can be understood as a capacity that allows us to remain available to respond appropriately without becoming completely hijacked by our reactions.

It doesn't necessarily make affection disappear.

It may allow us to express it more clearly.

Equanimity isn't about loving less, but learning to love without needing to control everything we love.

## When love and wisdom meet

We've talked about love as a capacity we can cultivate.

But let me offer a reflection.

What happens when we deeply understand that people are conditioned systems?

That their behavior depends on extraordinarily complex histories.

That many reactions arise without being consciously chosen.

That their mistakes needn't define them forever.

And that, like us, they're trying to find well-being while confronting difficulties they may not even understand.

Doesn't it become a little easier to love them?

Not necessarily automatically.

We can understand all these ideas intellectually and still feel aversion toward certain people.

But perhaps that understanding can gradually become a condition that encourages love.

And perhaps love can also encourage understanding.

When we stop requiring others to be different before recognizing their worth, we can listen to them better.

When we don't constantly need to prove we're right, we may discover perspectives we previously ignored.

And when we stop treating ourselves as enemies, perhaps we can observe our mistakes with a level of honesty that used to feel too painful.

Here I begin to suspect that love and wisdom are deeply connected.

I don't want to claim they're exactly the same thing.

But perhaps when understanding sufficiently transforms how we see other people, love can arise as a natural consequence.

And perhaps cultivating love is also a way of developing wisdom.

Not because all our preferences disappear.

Nor because we come to feel affection for every behavior.

But because we start relating to people with a broader understanding of what they are.

Complex beings.

Vulnerable.

Conditioned.

And valuable.

Exactly like us.

## A small experiment

Let's practice something simple.

Over the next few days, choose someone you have regular contact with.

Your partner, perhaps, a friend, a colleague, or a family member.

You don't need to choose the person you find hardest to tolerate.

Let's start reasonably.

Take a few moments to remember that this person has an inner life just as complex as yours.

They have worries.

Desires.

Memories.

Difficulties.

And probably many experiences you know nothing about.

Now ask yourself:

What could I do today to contribute to their well-being?

It needn't be anything extraordinary.

Perhaps listen to them.

Thank them for something.

Offer help.

Or simply give them a few minutes of your attention without thinking about the next thing you have to do.

Then observe what happens.

How did you feel?

How did the other person respond?

Did something change in your relationship?

Don't look for a particular result.

And above all, don't turn the exercise into a strategy for getting others to behave as you want.

We're practicing a way of relating.

Not manipulating the system to obtain rewards.

Finally, try something similar with yourself.

The next time you make a mistake, notice how you speak to yourself.

Does contempt appear?

Guilt?

A story about not being good enough?

Ask yourself how you'd respond if somebody you loved had made the same mistake.

Perhaps you can offer yourself some of that understanding.

Without denying what happened.

Without giving up on correcting it.

Simply remembering that you deserve affection too.

And if you have children, perhaps you can teach them a question I consider fundamental.

“Who do you love more than anyone else in the whole world?”

It doesn't much matter what they answer the first time.

The interesting thing is helping them discover that they, too, can be among the people they love most.

And that this affection can accompany them throughout their lives.

## A world built with our hands

There's an idea I've carried with me for quite some time.

The world isn't made better through our ideas alone. It's made better with our hands.

We can understand the deepest Buddhist teachings.

We can develop extraordinarily sophisticated theories of consciousness.

We can spend hours discussing emptiness, karma, and nirvana.

But if none of that knowledge changes, even a little, how we relate to others, perhaps we should ask what we're actually learning.

I'm not saying philosophical understanding is worthless.

Quite the opposite.

It can help us investigate experience and develop a broader view.

But love needs to find expression in the world.

In how we listen.

How we speak.

How we treat those who depend on us.

How we respond to people we find difficult.

And how we look after ourselves during challenging times.

We don't need to save all of humanity before dinner.

We can start with something much simpler.

A conversation.

A gesture.

An opportunity not to cause harm.

A small act of generosity.

And perhaps over time, those actions will help create conditions that encourage new ways of relating.

To ourselves.

To the people we love.

And to those we don't yet know how to love.

## Learning to love

We've explored love as a possible orientation for our ethical training.

Not as an emotion we must feel constantly.

Or an obligation to accept any behavior.

But as a capacity we can cultivate through understanding, attention, and our actions.

Love can help us recognize people's worth.

Compassion can prompt us to respond to suffering.

Appreciative joy can allow us to celebrate the well-being of others.

And equanimity can help us relate to it all without being completely dominated by our preferences and fears.

From our model's perspective, these qualities also depend on conditions.

We can cultivate them.

Develop habits that support them.

And learn to recognize tendencies that make their appearance difficult.

We won't always manage it.

There will be moments of tiredness, frustration, and conflict.

Sometimes we'll act in ways that contradict our values.

And then we'll have another opportunity to practice.

Recognize.

Repair.

Learn.

And continue.

Because love isn't about never making mistakes.

It's also about what we do when we realize we've made them.

And perhaps we should remember something we sometimes forget.

We don't have to choose between loving ourselves and loving others.

We can learn to do both.

In fact, perhaps the better we can care for ourselves, the more available we are to care for those around us.

And perhaps the better we understand other people, the easier it becomes to understand ourselves.

I don't know whether learning to love really is our destiny on Earth.

But if I had to choose a direction for our existence, I'd struggle to imagine a more beautiful one.

You don't need to become a perfect person before you begin to love. And perhaps learning to love, starting with yourself, is one of the best ways to stop demanding perfection of yourself.

In the next chapter, we'll explore the second training: samatha, the development of calm and concentration.

Because responding with wisdom and love takes more than good intentions.

It also helps to have a mind capable of recognizing what is happening.

Especially when somebody cuts in front of us in the supermarket queue.

And by the way, if you're traveling by plane, remember to put your oxygen mask on first.
