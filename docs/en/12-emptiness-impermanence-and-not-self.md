# 12. Emptiness, Impermanence, and Not-Self

I have a problem.

We have spent several chapters trying to explain that everything depends on causes and conditions, that our intentions are conditioned processes, and that the self may not be quite what we imagine.

And now we are going to talk about emptiness.

As if we hadn't made things complicated enough already.

The funny thing is that when I first began studying these concepts, I thought they were three entirely different great discoveries.

Impermanence: everything changes.

Not-self: there is no independent, permanent self.

Emptiness: things lack inherent existence.

Three profound, mysterious ideas, sometimes explained in ways that seem designed to ensure nobody understands them on the first attempt.

But over time I began to wonder something.

What if they were all pointing to different aspects of the same phenomenon?

What if our model of conditioning could help us understand them better?

Let's try.

And if we finish the chapter still confused, at least we will be able to say we have explored our confusion in considerable depth.

## Impermanence: everything changes

Let's start with the simplest.

*Anicca*, impermanence.

Buddhist teaching holds that conditioned phenomena are impermanent.

This does not simply mean that they will disappear someday.

It means that, because they depend on causes and conditions, they are subject to processes of arising, transformation, and cessation.

Think of your body.

The body you have now is not identical to the one you had as a child.

It has grown, aged, learned, fallen ill, and changed in countless ways.

And it will continue changing.

You can try to keep it healthy, train it, and take care of it.

But you cannot guarantee that it will remain exactly as it is.

The same happens with our emotions.

We can feel extraordinary joy and discover that a few minutes later it has vanished.

We can experience deep sadness and, some time later, surprise ourselves by laughing at something silly.

Even our thoughts change constantly.

Sometimes so much that we can defend an opinion with complete conviction in the morning and discover by the afternoon that we are not so sure anymore.

That happens to me fairly often.

Which makes writing a philosophy book a rather risky undertaking.

But impermanence does not have to be bad news.

If unpleasant experiences were permanent, we would be in serious trouble.

Sadness would never end.

Fear would never diminish.

And that terrible haircut you had as a teenager would still be with you forever.

Fortunately, things change.

And precisely because they change, we can learn, grow, and transform how we live.

From our model's perspective, impermanence does not appear as some mysterious property of things.

We can understand it as a consequence of conditioned phenomena depending on relationships and circumstances that are themselves subject to change.

When conditions change, the processes that depend on them can change too.

This does not mean everything changes at the same speed.

An emotion may last seconds.

A habit may persist for decades.

A mountain may remain recognizable for millions of years.

But relative stability is not the same as absolute independence from change.

And that distinction will matter for the concepts that follow.

## Not-self: who is experiencing all of this?

Now we come to *anattā*, a Pali term usually translated as “not-self” or “non-self.”

And this is where many people start to get nervous.

Because when we say the self does not exist, it sounds as though we are denying something extraordinarily obvious.

I am here.

You are reading.

We have memories, names, responsibilities, and relationships.

And if you receive a traffic fine tomorrow, I suspect you will not get out of paying it by explaining that the self is an illusion.

The official probably won't be terribly impressed.

But perhaps the teaching of not-self is more interesting than simply claiming we don't exist.

In the Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta, one of the Buddha's early discourses, we find an analysis of five groups of processes traditionally called aggregates, or *khandhas*.

They are:

1. Form (*rūpa*): the body and material phenomena.
2. Feeling tone (*vedanā*): the pleasant, unpleasant, or neutral quality of experience.
3. Perception (*saññā*): the recognition and identification of what is experienced.
4. Formations (*saṅkhārā*): a broad range of mental activities and dispositions, including volitions.
5. Consciousness (*viññāṇa*): forms of consciousness related to different modes of experience.

The Buddha invites us to examine these aggregates and recognize that they are impermanent, subject to conditions, and cannot be regarded as a permanent self that is completely independent and under our control.

Let's investigate.

Can you decide not to age?

No.

Can you choose exactly which emotion will appear five minutes from now?

Probably not.

Can you prevent any unpleasant thought from arising?

If you have tried meditating for more than thirty seconds, I suspect you know the answer.

Even consciousness, which we may experience as something continuous, varies with sleep, attention, and numerous physiological and mental processes.

From our model's perspective, we can regard what we call “self” as a dynamic organization of conditioned processes.

Our body.

Our memories.

Our emotions.

Our intentions.

Our personal history.

And the experience of being someone who lives through all of that.

We do not need to deny that people exist to investigate whether there is, in addition, a controller absolutely independent of those processes.

Perhaps the problem arises when we turn a dynamic organization of experience into a fixed, separate, unchanging entity.

And then expect that entity to have absolute control over what happens.

Curiously, our own mental system may help construct that image.

## The self that wanted to be perfect

For a long time, I had rather demanding ideas about who I should be.

I wanted to do things well.

I wanted to understand them.

I wanted to avoid mistakes.

And naturally, I wanted to be a good person.

So far, it doesn't sound especially problematic.

But my ideal self had one little peculiarity.

It was never completely satisfied.

There was always something else to improve.

Something I hadn't achieved yet.

Some reaction I shouldn't have had.

And the harder I tried to approach that perfect image, the more opportunities I found to discover my shortcomings.

Over time, thanks in large part to meditation, I began to observe that process differently.

That image of myself also appeared in my experience.

It was a construction made of thoughts, memories, expectations, emotions, and habits.

I did not need to regard it as an independent entity with absolute authority over my life.

And something curious happened.

I began to feel affection for it.

That self had been trying to protect me for many years.

It had developed strategies to deal with difficult situations.

Some had worked.

Others, frankly, could have benefited from a much more rigorous quality review.

But I did not need to hate them.

I could understand them and, when necessary, learn to respond differently.

In a sense, I stopped trying to eliminate my self.

I began relating to it differently.

And that seems to me a fundamental distinction.

Recognizing that our identity is conditioned does not mean we have to destroy it.

It means we can learn not to cling to it as though it were fixed and independent.

Our personal story can continue to be useful.

We can still use our names, meet our responsibilities, and love the people in our lives.

But perhaps we do not need to defend every idea about ourselves as though our existence depended on it.

## Emptiness: things do not exist on their own

And now we come to *śūnyatā*, emptiness.

One of the central ideas of Mahāyāna Buddhism.

Although teachings about emptiness and the absence of a self already appear in early Buddhism, emptiness receives especially profound philosophical development in later traditions.

One of its great exponents was Nāgārjuna, associated with the Madhyamaka school.

His analysis questions whether phenomena possess inherent existence or a completely independent nature of their own, called *svabhāva*.

Something needs clarifying here.

Emptiness does not mean things do not exist. In this interpretation, it means that they do not exist independently, in and of themselves, apart from causes and relationships.

Let's use an example.

Imagine a cup of coffee.

What is it, exactly?

A cup.

Perfect.

But what conditions contributed to its existence?

Someone obtained the raw materials.

Other people made the cup.

The coffee beans were grown and processed.

The product was transported.

Someone heated the water.

And you decided to make yourself a drink.

The cup of coffee in front of you depends on an extraordinary number of circumstances.

But we can investigate further.

What makes us consider it a cup?

Its shape.

Its function.

Its relationship to other objects.

Our ability to recognize and use it.

If we smash it into pieces, at what point does it stop being a cup?

And what if we use a cup as a flowerpot?

Has it stopped being a cup and become something else?

These questions help us recognize that objects do not always have such obvious conceptual boundaries as we imagine.

There are relationships among their components, functions, causes, and the ways we identify them.

From our model's perspective, we can explore emptiness as the absence of an inherent identity independent of those relationships.

But we must acknowledge that this explanation is only an approximation.

Nāgārjuna's philosophy is far more rigorous and profound than saying that everything is made of parts or that things need causes.

His critique reaches the very idea that phenomena have an independent essence.

And that includes the explanations we use to understand them.

## Emptiness is empty too

Here comes one of my favorite ideas.

If emptiness means that things lack inherent existence, what about emptiness itself?

Is it an absolute reality that exists independently of everything else?

Madhyamaka philosophy offers a particularly interesting response: we should not turn emptiness into a substantial entity either.

Emptiness need not be some kind of invisible substance from which the universe is made.

Nor a cosmic space containing everything.

Nor a universal consciousness hidden behind our experiences.

We can understand it as a way to question the inherent existence we attribute to phenomena.

And that includes our tendency to turn philosophical explanations into new absolute realities.

Which is frankly amusing.

We spend years trying to understand emptiness.

Eventually we think we have understood it.

And then discover that we must also stop clinging to our understanding of emptiness.

Buddhism has a rather peculiar sense of humor.

But there is something profoundly useful about this perspective.

It reminds us that an explanation can help us stop clinging to certain ideas without itself becoming a new object of attachment.

And this connects directly with the limits of our model.

If our model of conditioning also arose depending on causes and conditions, it makes no sense to turn it into a definitive description of reality independent of those conditions.

It is a tool.

And tools are meant to be used.

Not to have altars built for them.

## Two ways of talking about reality

The Madhyamaka tradition develops a distinction known as the two truths.

Conventional truth and ultimate truth.

Conventional truth concerns how phenomena exist and function in everyday experience, including the relationships and designations we use to understand the world.

Ultimate truth concerns the absence of inherent existence in those phenomena.

This does not mean there are two different universes.

One false universe in which we usually live, and another true universe hidden behind it.

We can understand them as two perspectives on the same phenomena.

Let's return to our cup.

From the conventional perspective, it is a cup.

We can fill it, wash it, break it, or give it to someone.

In an analysis of emptiness, we investigate whether it exists as an independent entity with its own essence, unrelated to anything else.

We do not need to deny its everyday functioning in order to recognize its dependence.

Nor do we need to believe conventional explanations are useless.

In fact, it would be rather difficult to live without them.

Imagine trying to order coffee in a café by explaining that you would like a temporary configuration of interdependent phenomena conventionally designated as a beverage.

The waiter would probably ask you to leave.

The two perspectives can complement each other.

The conventional view allows us to navigate the world.

Ultimate analysis invites us to question what we assume to be independent, fixed, or inherent.

And perhaps that combination can help us relate more flexibly to our experiences.

## And what about nonduality?

Here I want to share something from my own experience.

During my years of meditation I have experienced states in which the usual sense of separation between the experiencer and what is experienced changed profoundly.

Sometimes that distinction seemed to disappear.

These are experiences that certain contemplative traditions associate with nonduality.

For a long time I attached particular importance to them.

I felt I was discovering something profound about the nature of reality.

Perhaps a form of consciousness that transcended our usual experience of being separate individuals.

But over time I began asking myself a different question.

What if these experiences were also conditioned?

What if the feeling of being a separate observer and the experience of that separation disappearing were different configurations of experience?

That would not necessarily invalidate either.

But neither would their intensity or extraordinary nature, by itself, justify concluding that one reveals a final metaphysical reality.

I admit that possibility was a little unsettling.

For a while I felt as though some of the magic had disappeared.

But then I discovered something much more interesting.

I did not need to prove that those experiences revealed a universal consciousness.

Nor did I need to deny their value.

I could recognize that they had happened, that they had transformed how I understood myself, and that I could still learn from them.

And at the same time, I could keep my interpretations open.

Here it is worth distinguishing three concepts.

Not-self questions identification with a permanent, independent self.

Emptiness, especially in Madhyamaka, questions the inherent existence of phenomena.

Nonduality can refer, depending on the tradition, to experiences or understandings in which certain distinctions are no longer organized in the usual way.

These ideas are related in some teachings, but they are not synonyms.

Nor should we assume that a change in the sense of subject and object automatically amounts to realizing emptiness or attaining liberation.

The experience may be extraordinary.

But our explanations still need to be examined.

## So nothing matters?

At this point, someone might think:

“If everything changes, there is no independent self, and things are empty of inherent existence, what's the point of doing anything?”

A reasonable question.

But I think it starts from a misunderstanding.

The fact that things depend on conditions does not mean they have no value.

Quite the opposite.

Precisely because our actions have consequences, what we do matters.

If someone suffers, their suffering does not stop mattering because their experiences are conditioned.

If we love someone, that love does not cease to be valuable because it depends on our history, our body, and our relationships.

And if we make a mistake, the absence of an independent self does not remove our responsibility to try to repair it.

We can recognize a person as an extraordinarily complex collection of interdependent processes and love them deeply at the same time.

We do not need to find an eternal essence in someone to recognize their worth.

Indeed, perhaps understanding their vulnerability and their dependence on so many conditions can help us treat them with greater kindness.

This is where emptiness can meet compassion.

Not because emptiness automatically proves that we should love everyone.

But because questioning rigid ideas about ourselves and others can make room for a different kind of relationship.

A person who behaves unpleasantly does not have to be, essentially, a bad person. (Replace “person” with “four-year-old child” and notice how your interpretation changes.)

Their behavior has causes.

Their habits can change.

And our responses can also help transform the conditions of the relationship.

That does not mean tolerating harm.

We can set boundaries, protect ourselves, and demand accountability.

But perhaps we can do so without turning others into characters forever defined by their worst actions.

And perhaps we can offer ourselves the same opportunity.

## Three faces of conditioning

Let's return to our model.

We have explored three fundamental concepts.

Impermanence reminds us that conditioned phenomena change.

Not-self invites us to investigate whether what we consider our identity can be understood as a collection of processes without a permanent, independent controller.

Emptiness questions whether phenomena have inherent existence apart from their relationships and conditions.

They are not identical concepts.

Nor have they developed in exactly the same way across all Buddhist traditions.

But we can investigate a connection among them.

Perhaps impermanence, not-self, and emptiness are three complementary perspectives on the dependence of phenomena.

Impermanence highlights their relationship with change.

Not-self questions certain forms of identification and appropriation.

Emptiness examines the absence of inherent existence.

From our model's perspective, these perspectives can help us recognize that many things we consider fixed, independent, and completely ours are more dynamic and dependent than we imagine.

And that understanding can transform how we react.

When we recognize impermanence, perhaps it becomes easier to accept certain changes.

When we understand not-self, perhaps we need to defend our self-image less.

When we investigate emptiness, perhaps we stop attributing unchanging identities to people and things.

But we must not turn these possibilities into guaranteed results.

Understanding an idea intellectually does not mean we have transformed our habits.

And, as we will see when we study *vipassanā*, direct observation and training can play an important role in that transformation.

## A small experiment

Let's investigate something very simple.

Choose an everyday object.

It could be a cup, a pen, a photograph, or anything close to you.

Observe it for a few moments.

First, think about its history.

What conditions made its existence possible?

Where did its materials come from?

Who made it?

How did it reach you?

What else does it depend on to perform its function?

Next, investigate its impermanence.

Has it changed since it was made?

Could it break?

Will it be exactly the same in a hundred years?

Finally, notice how you identify it.

What makes it that object?

Its shape?

Its function?

Its history?

Its relationship with you?

You do not need to reach any philosophical conclusion.

Simply notice how something that seemed like a perfectly defined entity begins to reveal itself as part of a network of relationships.

Now you can do the same exercise with an idea about yourself.

“I'm impatient.”

“I'm intelligent.”

“I'm an insecure person.”

“I've always been this way.”

Investigate what conditions may have contributed to constructing that description.

Notice whether it has been true in every circumstance.

And ask yourself whether it necessarily has to remain true in the future.

Perhaps you will discover that some of the ideas you use to define yourself are more flexible than you imagined.

You do not need to reject them.

You can simply stop treating them as absolute truths.

## We don't need to destroy the world to understand it

We have taken a curious journey.

We started by investigating a cup.

We ended by questioning the inherent existence of phenomena.

And along the way, we cast doubt on some of our favorite ideas about who we are.

But there is something I do not want us to lose sight of.

We do not need to deny the world to learn to relate to it better.

People still exist in our everyday experience.

Pain is still pain.

Love is still love.

And actions still have consequences.

Impermanence does not make beauty worthless.

Not-self does not erase people's dignity.

And emptiness does not turn reality into an illusion that does not matter.

These teachings can invite us to relate differently to what we experience.

With less need to cling.

With less resistance to inevitable changes.

And perhaps with greater understanding and compassion for ourselves and others.

But we must remember something else.

All the explanations we have explored are interpretations we are using to understand our experience.

Including this very explanation.

We do not need to turn any of them into an unquestionable truth.

We can keep investigating.

And perhaps we will discover that the less we need to defend our ideas about reality, the more available we are to learn from it.

Things do not have to be permanent for us to love them. There does not have to be an independent self for us to care for ourselves. And the world does not need a fixed essence for our lives to have meaning.

Perhaps it is precisely its changing, interdependent nature that makes every encounter, every experience, and every opportunity to act with love so valuable.
