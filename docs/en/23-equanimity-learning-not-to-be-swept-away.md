# 23. Equanimity: Learning Not to Be Swept Away

Imagine you're enjoying a wonderful afternoon.

The weather is beautiful. You have no urgent obligations. You're sitting comfortably, having something you like. And for a few minutes, everything seems to be in its place.

Then a thought appears.

"What a shame tomorrow is Monday."

Congratulations.

You've just managed to let a Monday that hasn't even arrived spoil part of your Sunday.

Our minds have an extraordinary talent for these things.

We can be enjoying something pleasant and start suffering because we know it will end. We can face an unpleasant situation and add vast amounts of distress by trying to convince ourselves that it shouldn't be happening.

And we can spend much of our lives imagining how things ought to be instead of relating to what's actually happening.

Here we encounter one of the capacities that interests me most in Buddhist training.

Equanimity. Or *upekkhā*.

For a long time, I misunderstood that word rather badly.

I thought it meant staying calm. Never getting angry. Never feeling sad. Never reacting. A kind of permanent serenity.

But perhaps equanimity is considerably more interesting than that.

What if we could learn to experience life without constantly having to fight against whatever arises?

Let's investigate.

## What is equanimity?

*Upekkhā* is usually translated as equanimity. In Buddhist teachings, it appears in different contexts.

It's one of the four *brahmavihāras*, alongside goodwill, compassion, and appreciative joy. It's also one of the seven factors of awakening and plays a prominent role in descriptions of deep concentration states.

We shouldn't assume all these uses are exactly identical. But we can recognise a common orientation.

The ability to remain balanced in the face of pleasant and unpleasant experiences without being completely dominated by attraction and rejection.

That doesn't mean every experience feels the same. Pain can still be unpleasant. Good news can still bring joy. And losing someone we love can cause profound sadness.

Equanimity doesn't need to erase those differences. It can change our relationship with them.

Imagine an unpleasant emotion arises. Normally, perhaps you try to get rid of it immediately, construct a story to justify it, or distract yourself to avoid feeling it.

But there's another possibility: recognise that it's present. Allow it to be there for a while, when it's safe to do so. And respond to the situation without first needing the emotion to disappear.

We're not talking about resignation. We're investigating a different way of relating to experience.

## Pain and the second arrow

There's a Buddhist teaching I find particularly helpful in understanding equanimity.

We find it in the *Sallatha Sutta* (SN 36.6), known as the discourse on the arrow.

The Buddha uses the image of a person struck by two arrows. The first represents a painful experience. The second represents the additional suffering that certain mental responses can create.

Imagine stubbing your little toe against a table.

The first arrow arrives immediately.

Pain. A lot of pain. And perhaps a verbal expression that wouldn't be especially appropriate at a religious ceremony.

But then something else may appear.

"Who put this damned table here?" "This always happens to me." "I'm hopeless." "Now it'll hurt all day."

You may end up angry with the entire family because someone moved a piece of furniture two inches.

The first arrow was the pain. The subsequent reactions may add new forms of suffering.

Of course, this image is a simplification. Not all psychological suffering can be neatly separated from the initial pain, and we shouldn't use it to blame people for painful experiences they didn't choose.

But it lets us examine an important distinction.

Experiencing pain is one thing. The relationship we develop with that pain is another.

Perhaps we have some capacity to change that relationship. Not in every case, necessarily. And not immediately. But we can learn.

## Equanimity is not indifference

This is one of the most important misunderstandings to avoid.

Imagine someone you love is suffering.

You could respond in two ways.

The first: "Everything is conditioned. I mustn't become attached. Their suffering doesn't affect me."

The second: "I can see they're suffering. I care deeply. I want to help, and I need enough clarity to respond appropriately."

Which sounds closer to mature compassion?

To me, certainly the second.

The first may simply be indifference dressed up as wisdom. We need to be careful with that.

Equanimity doesn't mean things stop mattering to us. Nor does it mean remaining passive in the face of injustice or suffering.

We can be equanimous and act firmly. Protect our children. Set boundaries. Face difficult situations. Recognise harmful behaviour.

The difference is that perhaps we don't have to become completely trapped in hatred, fear, or despair in order to act.

Equanimity isn't about loving less. It can help us love without losing our ability to respond.

This connects with our chapter on love. Perhaps one of the deepest ways of caring for someone is to remain sufficiently available when they need us most.

## Acceptance doesn't mean approval

Here's another common confusion.

If we accept our experience, does that mean approving everything that happens?

No.

Imagine it's raining. You can get angry, insult the clouds, or spend half an hour explaining that the universe should have organised its weather conditions better.

But you'll probably fail to stop the rain.

Or you can recognise that it's raining—and pick up an umbrella.

Accepting the fact that it's raining doesn't mean you like it. Nor that you have to stand there getting soaked. You've simply stopped fighting a fact that's already happening, and now you can act appropriately.

That seems obvious when we're talking about the weather. It's not always so simple with emotions.

Imagine you're angry. You may start thinking:

"I shouldn't be angry." "After all these years of meditation, this shouldn't still happen." "A wise person wouldn't react like this."

Now you're angry. And you're angry about being angry.

A magnificent two-for-one offer on suffering.

But what if you simply recognise that anger is present? You don't need to approve of it, act on every impulse, or turn it into an identity.

You can acknowledge its presence and decide how to relate to it.

I find this distinction fundamental.

Accepting that an experience is happening doesn't mean giving up on changing the conditions that produced it or dealing with its consequences.

Sometimes acceptance is precisely what allows us to act more clearly.

## The struggle that feeds suffering

Let's return to our model of the conditioned mind.

Imagine an unpleasant sensation appears. It may favour rejection.

Then a thought arises: "This shouldn't be happening."

That thought can generate further unpleasant sensations, which may reinforce the rejection even more.

We've created a self-sustaining dynamic. In our model, we can see it as a feedback process.

Now imagine a different response.

An unpleasant sensation appears. You recognise it. Perhaps aversion appears too—and you recognise that as well.

You don't need to eliminate either one immediately. You can observe them. Perhaps you discover that you don't have to add further thoughts that reinforce the struggle.

The original experience may continue. But some of the processes that normally accompany it may stop being fed in the same way.

This is especially interesting in relation to dependent origination.

In Buddhist teachings, *vedanā*—the pleasant or unpleasant feeling tone of experience—can condition *taṇhā*, craving. But practice allows for the possibility of feeling a sensation without necessarily continuing to feed that response.

From our model's perspective, equanimity might help change some of these relationships.

Not because sensations cease to exist, but because our responses to them may change. And perhaps certain forms of suffering will lose some of their fuel.

## Can we feel anger with equanimity?

I find this question especially interesting.

Imagine someone treats you unfairly. Anger appears. Your body becomes activated. Defensive thoughts arise.

But you recognise what's happening. You don't have to identify completely with the emotion or act on every impulse. You can maintain some clarity.

Does that mean you're experiencing equanimity?

Perhaps, at least to some degree.

But we must distinguish between recognising an emotion and having completely extinguished its roots.

Buddhist tradition describes final liberation in much more demanding terms than simply being able to observe anger without acting on it.

So we shouldn't conclude that someone fully liberated necessarily continues to experience hatred in the ordinary sense but has learned to control it. Our model doesn't demonstrate that.

What we can investigate is how different degrees of equanimity influence our everyday responses.

Perhaps some emotions lose intensity, certain interpretations stop seeming quite so convincing, or we can respond more clearly even when discomfort arises.

And something else matters: equanimity shouldn't be used to hide emotions we need to understand.

We can appear calm while continuing to feed resentment. Avoid a difficult conversation and tell ourselves we're being equanimous. Or emotionally disconnect from a situation that feels too painful.

Not every lack of visible reaction is equanimity. Sometimes it may be avoidance, suppression, or disconnection.

The distinction isn't always easy to recognise. That's why we need honesty—and external support when necessary.

## Balance doesn't mean standing still

For a long time, I imagined equanimity as a kind of motionless state: a perfectly calm, unchanging mind, like the surface of a lake when there's no wind.

Lately, another image seems more useful to me.

A person walking.

When we walk, our bodies maintain balance through continuous adjustments. We don't remain perfectly motionless. We move, respond to uneven ground, correct small imbalances, and keep going.

Perhaps equanimity can be understood in a similar way.

Not as the absolute absence of change, but as a dynamic ability to maintain or recover some stability.

An unpleasant experience can affect us. An intense emotion may arise. We may even temporarily lose our balance. But we can develop capacities that help us recognise what happened and regain a more appropriate response.

From our model's perspective, that's especially interesting. We don't need to imagine a mind that never changes. We can examine how certain tendencies favour greater stability under changing conditions.

Perhaps a trained mind isn't one that always stays the same, but one that can adapt without getting continually trapped in harmful patterns.

Of course, this is a functional interpretation, not a complete definition of equanimity across all Buddhist traditions.

## The problem of wanting to control the universe

There's something I've gradually recognised in my own life.

For a long time, I've tried to control a great many things.

My future. My emotions. My relationships. My work. What other people thought. And, naturally, whatever my children decided to do five seconds after I asked them to do something.

An extraordinarily ambitious undertaking. With a success rate that leaves considerable room for improvement.

But perhaps the problem wasn't simply that I needed better control techniques. Maybe I also needed to examine my relationship with uncertainty.

Because a huge part of our experience depends on conditions we can't determine completely.

We can influence. Learn. Act. But we can't guarantee every outcome. And perhaps trying to do so is a major source of suffering.

Equanimity offers another possibility.

We can develop intentions, make decisions, work towards goals, and recognise at the same time that results don't depend on us alone.

That's not resignation. It's a way of relating to the limits of our influence—and perhaps of stopping our demands that the world provide something it never promised.

## Equanimity needs love

I want to return to our three trainings, because there's a danger I consider particularly important.

We can learn to observe unpleasant experiences without reacting too strongly. Develop calm and perhaps some distance from our thoughts and emotions.

But what happens if we use these capacities to stop engaging with life? To avoid difficult relationships, ignore other people's suffering, or convince ourselves nothing matters very much?

Perhaps we're developing a form of disconnection. Not necessarily wisdom.

That's why I believe equanimity should be accompanied by love.

Love helps us remember that well-being matters. Concentration supports our ability to remain present. Investigation helps us understand how responses arise. And equanimity can allow us to relate to those experiences without being dominated by attraction and rejection.

They're not independent capacities. They support one another.

In fact, perhaps an equanimity that makes us unable to care for others deserves particularly careful investigation.

Our goal isn't to suffer less by caring less. It's to learn to love in a way that generates less unnecessary suffering—for ourselves and those around us.

## We can also be equanimous with pleasure

So far, we've talked mostly about unpleasant experiences. But equanimity matters when pleasure arises, too.

Imagine enjoying something wonderful: a conversation, a song, an afternoon with your children, or a particularly pleasant meditative state.

Perhaps a thought appears: "I wish this would never end."

Understandable. But notice what happens.

The experience is still here, yet perhaps you've already begun worrying about losing it. Wanting to preserve it may introduce restlessness, and that restlessness can change the pleasant experience itself.

In our model, we find an interesting dynamic. Pleasure isn't necessarily the problem. The attachment we develop towards it may be.

Here, my experiences with *pīti* taught me something important.

I could enjoy extraordinary well-being, and also recognise that it depended on conditions—and that those conditions would change.

I didn't need to turn every pleasant experience into something I had to reproduce forever. I could enjoy it, appreciate it, and allow it to end.

Not always easy. But perhaps it's a capacity we can train.

Equanimity lets us enjoy things without needing to turn pleasure into a possession. That can deeply transform our relationship with happiness.

## A small experiment: allowing an experience

Let's try something simple.

Choose a mildly unpleasant everyday experience. Not a traumatic situation, intense pain, or anything requiring urgent intervention.

It could be slight impatience, a moment of boredom, or minor discomfort.

When it appears, try to observe it.

What sensations are present? Is there tension, restlessness, an unpleasant feeling tone?

Now notice whether a reaction arises. Perhaps you want it to end. Perhaps you begin thinking you shouldn't feel this way, or construct a story about how unfair the situation is.

You don't need to fight those thoughts. Just recognise them.

Then try something different. For a few seconds, allow the experience to be present without immediately trying to change it.

Not because you must endure it, but because we're investigating what happens when we stop adding an automatic struggle.

Perhaps the sensation changes. Perhaps it remains. Or perhaps you discover that you need to act. Any of these possibilities can offer information.

Then ask yourself: Can I respond to this situation without first needing every trace of discomfort to disappear?

Sometimes the answer will be yes. At other times you'll need to rest, change activities, or ask for help.

We're not trying to develop unlimited tolerance for suffering. We're exploring our relationship with it—and remembering that caring for ourselves is also part of the path.

## Equanimity towards our own mistakes

There's one application of equanimity I consider especially important: how we relate to our mistakes.

Imagine you've reacted badly during an argument. You recognise that you've hurt someone. And an unpleasant feeling arises—perhaps guilt, shame, sadness, or frustration.

Now you can react against those emotions.

"I shouldn't have done that." "I'm a bad person." "I'll never change."

Perhaps you become so caught up in suffering over your self-image that you can hardly attend to repairing what happened.

But there's another possibility.

Recognise the mistake. Recognise the discomfort. And act.

Apologise when appropriate. Listen. Repair. And change the conditions that contributed to the behaviour.

Here, equanimity doesn't eliminate responsibility. It may help us assume it without being paralysed by self-judgement.

This connects to something we've said repeatedly: we don't need to hate ourselves in order to learn.

We can be demanding about our actions and, at the same time, compassionate towards our own humanity.

Perhaps that combination is far more helpful than trying to punish ourselves into perfection.

## Equanimity isn't a trophy, either

There's one last trap.

We begin to develop some equanimity. We notice that certain things affect us less, that we react more calmly. And perhaps we start constructing a new identity.

"I'm an equanimous person." "These things don't affect me anymore." "I've overcome certain emotions."

Wonderful.

Until something unexpected happens one day. And we react.

Then a new source of suffering appears. How could such an equanimous person possibly become angry?

Perhaps because equanimity itself can become an object of attachment: an image we feel compelled to defend.

Our model offers us a useful question again.

Are we learning to relate to experience more skilfully, or constructing a new story about who we are?

We don't have to turn every capacity we've developed into a permanent identity. We can recognise that we've learned, appreciate the changes, and continue investigating.

Without needing to guarantee that we'll never react again.

Perhaps an important part of equanimity is precisely not needing to be equanimous all the time. And kindly recognising when we discover that we still have something to learn.

## Learning not to fight

We've explored equanimity as a capacity that can transform our relationship with pleasant and unpleasant experiences.

It doesn't mean indifference, require resignation, or demand the elimination of every emotion.

It can help us recognise what is happening without automatically adding new patterns of attachment and aversion.

In our model, we can regard it as a tendency that changes the responses arising to certain experiences.

The unpleasant sensation may remain. The emotion may be present. But perhaps we don't need to feed every interpretation and reaction that normally accompanies it.

The same is true of pleasant experiences. We can enjoy them, appreciate them, and allow them to change—without needing to possess them.

But I want to end with an important distinction.

We're not trying to learn to put up with absolutely anything.

Some situations require action: protecting ourselves, setting boundaries, asking for help, or changing the conditions that generate suffering.

Equanimity doesn't replace those actions. It can help us carry them out more clearly.

Perhaps the real question isn't how to ensure that nothing unpleasant ever happens, but how to relate to unpleasant experiences without turning each one into a war.

A war against the world, other people, or ourselves.

Because perhaps a significant part of our suffering comes from precisely those struggles, and perhaps we can learn to stop feeding them.

Not always. Not perfectly. But with growing understanding.

Equanimity isn't about life ceasing to affect us. It's about learning to live without needing to fight it constantly.

And perhaps, when we stop spending so much energy on that fight, we'll discover that we have much more left for something I consider considerably more interesting.

Loving. Learning. And acting.

In the next chapter we'll explore some of the obstacles that can arise during practice.

Because, naturally, learning to relate to experience doesn't mean our minds will stop making things difficult for us.

And I suspect they still have a few surprises in store.
