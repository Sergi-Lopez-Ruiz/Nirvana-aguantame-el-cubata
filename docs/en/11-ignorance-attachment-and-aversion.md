# 11. Ignorance, Attachment, and Aversion

There is something I have always found curious.

Human beings are capable of learning extraordinarily complex things.

We can build airplanes, understand how stars work, and develop machines capable of discussing philosophy.

And yet we can spend twenty years getting angry because someone leaves the toilet seat up.

Something doesn't quite add up.

How can we be so intelligent and, at the same time, keep repeating behaviors we know perfectly well make us suffer?

Perhaps the problem is that we are confusing two things.

Knowing something.

And having learned to live according to it.

This distinction brings us to three fundamental Buddhist concepts: ignorance, attachment, and aversion.

And also to one of our model’s central hypotheses.

Perhaps ignorance is not merely something we do not know. Perhaps it is also a conditioned way of experiencing and responding that helps perpetuate itself.

Let's investigate.

## The three unwholesome roots

In Buddhism we find three mental roots considered unwholesome, known as *akusala-mūla*.

They are *lobha*, *dosa*, and *moha*.

They are commonly translated as greed, aversion, and ignorance or delusion.

In everyday language, we can relate them to three tendencies:

**Attachment:** We want to obtain, keep, or take possession of certain experiences.

**Aversion:** We want to avoid, reject, or eliminate what we find unpleasant.

**Ignorance:** We do not adequately understand the nature of our experience or the conditions that generate suffering.

A clarification is in order. *Lobha* more precisely means greed or grasping; *moha* is usually translated as delusion or confusion, and is closely related to *avijjā*, the fundamental ignorance found in dependent origination.

The terms are not completely interchangeable, but they are deeply connected.

And why are these considered unwholesome roots?

Because they can give rise to actions and intentions that cause suffering to ourselves and others.

This does not mean that every time we want something, we are doing something wrong.

Nor that experiencing an unpleasant emotion is a moral failure.

What interests us is investigating what happens when our relationship with experience becomes dominated by the need to possess some experiences and reject others.

And how certain interpretations can sustain that dynamic.

## Attachment: when wanting is no longer enough

Imagine you have a new car.

You love it.

You enjoy driving it, you like its design, and you are delighted with your purchase.

So far, wonderful.

But one day you notice a small scratch on the door.

It is barely visible.

The car works exactly as before.

It is still comfortable, safe, and perfectly capable of taking you wherever you want to go.

And yet now you have a problem.

You cannot stop looking at the scratch.

Every time you approach the car, your eyes find it.

Perhaps you start wondering who did it.

Perhaps you get angry.

Perhaps you remember how beautiful it looked before.

The car has hardly changed.

But your experience of owning it has changed enormously.

What happened?

We can try to understand it through our model.

First, a pleasant experience appears.

Then we develop a preference: we want it to continue.

That preference can become an expectation.

And the expectation can become a need.

“My car must stay perfect.”

When reality no longer meets that condition, distress arises.

The problem is not necessarily enjoying the car.

Nor wanting to look after it.

The problem arises when our satisfaction begins to depend rigidly on certain conditions remaining just as we want them.

And the more tightly we cling to them, the more vulnerable we may become to change.

This does not mean we must stop loving people, caring for our possessions, or pursuing important goals.

It means we can investigate the difference between enjoying something and needing it to be a particular way in order to feel okay.

And that difference can profoundly change our relationship with the world.

## Aversion: the war against what is already happening

Attachment tries to keep or acquire certain experiences.

Aversion tries to push others away.

Imagine you have a headache.

It is unpleasant.

You would rather not have it.

Nothing surprising so far.

But perhaps another thought appears.

“I shouldn't have a headache.”

Then another.

“Of all days, today, when I have so much to do.”

And then:

“This is going to ruin my day.”

The pain is still there.

But now you also have anger, frustration, and worry.

You are not merely experiencing pain.

You are fighting the fact that you have it.

In Buddhism we find an image we have already mentioned: the two arrows.

The first represents pain.

The second, the suffering our reactions can add.

What is especially interesting is that the second arrow does not always require a conscious decision.

Aversion can arise automatically.

It may be a tendency learned over many years.

We might not even realize we are rejecting our experience.

We simply feel distress and, almost immediately, begin to fight against it.

From our model’s perspective, we can understand this reaction as a conditioned process.

An unpleasant experience encourages rejection.

Rejection generates new unpleasant sensations and thoughts.

Those experiences feed still more aversion.

And the system can enter a feedback loop.

But here an important possibility appears.

We can learn to experience something unpleasant without necessarily adding a fight against it.

That does not mean passively accepting harmful situations.

If you have a headache, it may be sensible to rest, drink water, or seek medical care when appropriate.

The absence of aversion does not require us to give up acting.

We can try to resolve an unpleasant situation without needing to hate the fact that it is happening.

And that difference is worth exploring.

## Ignorance: more than not knowing

Now we come to the concept I find most interesting of the three.

Ignorance.

In Buddhism, *avijjā* does not simply mean a lack of knowledge.

It refers to failing to understand fundamental aspects of experience, especially suffering, its causes, its cessation, and the path leading to that cessation.

But there is something curious.

Often we know certain behaviors hurt us.

We know obsessing over an argument is not helpful.

We know constantly punishing ourselves for our mistakes can increase our distress.

We know responding aggressively can make a situation worse.

And yet we keep doing these things.

If ignorance were simply a lack of information, reading a good book would be enough.

Perhaps this one, if we are lucky.

But we all know that is not how things work.

We can understand an explanation intellectually and continue reacting in exactly the same way.

That is why I want to propose an interpretation of ignorance through our model.

What if ignorance were also a configuration of conditioned processes that encourages and perpetuates certain ways of perceiving, interpreting, and reacting?

Imagine a person who has learned to interpret any criticism as a personal attack.

They do not have to think consciously:

“I am going to interpret this as a threat so I can get angry.”

They simply hear the criticism, and a reaction appears.

Perhaps a memory is activated.

Perhaps bodily tension arises.

Perhaps a story about the injustice they are suffering immediately springs to mind.

And that story encourages a defensive response.

The person may know intellectually that not every criticism is an attack.

But their system keeps responding as though it were.

From this perspective, ignorance would not just be a failure to know a truth.

It could also manifest as a collection of tendencies that organize our experience in ways that feed suffering.

And this lets us explore another question.

How does this configuration sustain itself?

## A self-feeding system

Let's return to our person who interprets criticism as a threat.

They receive criticism.

They feel attacked.

They respond aggressively.

The other person gets angry.

And that response seems to confirm their original interpretation.

“See? I knew they were against me.”

The experience ends up reinforcing the belief that helped produce it.

In systems theory, we can describe this dynamic as a positive feedback process.

That does not mean it is pleasant.

It means the consequences of a process help reinforce that process.

And here we find a fascinating possibility.

Some conditioning can help create precisely the circumstances that favor its own continuation.

A distrustful person may behave in ways that make it harder for others to trust them.

Someone who constantly fears rejection may develop defensive responses that complicate their relationships.

And someone who identifies strongly with being incapable may avoid experiences that would allow them to discover new abilities.

This does not always happen.

People are far too complex to reduce their behavior to a single dynamic.

But these examples help us understand how certain interpretations can sustain themselves through their own consequences.

Now imagine something even more interesting.

What if we are not talking about a particular belief?

What if there are general tendencies that lead us to appropriate pleasant experiences, reject unpleasant ones, and identify with the stories we build around them?

We might be dealing with much deeper processes.

Processes that are not limited to a particular episode of anger, worry, or habit.

Rather, they take part in our habitual way of experiencing the world.

And perhaps that perspective can help us understand why ignorance occupies such an important place in Buddhist teachings.

## The self can be part of the problem, too

For a long time, I had a rather complicated relationship with my self-image.

Like many people, I had constructed a story about who I was, who I should be, and what I needed to achieve to feel satisfied with myself.

And of course, my ideal self had rather demanding expectations.

It wanted to do everything right.

Understand everything.

Never make a mistake.

Be a good person.

And, if possible, accomplish all that by Friday.

Over time, especially through meditation, I began to observe this process differently.

My self-image was not a fixed, independent entity.

It was also something that appeared in my experience.

Thoughts.

Memories.

Expectations.

Judgments.

Sensations.

And a story that was constantly being constructed and revised.

At first, recognizing this can be a little disconcerting.

If what I call “myself” is also conditioned, what does being me really mean?

But over time, something happened that I did not expect.

I began to relate to that self-image much more kindly.

I no longer needed to fight it constantly.

Nor eliminate it.

Nor prove it wrong.

I could recognize it as part of my experience, understand some of the conditions that had created it, and even appreciate the role it had played for years.

Because that self had tried to protect me.

It had tried to help me live.

Sometimes in rather clumsy ways, admittedly.

But it had also helped bring me this far.

And I discovered that I could feel affection for it.

I did not need to destroy my self.

I could learn to stop placing it at the center of everything.

This does not mean I had stopped experiencing a personal identity.

Nor does it prove any metaphysical claim about the existence or nonexistence of the self.

It simply changed my relationship with certain processes that I had once regarded as an unquestionable part of who I was.

And that transformation had profound consequences.

Perhaps we do not need to hate our conditioning in order to learn to transform it.

Perhaps we can understand it.

And sometimes even thank it for what it was trying to do for us.

## Wisdom does not have to be the opposite of ignorance

Here we come to one of the hypotheses that interests me most.

We tend to imagine ignorance and wisdom as two opposing extremes.

Where there is ignorance, there is no wisdom.

Where there is wisdom, ignorance disappears.

And certainly, Buddhist teachings describe a fundamental relationship between overcoming ignorance and developing understanding.

But our model allows us to explore a different perspective.

What if, rather than imagining them as two opposing entities, we looked at how they functionally manifest in the system?

Ignorance might be expressed through conditioned tendencies that perpetuate certain forms of suffering.

Wisdom might manifest through abilities and tendencies that allow us to recognize those processes and transform our relationship with them.

We would not be talking about two substances.

Nor necessarily about two completely independent systems.

We would be looking at different dynamics within a set of interdependent processes.

Imagine someone criticizes you.

A defensive response learned over many years may appear.

But another understanding can arise as well.

“Perhaps this person is trying to help me.”

“I don't need to prove I'm right.”

“I can listen before responding.”

Both tendencies can influence what happens.

And through practice we can develop conditions that favor clearer and more compassionate responses.

At first, this may require considerable effort.

But over time, some of these responses can become more natural.

Perhaps until certain conflicts that once seemed inevitable stop arising.

Wisdom would not merely mean knowing the correct explanation, but having profoundly transformed how we relate to experience.

This is not a complete definition of Buddhist wisdom.

It is a functional proposal we will attempt to explore through our model.

And there is something I find especially important.

We do not need to assume that ignorance has an independent existence that we must destroy.

We can investigate what conditions sustain it and what happens when those conditions cease to operate.

## Do we need to eliminate all conditioning?

At this point, we might conclude that our goal is to eliminate every habit, tendency, and conditioned response.

A sort of thorough spring-cleaning of the mind.

Out with attachment.

Out with aversion.

Out with ignorance.

And while we're at it, we could erase that song that has been playing in our heads for three days.

But perhaps we are framing the problem incorrectly.

The mind uses habits constantly.

Thanks to them, we can walk, speak, drive, recognize people, and carry out countless activities without having to learn everything from scratch each morning.

Not all conditioning is harmful.

In fact, much of the Buddhist path consists precisely in developing wholesome conditions.

Mindfulness.

Concentration.

Generosity.

Compassion.

Equanimity.

Wisdom.

In our model, these capacities also depend on causes and conditions.

And they can be strengthened through practice.

So the goal does not seem to be eliminating every form of conditioning.

What interests us is understanding which processes contribute to suffering and which favor its cessation.

Here we must maintain a distinction.

The Buddhist ideal of liberation goes beyond simply acquiring good habits.

But to understand the path toward it, it is useful to recognize that the training itself uses conditioned processes.

We use conditions to transform conditions.

And perhaps, at some point, certain dynamics no longer require our intervention because the conditions that allowed them to arise are no longer present.

## Love as a form of wisdom

There is something I want to emphasize before we continue.

When we speak of wisdom, we usually think of understanding things.

Having knowledge.

Being able to formulate profound explanations of reality.

But imagine two people.

The first knows every Buddhist theory, can recite the twelve links of dependent origination, and can spend hours debating the nature of the self.

Yet they treat others with contempt.

The second may never have read a philosophy book.

But they know how to listen.

They recognize the suffering of others.

They do not need to humiliate anyone to feel better about themselves.

And they respond with kindness even when things get complicated.

Who has developed an understanding that is more valuable for living?

I am not trying to turn this into a competition.

Nor am I claiming that love and wisdom mean exactly the same thing in every Buddhist teaching.

But from our perspective we can explore a deep connection between them.

When we better understand the conditions influencing our behavior and that of others, it may become easier to stop reducing people to their worst actions.

We can recognize that people suffer.

That their responses depend on histories we barely know.

That some of their behaviors may be harmful without our having to hate them.

And that we ourselves are subject to similar processes.

That understanding can foster compassion.

But we can also investigate the relationship in the other direction.

Perhaps learning to relate with love makes deeper understanding possible.

When we stop constantly defending our self-image, we can listen better.

When we do not need to reject someone who thinks differently, we may discover things we had not seen before.

And when we learn to treat ourselves with kindness, it may become easier to recognize our own mistakes without having to justify them.

From this perspective, love and wisdom can reinforce each other.

Not as two completely separate goals.

But as abilities that help transform how we relate to the world.

And perhaps love, when born of deep understanding, can also be seen as an expression of wisdom.

## We don't need to win a war against our mind

A temptation often appears when we begin practicing meditation.

We discover a reaction that causes us suffering.

And decide to eliminate it.

We notice attachment and want to stop having attachment.

We recognize aversion and begin to reject our aversion.

We discover ignorance and become angry about still being ignorant.

It is a fascinating strategy.

We try to eliminate suffering by using precisely some of the dynamics that help produce it.

And then we are surprised that it does not work particularly well.

Perhaps we can do it differently.

We can observe a reaction without immediately turning it into an enemy.

We can recognize that certain tendencies developed from causes we do not always understand.

And learn to modify the conditions that sustain them.

This does not mean allowing just any behavior.

If a reaction could harm another person, we need to act to prevent it.

Understanding and compassion do not replace boundaries or responsibility.

But they can change how we work with those tendencies.

We do not need to hate anger to learn not to act aggressively.

We do not need to reject fear to learn to relate to it better.

And we do not need to despise our ignorance to develop wisdom.

Perhaps we can begin by understanding it.

## A small experiment

Let's investigate a common reaction.

Think of something you find unpleasant.

It does not have to be an intense experience.

Perhaps someone interrupts you while you are speaking.

Your children do not listen to you.

A colleague questions one of your ideas.

Or someone hangs the toilet paper in an orientation clearly opposed to the fundamental principles of civilization.

The next time it happens, try observing three things.

**First: the experience.**

What are you feeling?

Is there an unpleasant bodily sensation?

Does tension appear?

Irritation?

Discomfort?

You do not need to change it.

Simply recognize what is happening.

**Second: the interpretation.**

What story is your mind constructing?

“They don't respect me.”

“They always do the same thing.”

“This shouldn't be happening.”

“I'm right.”

Notice whether this interpretation helps maintain or increase your distress.

**Third: the response.**

What intention is appearing?

Do you want to argue?

Run away?

Impose your opinion?

Can you recognize another possibility?

Perhaps you can listen.

Perhaps you need to set a boundary.

Perhaps you can stop feeding an unnecessary argument.

There is no universal answer.

What we are investigating is how our interpretations influence our reactions and what happens when we introduce different conditions.

And one last thing.

If you discover that you have reacted exactly as you always do, you do not need to get angry with yourself.

You have just observed some conditioning.

That is already valuable information.

## Transforming the conditions

We have explored three fundamental Buddhist concepts: ignorance, attachment, and aversion.

The tradition regards them as deep roots of unwholesome actions and suffering.

Through our model, we have proposed regarding them also as conditioned dynamics that can be reinforced through our interpretations and responses.

Attachment leads us to cling to certain experiences.

Aversion drives us to reject others.

And ignorance can help sustain the ways of perceiving and responding that feed both processes.

But we have also explored something important.

Practice can develop different conditions.

We can learn to observe our reactions.

Understand their causes better.

Develop equanimity.

Cultivate compassion.

And gradually transform some tendencies that once seemed inevitable.

We do not need to become perfect people.

Nor imagine there is a defective part of us that we must destroy.

We can investigate which conditions favor suffering and which allow it to stop being fed.

And perhaps, over time, we will discover that some things we have spent years fighting begin to lose their influence.

Not because we have won a war.

But because we have stopped fighting ourselves.

Perhaps the path is not about defeating our mind, but learning to understand and care for it with enough wisdom that it stops hurting itself.
